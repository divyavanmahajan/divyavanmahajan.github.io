## Thesis

Thariq Shihipar, recorded at AI Engineer CODE as he left Anthropic for OpenAI, argues that AI is both an industrial revolution (cheap, reliable software at scale) and a renaissance (time to raise quality and build personal things). After a year focused on software factories, he says the bottleneck is no longer tokens or intelligence but how humans work with the model, so builders should put the human feedback loop back in.

## Key points

- **What he got wrong.** Shihipar says he was "Bash is all you need" and "React is all you need" pilled. As models get more general they work in human formats, even drawing a front end in Figma via computer use, which keeps their work understandable.
- **A /human folder.** In his fighting game he keeps a scripting-language folder with strict lint rules (dense comments, no single-character variable names) so the files read like specs but are real code he can tweak. He notes that a perfectly balanced game, like rock paper scissors, is boring.
- **Simulated balance fails.** A host describes a card game, Frontier Lab, where Claude simulated thousands of games and found economic victory always won, but could not fix the balance; Shihipar suggests adding variance, as roguelikes do.
- **Decision models are under-used.** He thinks harnesses rarely use decision models such as Jev or Luna, and would build a support bot mostly on them, falling back to inference when needed. The hosts push back that fast, cheap models are usually smaller and dumber.
- **Personal software is hard to get right.** He built an alternative macOS dock and stopped using it, and is now trying a to-do list combined with a screen-time manager. A host says cheap prototypes help him learn what he actually wants.
- **RSI as knowledge work.** Shihipar hopes recursive self-improvement turns AI research and fields like biology into knowledge work, with humans deciding which experiments get compute. He also discusses "prosthetics for achievement", such as WoW guild leadership, as proof of ability for young people.
