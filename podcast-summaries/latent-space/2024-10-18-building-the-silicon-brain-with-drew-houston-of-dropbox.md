## Thesis

Drew Houston, Dropbox's founder and CEO, tells Alessio Fanelli that large models let us bottle cognitive energy the way the Industrial Revolution bottled mechanical energy. He sees knowledge work as cognitively polluted by tools that push every burden onto humans, and positions Dropbox Dash as an attention filter that finds, organises, shares and secures work across platforms.

## Key points

- **CEO as AI engineer.** Houston says he has coded over 400 hours this year. His stack is VS Code with continue.dev routed through his own proxy (Sonnet 3.5 as default), plus a custom local inference setup; he carries a gaming laptop with a GPU on planes.
- **Timing over prediction.** Using self-driving as the example, Houston says forecasts are usually right on direction but wrong on timing; the traction is at "level one" assistive products like Copilot tab-complete, not full autonomy.
- **RAG and long context.** Both are needed, like RAM and disk; a company-wide brain will not fit in a context window, and products at scale need hybrid architectures with small fine-tuned models.
- **From sync to Dash.** A January 2023 memo made Dropbox "AI-first"; engineers built the FileGPT prototype. Dash, Stacks and Dash for Business (launched the day of recording) integrate with Google Drive and OneDrive and give IT cross-platform sharing control, replacing the manual "unshare guy".
- **Where value accrues.** Houston calls LLMs a self-commoditizing business and expects value at Nvidia and at the application layer. He argues Dropbox's trust advantage is that it sells no ads and does not train foundation models.
- **Open source and founder mode.** Open models drove 10-100x price-performance gains; for compute he advises renting, not buying, for now. On founder mode, he describes leaning back too far and leaving a leadership vacuum, and urges founders to learn systematically, mostly by reading.
