## Thesis

Astro Teller uses X's fifteenth anniversary to tell the origin stories of Waymo and Wing. Co-founder Sebastian Thrun argues that almost anything technically possible can be built if a small, focused team tries hard enough, while the Waymo and Wing leaders show that the hard work after the first demo is safety, simplicity, cost and public trust.

## Key points

- **Why self-driving.** Thrun says Larry Page pushed him to build a car that could drive anywhere in California when 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge robots managed only 10 to 20 miles. Thrun was motivated by road deaths, including a friend killed when he was 18.
- **The level-four pivot.** Andrew Chatham recounts a freeway-assist trial in which Googlers, knowing they were filmed, fell asleep or used two phones, so the team cancelled it and aimed for full door-to-door autonomy. He says deep learning from around 2014 made the service possible and cites 82% fewer airbag-deployment crashes and over 150,000 rides per week.
- **Small, secret teams.** Thrun modelled X on Lockheed's Skunk Works: no finance staff, a moratorium on PowerPoint, and teams of about 10 people, because the smaller the team, the faster it moves.
- **Simple hardware for Wing.** André Prager describes moving from a tail-sitter drone that kicked up dust to a hovering design with a passive hook and an auto-loader with no moving parts, arguing that simplicity is what lets the system scale.
- **Noise and regulation.** Prager found complaints came from people under the cruising path, not recipients, and fixed it with slower, uneven-length propellers. Margaret Nagel says Wing went from per-flight approvals to over 450,000 commercial deliveries, with DoorDash, Walmart and an NHS blood-sample route in London.
- **Prototyping culture.** Joe Sargent's Design Kitchen builds quick, rough prototypes, and Teller's rule is to tackle the riskiest part of the problem first.
