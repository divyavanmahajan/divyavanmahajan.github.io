## Thesis

Astro Teller revisits two X projects linking brains and technology. Sarah Laszlo describes Valkyrie, which bet that the fruit-fly brain's wiring could preview the future of AI, and why she asked to shut it down when the evidence fell apart. Obi Felten explains how Amber built a cheap EEG that could detect depression and anxiety, but was stopped because the business case, not the technology, did not hold up.

## Key points

- **Valkyrie's pitch.** Laszlo built on Google Brain's nanometer-resolution map of the Drosophila brain, arguing that neuromorphic computing could match a brain's efficiency: a fruit-fly brain uses less power than a AA battery yet navigates in 3D.
- **Results with Wing and Mineral.** Using reservoir computing, the team matched Wing's drone image segmentation accuracy and trained a strawberry-plant classifier for the agriculture moonshot in six steps, against tens of thousands of steps for convolutional networks.
- **The honest kill.** Interns re-ran every experiment with random networks, which did just as well given the right sparsity, a value that did match the fly brain. Laszlo still believes AI should become more neuromorphic and says DeepMind is revisiting the idea for energy reasons.
- **Amber's technology.** Felten says depression shows up as a compressed event-related potential (anhedonia). Machine learning made the signal readable for individuals, and the team built a cap and amplifier for about $400-500, against $70,000 lab devices. An intern's finding extended the model to anxiety.
- **Why it stopped.** Of three kill criteria (technology, product, business), the business failed: over 90% of health spending goes on human-delivered care, and diagnosis competes with the free PHQ-9 questionnaire. The hardware and software were open-sourced and 50 devices donated for EEG research in the Global South.
- **What came next.** Felten founded Flourish Labs, which trains young adults as peer supporters and gets insurance to pay for the service.
