## Thesis

David Guerena, an agricultural scientist with CIAT (part of CGIAR) based in Arusha, Tanzania, tells Astro Teller that plant breeding has barely changed in 10,000 years and is too slow for a changing climate. He argues that computer vision built with Mineral, X's computational agriculture project, can replace error-prone manual scoring, first on rovers and now on smartphones, and lets breeders work on farmers' own fields.

## Key points

- **Who breeds for smallholders.** Guerena says companies such as Syngenta and Monsanto target large industrial farms, while most of the world's farmers work one to three acres. In East Africa, common beans supply about 90% of protein for 50 to 100 million people.
- **Climate pressure.** In northern Tanzania, he says, the short rainy season has become so erratic that farmers no longer plant in it, cutting food and income.
- **From rover to phone.** Mineral first brought a rover to wheat and maize fields in Mexico. Frequent imaging produced continuous flower and pod curves that breeders had never seen, revealing which varieties hold flowers under heat and drought.
- **Accuracy and speed.** Guerena reports smartphone heritability within about 2-3% of hand counts, with a whole plot imaged in under 30 seconds versus 3 to 5 minutes of visual scoring.
- **On-farm breeding.** Yields on farmers' fields correlate only 8-40% with research stations, so he wants phones on farms. Farmer feedback, including work by breeder Clare Mukankusi in Uganda, raised cooking time and frost tolerance as breeding goals.
- **Faster cycles.** Combining phenomics with genomics and marker-assisted selection could, he estimates, cut a 10-year variety cycle to two or three years. He also tells how his team secretly built "Bruno", a push cart that echoed Mineral's first prototype.
