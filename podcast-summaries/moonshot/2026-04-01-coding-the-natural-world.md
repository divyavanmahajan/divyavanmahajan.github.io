## Thesis

Astro Teller argues that the 21st century will be remembered as much for biology becoming an engineering discipline as for AI. Two guests make the case from opposite scales: Brad Zamft, CEO of X spinout Heritable Agriculture, on predicting which plant genes to change, and Relly Brandman, project lead of X's A-Life, on a "virtual cell" that guides how microbes are engineered to manufacture materials.

## Key points

- **Agriculture must keep improving.** Zamft describes a $3 trillion industry producing 10 billion tons of food a year, and says yield and resource efficiency come first, followed by pest, drought and heat resistance. He notes one R&D head said the industry faces "a COVID every year" in some crop.
- **Real-time signals, not just DNA.** Zamft says DNA is inherited and says little about how a plant is responding now; RNA and other molecular data reveal that state. This insight became the basis of Heritable.
- **Early validation.** According to Zamft, the team first predicted leaf-number genes in a fast-growing model plant, confirmed them with gene editing, then found new flowering-time genes in corn within 18 months, later running field trials in Illinois and root sampling in Wisconsin.
- **Spinout lessons.** Zamft says pitching around 100 investors imposed discipline, and that X's people-first culture is the thing he insists on keeping.
- **From trial and error to predictive design.** Brandman says scientists can edit cell DNA but cannot predict the outcome, and scale-up to swimming-pool-sized fermentation tanks often fails late. A-Life models the cell and its environment as one system, simulating millions of experiments and recommending the 10-30 most valuable ones to customers' lab teams.
- **Small, messy data.** Brandman explains that biology data sets are small and heterogeneous, so the team started by encoding known biology and now plans to move from manual pipetting to robotic systems after finding which data most improves model performance.
