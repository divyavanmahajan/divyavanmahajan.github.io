## Thesis

Astro Teller frames energy as possibly the ultimate moonshot and contrasts two X projects. Blixt, led by Matt Forkin, tested a decades-old Z-pinch fusion anomaly and was wound down when the data showed no path to a power plant, which Teller and Forkin call a smart bet. Tapestry, led by Page Crahan, treats the grid as humankind's largest machine and uses AI to help operators map, plan and optimise it.

## Key points

- **Origins.** Will Regan, formerly at ARPA-E, surfaced 1980s Naval Research Lab and Los Alamos results where frozen-hydrogen Z-pinch plasmas looked unexpectedly stable. Teller explains the expected-value logic: a 10% shot at $10,000 beats a 50% shot at $100.
- **The experiment.** Forkin's team simulated for two years, then built a facility in Alameda, pulsing current through a 150-micron frozen hydrogen wire. The first shot came in December 2023; after a few hundred shots they saw an instability growing early on.
- **Closing it down.** Forkin put the odds at around 1% and says the project is ending and the results will be published to give the field closure.
- **The grid problem.** Crahan describes 80 million km of grid built on an 1880s design and an energy trilemma of infrastructure, cost and reliability, with roughly seven-year waits to connect new generation.
- **Tapestry's approach.** After Ivo Stivoric's team talked to 80 utilities, Tapestry combined operators' imperfect blueprints with Street View and other geospatial data into a "Google Maps for electrons", then built tools for managing assets, planning and real-time optimisation.
- **Chile.** Juan Carlos Olmedo, CEO of Chile's national grid, aimed to phase out coal by 2030 on a 3,100 km, isolated grid. He says planning analysis that took three to six months can now compare many alternatives in a day, and Crahan says the tool now guides long-term investment for 20 million people.
