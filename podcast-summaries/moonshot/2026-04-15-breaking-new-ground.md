## Thesis

Astro Teller argues that building design still works as if every building were the first: slow, sequential iterations that each take months. Architect Michelle Kaufmann recounts X's first attempt to fix this with software (Genie, later the spinout Flux), which she says was ahead of the technology and the industry. Adrian Walker and Julia Ling of Anori argue that AI and data-sharing now make the same goal achievable.

## Key points

- **From prefab to X.** Kaufmann, who started at Frank Gehry's office, built 54 prefab homes before two factory partners failed within two weeks in the 2008 crash. Sebastian Thrun then introduced her to Teller's idea of making good architecture accessible through AI and data-sharing.
- **"Intelligent clay."** Kaufmann describes Flux's P12 project, an office building designed and built within 12 months. Users set priorities, entered an address and got an engineered building with cost in 10 to 60 seconds, work she says normally takes five months.
- **Why Flux struggled.** Developers would not take the risk of going first, computing power was not ready, and the industry was not open to change, according to Kaufmann.
- **Scale of the problem.** Walker cites over 30% of senior builders retiring within five years, 20-25% contingency per building, a US shortfall of 4-5 million housing units and about 250 million globally.
- **Engineering geometry, not pictures.** Ling says Anori generates precise geometry checked against regulations and pulls coupled constraints forward, instead of passing them between architects and engineers. Walker says regulators would see which rules an application breaks.
- **Next steps.** Walker plans the first AI-assisted pilot buildings within a year, with interest from the US, Rio de Janeiro, New Zealand, Australia and the EU.
