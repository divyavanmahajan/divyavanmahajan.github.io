## Thesis

Astro Teller traces one origin story of modern AI: Andrew Ng and Jeff Dean's bet, made at X about 15 years ago, that scaling neural networks on Google's infrastructure would beat decades of hand-tuned algorithms. The episode then follows Project Ada, X's effort to have AI evolve and write code, and ends with the guests' views on jobs, cheap intelligence and using AI to come up with new inventions.

## Key points

- **Scale was heresy.** Ng says his Stanford students had shown that bigger learning systems performed better, but senior researchers dismissed the idea. His "one learning algorithm" hypothesis, inspired by neural rewiring experiments, got him yelled at during a National Science Foundation workshop.
- **Sushi with Larry Page.** Sebastian Thrun suggested pitching Google's compute to Page, and that dinner launched what became Google Brain. Dean, who had built a parallel neural network training thesis on a 32-processor machine, joined after meeting Ng in a Google micro-kitchen.
- **Average cat.** Trained without labels on 10 million random YouTube frames, the network learned a cat detector, which made the New York Times front page. Dean also explains word vectors (word2vec), where "king minus man plus woman" lands near "queen".
- **Project Ada.** Benoît Schillings and Olivia Hatalsky describe training models on public code and its history to evolve and autocomplete code, including an early tool called "baby Jeff". Hatalsky points to banking systems built on decades-old code that few people still know. The work moved to Google; Schillings solved a magnet-placement problem in 20 minutes instead of days.
- **Jobs and access.** Scott Huffman (data science agent, Google Labs) expects non-coders to build whole apps soon, and compares the shift to bookkeepers giving way to accountants. Hatalsky warns against models regressing to the mean. Ng argues AI can make intelligence cheap; Dean wants capable models to be far cheaper to serve.
- **Invention machines.** Schillings describes language models that read every new paper and pair research results with problems, which has already turned up unusual and more efficient chip designs.
