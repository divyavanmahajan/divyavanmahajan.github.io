## Thesis

John K. Thompson, author of the book Data for All, argues that people own the data they generate and that companies should pay for it as they pay for any other raw material. He traces the habit of handing data over for free back to Arthur C. Nielsen, and says EU legislation is building the structures that will let individuals control and monetise their data.

## Key points

- **A 100-year precedent.** Thompson, who once worked at Nielsen, says Nielsen's deal to receive grocery stores' usage data for free set a precedent that still shapes how data is treated. He compares Facebook to a builder who expects free lumber.
- **The EU leads.** He calls GDPR a success and points to the Data Act, the Data Governance Act and the Digital Markets Act as the basis for data pools, unions and exchanges. He expects the EU to be there within 18 months, Australia within about 24, and says California plus five other US states have privacy laws.
- **What a data exchange is.** Thompson describes a third-party legal entity where individuals set prices and usage policies, block companies they object to, or route earnings to charity. Exchanges cannot store or sell the data themselves. He likens pricing to music royalties; Benson compares it to a stock exchange.
- **Location data.** Thompson keeps location services off and his phone off at night, using a landline. Benson admits he sleeps with his phone on the nightstand.
- **Advice for practitioners.** Thompson says analytics teams need not change much: they already pay for external data, and in future will simply pay "a different somebody". He welcomes government regulation and urges teams to combine many sources, derived data and synthetic data rather than rely on one dataset.
- **Outlook.** He is bullish on data careers, saying that turning every American student into a data scientist would supply perhaps a tenth of the demand. He adds that market research has produced more US millionaires than any other industry in the last decade.
