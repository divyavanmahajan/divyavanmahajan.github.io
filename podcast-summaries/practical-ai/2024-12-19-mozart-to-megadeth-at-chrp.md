## Thesis

Jeff Smith, founder and CEO of CHRP, argues that music works as a mirror for mood, and that passively analysing people's existing streaming habits gives organisations a better wellbeing signal than surveys. The company is named after the canary in the coal mine: an early indicator. Smith stresses that the tool is for screening, not diagnosis, and that privacy and trust have to come first.

## Key points

- **Why surveys fail.** Smith says leaders spend heavily on perks but get poor data from pulse surveys because people skip them or answer cautiously. One CHRO told him a single survey cost $250,000, took 90 days to return and described a moment that had already passed.
- **How it works.** Users opt in with Spotify, Apple or YouTube. Smith says CHRP spends three to four weeks building a personal baseline from acoustic features and behaviour (skips, repeats, tempo) and then tracks deviation; podcasts and audiobooks add context. He cites an average of 22 hours of music listening a week.
- **The model.** Songs map to vectors clustered around "level one" emotions such as energetic, melancholy or aggravated, which are weighted into workplace "level two" attributes like motivation, stress and balance. Traditional ML does the analysis; generative AI writes the personalised messages.
- **Two outputs.** Organisations see anonymised, clustered dashboards by team or division, deliberately never individuals. Employees get a weekly private "e-score" and tailored perks.
- **Other verticals.** Smith describes several hundred therapists signing up, university athletics departments, and a military base piloting it with 120 troops after a high number of suicides in that group in the past year.
- **Ethics.** Smith says CHRP has held back retail use cases and turned away executives who wanted it to "fix" a culture he saw as the leadership's own doing.
