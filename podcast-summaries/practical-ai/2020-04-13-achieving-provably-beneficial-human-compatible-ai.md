## Thesis

Stuart Russell, Berkeley professor and author of "Human Compatible", argues that the standard model of AI (give a machine a fixed objective and let it optimise) becomes dangerous as systems grow more capable. He proposes a model in which human preferences stay with the human, the machine knows it is uncertain about them, and so the smarter the AI, the better the outcome for people.

## Key points

- **History of the field.** Russell traces AI from deterministic planning and logic when he started around 1975, through the 1980s expert-system bubble and an AI winter (his course fell to about 25 students in 1990; it is now about 900), to Judea Pearl's probabilistic methods and deep learning from around 2011-2012.
- **Fixed objectives backfire.** Russell says social media content-selection algorithms, optimising click-through, reshape users into more predictable clickers. He argues a system with a fixed objective has an incentive to resist being switched off.
- **Uncertain objectives.** Russell's alternative machine asks permission when trade-offs are unclear, such as a $15 hotel coffee, and welcomes human interference as information. He links this to inverse reinforcement learning, which infers a reward function from observed behaviour.
- **Loss functions matter today.** Russell says uniform loss functions treat confusing Norfolk and Norwich terriers as just as bad as Google's mislabelling of a person as a gorilla. He urges practitioners to think about their objective and their system's possible scope of action.
- **Deep learning is not AGI.** Russell argues deep learning cannot reason, accumulate knowledge or transfer learning, and is one piece alongside symbolic logic, probabilistic reasoning and planning.
- **Safety as engineering, plus regulation.** Russell would rather not call it "AI ethics", comparing it to nuclear and bridge engineering, where safety is built in. He wants public-facing algorithms regulated in a way analogous to pharmaceuticals. Benson leads AI ethics at Lockheed Martin.
