## Thesis

In the fourth "AI in Africa" spotlight, Joyce Nabende (Makerere AI Lab), Leo Mutuku (Local Development Research Institute, Kenya) and Godliver Owomugisha (Busitema University) argue that AI can help smallholder farmers cope with climate change and food insecurity, but only if it is built on locally collected, openly shared data and designed together with farmers, extension workers and local government.

## Key points

- **Why Africa is different.** Mutuku says most production comes from subsistence smallholders whose rain-fed farming is becoming unreliable, so a failed crop means hunger; AI can target help at household rather than regional level.
- **Data must be collected locally.** Mutuku's team uses mobile phones and ODK apps to map farm boundaries, inputs and commodity prices. Nabende's lab collects crop pest and disease images and is building speech recognition for farmer call-in radio shows.
- **Open data and citizen science.** Mutuku argues open data addresses Africa's data sparsity and lets communities contribute and reuse data; Owomugisha stresses publishing collection protocols.
- **Trust through peer educators.** Mutuku cites one extension officer per 10,000 farmers against an ideal of 1 to 400, so LDRI trains peer farmer champions and treats Kenyan county governments as co-designers.
- **Early detection and recommendations.** Owomugisha uses spectrometry to detect cassava disease about six weeks after infection, before visible symptoms, and wants low-cost devices. Nabende describes a recommender in the lab's AdServe app that answers farmer questions in local languages.
- **Localised early warning.** Mutuku's system combines phone-camera ground truth with Earth observation data for intercropped plots of 0.5 to 3 hectares, and sees yield estimates feeding insurance and financial services.
