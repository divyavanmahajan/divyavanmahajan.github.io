## Thesis

Damien Riehl, a former tech litigator who now works with large language models at vLex, tells Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson that generative AI shows "human creativity" is often brute-forceable, which puts pressure on copyright and patent regimes dating back to the 1700s. He expects regulation to lag, patents to lose value, and knowledge workers to stay ahead only by using the tools.

## Key points

- **All the Music.** With Noah Rubin, Riehl generated and wrote to disk 471 billion melodies. He says a melody behind a $2.8 million jury verdict against Katy Perry appears 8,128 times in that dataset; the judge later reversed the verdict, finding the melody unoriginal.
- **All the Patents.** With Mike Bommarito, who helped show GPT-4 beating 90% of humans on the bar exam, he is recombining existing patent claims in vector space to create prior art.
- **Who owns machine output.** Riehl says the US Copyright Office treats fully machine-generated work as uncopyrightable, and a human who adds a little on top gets only "thin copyright". He cites the Google Books ruling on transformative fair use when discussing how models learn from copyrighted books.
- **Provenance.** On Whitenack's question about LLaMA-based models, Riehl uses the "fruit of the poisonous tree" idea: training against a licence (such as academic-only terms) may taint downstream models, though he stresses it is not legal advice and proof is hard.
- **Patents fading.** Citing the Alice decision and the Thaler case, he expects AI to speed the decline of patents, which he calls "a license to sue" for things obsolete in three years.
- **Four worlds of work.** Riehl describes a path from 2022 productivity to layoffs once employers capture 10x gains, but contrasts it with the 1979 spreadsheet, which created more accounting work. He wrote a 17-page cover story in three hours instead of 17 with GPT.
