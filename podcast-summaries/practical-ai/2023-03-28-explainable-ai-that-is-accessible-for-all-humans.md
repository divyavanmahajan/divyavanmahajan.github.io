## Thesis

Beth Rudden, CEO of Bast AI and formerly at IBM, argues that trustworthy conversational AI needs an explicit map of what it knows. That map is an ontology built from the user's own data, which records where each fact came from, while generative models only supply phrasing. She says this makes answers attributable, reduces made-up responses, and lets small organisations build AI without large models or big hardware.

## Key points

- **Background.** Rudden trained as an archaeologist in Greek and Latin and helped build IBM's data scientist certification, accredited through the Open Group. She says the semantic side of NLP was neglected once GPUs made statistical models dominant.
- **"Cats", not bots.** Bast AI's conversational agents, named after the Egyptian cat goddess, ingest a document into a knowledge graph. Each entity keeps its source paragraph and page, so the agent can show where an answer came from. She says about 50 models run through the company's orchestration, and easy questions are answered from an OpenSearch corpus.
- **Generation as a utility.** Rudden says a ChatGPT-style model will describe a toy that does not exist, while an ontology-backed agent will say the toy is not in stock. She calls generative models a stochastic parrot with no understanding.
- **Practical use.** She tells non-technical users to test chatbots on things they know well. She describes a Maryville University workshop where 138 teachers and students used ChatGPT-generated lists of daily tasks to decide what to eliminate, reduce, raise or create.
- **Bias and access.** Whitenack raises concerns about Western, English-focused NLP. Rudden argues that wider variance of data and people gets closer to the truth, citing the diversity prediction theorem. She objects that current models were trained on data harvested without consent.
- **Outlook.** Rudden hopes attribution becomes the valued feature across modalities. She argues that cheap words open the field to visual thinkers, citing Temple Grandin's book Visual Thinking.
