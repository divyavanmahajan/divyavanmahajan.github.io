## Thesis

Gautham Pallapa, Principal Director of AI and Innovation at Scotiabank, argues that AI adoption in a risk-averse industry like banking should start from a clear definition of value and success, not from the technology. He sees agents as tools that take over repeatable, low-value work so people can move "up the stack", but says trust in agents is not yet high enough to remove humans from the loop.

## Key points

- **Value first, then AI.** Pallapa's sequence is: identify what value means for the organisation, quantify success, then ask how AI could help. His book Invaluable, written with a co-author over about two years, formalises this into an eight-part "value transform" drawn from 600+ hours of executive workshops and 400+ interviews.
- **Where banks will and won't go.** He would not yet connect personal accounts to an agent that performs transactions. Lower-risk targets are customer service agents, chat assistants, compliance and risk support, and help for advisers.
- **People as the value creators.** Pallapa says technology only accelerates; people create value. He describes current models as "nondeterministic probabilistic models" that lack the context, intent and incident experience humans bring, and that start hallucinating when given too many instructions.
- **Interns as a startup.** He rebooted the internship programme so teams with a tech lead, design lead and product owner pitch executives for funding. He bans "vibe coding" on premises and challenges teams to justify why they need an agent, calling the alternative "resume driven development".
- **Agent teams that mirror human teams.** Rather than starting with swarms, Pallapa suggests reversing Conway's Law: build a balanced team of BA, design and testing agents under an orchestrator, with shared memory, guardrails and an evaluation jury. His team shared five agentic AI patterns at MCP Dev Summit.
- **Rethinking the SDLC.** He is experimenting with whether the software development life cycle should become looped feedback rather than sequential steps, but says he does not yet have the data to recommend an approach.
