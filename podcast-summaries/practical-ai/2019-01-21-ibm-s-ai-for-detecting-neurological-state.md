## Thesis

Guillermo Cecchi and Ajay Royyuru of IBM Healthcare and Life Sciences Research argue that language is a direct window on brain health, so speech analysis can give psychiatrists and neurologists measurable signals they currently judge by ear. Recorded with consent on a phone, these samples move observation from short, occasional clinic visits to more continuous measurement, while diagnosis stays with the licensed clinician.

## Key points

- **Why language.** Cecchi says psychiatric evaluation already relies heavily on how patients speak, yet the field barely uses modern language tools. Disrupted language appears in psychosis and even in conditions seen as motor disorders, such as Parkinson's.
- **Out of the clinic.** Royyuru describes a phone app that prompts subjects with questions in sessions of five to ten minutes. This stretches observation from a 20-30 minute appointment to daily life and helps patients who struggle to attend appointments.
- **Consent by design.** Royyuru stresses that recording is never always on: participants start each session, review what was captured and decide whether the clinician sees it.
- **Features and findings.** Cecchi lists voice and acoustic features, phoneme structure, the vowel space, grammatical parsing and semantic embeddings. His team separated Parkinson's patients on and off levodopa using voice frequencies the human ear cannot detect, and measured "flight of ideas" in psychosis as semantic coherence.
- **Automating the cookie theft task.** Cecchi says describing this decades-old picture test lets them infer clinicians' cognitive scales with high accuracy, remotely and often.
- **Bias and output.** Cecchi says tracking the same individual over time accounts for much personal and group bias. Royyuru notes the retrospective work covered English, Spanish and Portuguese, and that the system brings clinically relevant features to the surface rather than issuing diagnoses. Next, Cecchi wants to apply the approach to therapy sessions.
