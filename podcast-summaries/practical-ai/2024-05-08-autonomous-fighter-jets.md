## Thesis

Chris Benson and Daniel Whitenack use the X-62A VISTA, an F-16 flown in simulated combat by an AI autopilot, as evidence that the next wave of AI is about embedding models in physical machines rather than building ever-larger foundation models. Benson argues public trust, not technical capability, is now the main brake. They also cover the Linux Foundation's Open Platform for Enterprise AI and the rapid arrival of AI video generation.

## Key points

- **An AI-flown F-16.** Benson (who discloses that his employer Lockheed Martin is involved, though he is not) describes the X-62A: the Secretary of the US Air Force rode in the front seat during a simulated dogfight with neither human touching the controls, and the safety pilot has not needed to intervene in tests.
- **Trust is the bottleneck.** Benson recalls a 2020 DARPA simulator event where an AI pilot beat a top Air Force instructor, notes that the FAA has authorised no AI systems for commercial airliners, and says the Boeing 737 MAX problems (automation, not AI) have shaken public trust.
- **AI in the physical world.** Whitenack cites an MIT Media Lab investor panel arguing the era of the foundation-model builder is in some ways over and the opportunity is embedding AI in retail, manufacturing and agriculture. He mentions Windracers, whose drones deliver mail to UK islands but struggle to find airspace for testing.
- **Testing through gateways.** Benson explains that the FAA lets companies earn their way through small-scope trials, while the military has dedicated airspace such as Edwards Air Force Base.
- **OPEA blueprints.** Whitenack describes the Linux Foundation project, backed by Intel, Anyscale, Cloudera, DataStax, Hugging Face and others, which offers RAG blueprints, reference implementations, open microservices and evaluation scorecards. Benson sees it as a way to avoid every company building incompatible stacks.
- **Video generation snowball.** The hosts compare Microsoft's VASA-1 and Alibaba's EMO to the DALL-E era, note Hugging Face passing one million hosted models, and hope for uses such as personalised tutors.
