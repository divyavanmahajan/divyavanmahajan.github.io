## Thesis

David Yakobovitch, principal data scientist at Galvanize and host of the HumAIn podcast, argues that data science roles are specialising quickly and that training programmes often oversell quick outcomes. He also argues that automation is already removing jobs, so practitioners should design systems with humans in mind from the start, an approach he calls human-centred AI.

## Key points

- **From actuarial work to teaching.** Yakobovitch moved from applied maths into actuarial science in 2010, spent about eight years building Python data solutions in financial services, then worked on enterprise reskilling at General Assembly and now Galvanize. He says his father, who ran an electronics repair business until the industry changed, learned Python with his coaching.
- **Training gaps.** He says many bootcamps and university programmes rush people into industry without enough preparation. Full-time bootcamps of 60 to 100 hours a week for three months are hard for people with no prior experience.
- **Specialised roles.** Yakobovitch separates ML engineers (software background) and data engineers (cloud and distributed systems) from data scientists, and predicts a "data science as a service" segment, naming Neptune, Spell and Weights & Biases. He advises applicants to meet about 75% of listed requirements rather than every box.
- **Automation and jobs.** Using examples such as self-checkout lines, automated customer service, robot butlers at a JW Marriott in Shenzhen and Deloitte's "super jobs" report, he argues that efficiency pressure will affect every industry. Whitenack adds a JP Morgan experiment with an AI copywriter.
- **Human-centred and empathetic design.** He links the term to Stanford's institute with Fei-Fei Li and says builders should test designs for different ages, languages and settings. Whitenack raises Google Translate's roughly 100 languages against more than 7,000 spoken worldwide.
- **Practical steps.** Yakobovitch says pre-built cloud AI services bring their bias into your product. He recommends writing down who your customers are, pairing engineers with business strategists, and reading Amy Webb's trend report and Matt Turck's yearly data and AI landscape.
