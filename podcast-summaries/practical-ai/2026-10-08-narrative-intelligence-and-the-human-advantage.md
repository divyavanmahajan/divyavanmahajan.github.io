## Thesis

Reed Frerichs, a leadership coach who moved from acting and film production to startups, argues that "narrative intelligence", the skill of updating your own story in real time, is a human advantage while AI reshapes roles. His framework is own, author, act: accept where you are, write the next chapter, then take a first small step. Daniel Whitenack connects this to his own experience as a CEO leading AI adoption at Prediction Guard.

## Key points

- **From pitches to coaching.** Frerichs met Whitenack in the Intel Ignite accelerator, where he ran deal flow and began helping founders tell their pitch like a movie. He now coaches Sloan Fellows at MIT and teams at Series A and B startups.
- **Stories people hide.** Many international MIT students want to sound like famous American founders, Frerichs says, while hiding the resilience in their own backgrounds. His first talk on the idea was at South by Southwest and draws on Joseph Campbell's hero's journey.
- **Ownership and AI adoption.** Whitenack admits that pride in his own code and SDLC standards made him a "liability" to his team's AI adoption. Frerichs replies that founders often feel they must pretend to know everything, and that one AI company he coaches calls its programme "the ownership project".
- **Impostor syndrome is normal.** Frerichs says nearly every founder has it and recommends expecting it. He stresses values alignment, emotional intelligence (self-awareness, responding rather than reacting) and small habits in the style of Atomic Habits.
- **Trying on identities.** Facing constant acceleration, which Chris Benson says he struggles with, Frerichs separates a stable core self from identities you can try on, such as calling yourself a leader without being CEO.
- **Reaching the sceptical.** For people who fear AI, Frerichs starts with something practical they care about. His parents warmed to it while planning a family reunion trip. Whitenack gives the same advice to engineers building their first agent.
