## Thesis

Nestor Maslej, research manager at the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI, walks Daniel Whitenack through the seventh edition of the AI Index. His main points: the cost of frontier models is pushing research into the hands of a few companies, regulation is growing fast and becoming more restrictive, and current benchmarks and risk analysis do not match how AI is actually used.

## Key points

- **How the report is made.** Maslej says the Index combines its own data collection (for example on national AI legislation) with partner data from Accenture, GitHub, McKinsey and LinkedIn, guided by a steering committee that includes Jack Clark, Erik Brynjolfsson and James Manyika.
- **Training costs.** Working with Epoch AI, the Index estimated training costs of about $80 million for GPT-4 and $190 million for Gemini. Maslej links this directly to industry dominating frontier research and asks who can afford models costing a billion dollars or more.
- **Regulation shifts to the states.** In 2023 about 40 AI-related bills became law at US state level against one at federal level. Maslej notes the rules come from many agencies and are moving from exploratory to restrictive, citing California's SB 1047 and its possible effect on open source developers.
- **Running out of data.** He sees reasons for optimism (synthetic data, more efficient algorithms, tools like Meta's Segment Anything) and for concern: Epoch estimates text data could run out in about four years at current scaling.
- **Benchmarks and risk.** Developers all report MMLU, coding and GSM8K scores, but there is no consensus on responsible-AI benchmarks such as TruthfulQA or RealToxicityPrompts. Maslej argues long-term existential risks are hard to analyse because the arguments are mostly theoretical.
- **Public mood versus productivity.** People in the US, Canada and France are pessimistic about AI, while those in Indonesia, Thailand and Mexico are more positive, even as studies show AI makes workers more productive. Maslej puts the gap down to uncertainty about jobs.
