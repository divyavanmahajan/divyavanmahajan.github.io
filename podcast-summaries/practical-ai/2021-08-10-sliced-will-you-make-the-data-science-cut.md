## Thesis

Meg Risdal (Kaggle) and Nick Wan describe SLICED, a "Chopped for data science" show streamed on Nick's Twitch channel, where contestants get an unseen dataset and two hours to build predictive models and visualisations. They argue its real value is community: it replaces the social side of pre-pandemic conferences, humanises well-known data scientists and acts as a strong, time-boxed learning exercise.

## Key points

- **Format and scoring.** Wan explains that points come from model performance on Kaggle, the hosts' qualitative judging of data visualisation, and hidden "golden features", such as extracting bedroom and bathroom counts from Airbnb listing text.
- **Origins.** Risdal says the idea began as a joke on Wan's Wednesday data science roundtables; a pilot season followed, then a 16-contestant season. Wan says virtual conferences had removed the networking he valued.
- **Viewers play along.** Datasets sit on Kaggle, so viewers can submit alongside contestants, chat, and make friends via Twitch and the hashtag, Wan says.
- **Levelling the field.** Contestants are asked not to bring supercomputers; Colab and Kaggle notebooks help equalise access. Wan says datasets are curated at tens of thousands of rows and tens of columns so heavy compute gives no edge.
- **Tasks and the modelling meta.** Risdal highlights an animal-shelter outcome task (multi-class) and a New York Airbnb price regression scored with RMSLE. Wan notes the meta moved from out-of-the-box gradient boosting to grid search and Bayesian optimisation.
- **Learning by exposure.** Risdal says contestant Ethan Douglas told her he matured more as a data scientist in a few months of SLICED than in years of self-teaching; she advises time-boxed goals.

## Quotes

> "potential embarrassment in front of a worldwide audience watching you live code is a very strong forcing function to learn." — Meg Risdal

> "our data sets are also curated in a way that the training is never going to usually be more than minutes." — Nick Wan
