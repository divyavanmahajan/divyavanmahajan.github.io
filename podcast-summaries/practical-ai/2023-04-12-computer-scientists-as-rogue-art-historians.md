## Thesis

Amanda Wasielewski, an art historian in the digital humanities program at Uppsala University and author of "Computational Formalism: Art History and Machine Learning", argues that computer scientists classifying art by visual features are unknowingly repeating early formalist art history. She says machine learning is a useful tool for art historians, but only if practitioners on both sides recognize that categories like style are interpretive.

## Key points

- **Art as a harder dataset.** Wasielewski says many computer vision papers treated object recognition in photos as solved and turned to stylized paintings as a harder test. Finding a dog in a painting, she notes, is of little use to an art historian.
- **Distant viewing.** She traces comparison back to Heinrich Wölfflin's double slide projector; machine learning extends this to mass comparison, the art-history counterpart of "distant reading" in literary studies.
- **Machines do not see space.** Using the apocryphal Cold War tank classifier and a COVID lung study that learned children versus adults, she argues models treat images as flat surfaces of pixels without understanding three-dimensional space.
- **Unstable categories.** One study wanted to "fix" confusion between action painting and abstract expressionism; Wasielewski explains these are competing terms from rival critics, not distinct classes. Unsupervised clustering does not escape the problem, since clusters still need names.
- **Forgery and photographs.** Deep learning both creates and detects forgeries. Citing the AI-generated image of the Pope in a puffy coat, which she says she did not question herself, she asks whether photorealistic generated images count as photographs.
- **A practical future.** She expects art history, which she calls fairly technophobic, to adopt these tools for cataloguing, such as identifying unrecorded artworks from old exhibition photographs. Benson notes similar authenticity problems in the intelligence community.
