## Thesis

In a hosts-only episode, Chris Benson and Daniel Whitenack read IBM's stock collapse as an early sign of a "post-agentic" economy. Benson argues that agents working with other agents at massive scale will erode the value of enterprise software built around human interfaces, and that companies and workers must adapt rather than hold on to old roles. Whitenack backs this with what he sees among Prediction Guard's customers.

## Key points

- **IBM as a canary.** Whitenack cites a 25% single-session drop, about $70 billion in market value and IBM's worst day in over fifty years. Analysts linked it to IT budgets moving from enterprise software and services to hardware and infrastructure, partly from fear of being priced out of AI. He says Workday, Salesforce, ServiceNow and Adobe also fell that day, while chip makers rose.
- **Squeezed from both sides.** Benson notes China now treating its cheap open models as a strategic asset that could be restricted, which narrows the fallback from expensive US proprietary models. Whitenack describes a Chinese gray market reselling discounted access to OpenAI and Anthropic APIs.
- **Fine-tuning returns.** Benson hears organisations reconsidering training and fine-tuning small models. Whitenack points to tools like Unsloth and notes that long-running cloud agents change the economics compared with pay-as-you-go APIs.
- **Agents at enterprise scale.** Whitenack says Prediction Guard recently spoke with one company running 70,000 agents and another with about 6,000. He contrasts vendors exposing their platforms via MCP with those building closed, proprietary agents.
- **Agent-to-agent interaction.** Benson expects most cross-system interactions to happen between agents without humans in GUIs, making permissions and MCP management at scale an open market. He predicts "loop engineering" will be outdated before the year ends.
- **Anthropic workspace paper.** Whitenack and Benson discuss Anthropic's paper on verbalizable representations forming a global workspace in language models, which Benson compares to working memory.
