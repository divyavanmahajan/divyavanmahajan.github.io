## Thesis

Ismael Valenzuela and Gregory Richardson of BlackBerry argue that AI is one more tool in a long-running contest between attackers and defenders, not a decisive weapon for either side. Valenzuela says attackers mostly use it in the early phases of an attack, while Richardson says defenders benefit most from prediction and classification, not natural-language interfaces. Both insist human analysts stay central.

## Key points

- **Crime follows the money.** Richardson recalls an FBI report from around 2010-2012 stating cybercrime profits had overtaken global heroin, cocaine and marijuana sales combined, which he sees as the point organised crime moved online. Valenzuela adds nation-state espionage and hacktivism as other motives.
- **Signatures don't scale.** Valenzuela cites BlackBerry's quarterly report: a 53% rise in unique malware, over 11,000 unique samples per quarter, which is why it has relied on predictive machine learning since the Cylance days.
- **LLMs aren't the SOC's problem.** Richardson says no SOC analyst has ever told him they needed natural language to query their tools. He sees the value in classifying scattered alerts across network, endpoint and cloud into a small number of attack cases to cut alert fatigue.
- **What attackers actually do.** Valenzuela finds no autonomous hacking agents, but notes much better phishing, including in fluent Japanese, and expects more deception through deepfakes and greater scale.
- **Tool sprawl.** Richardson describes thousands of vendors and fragmented stacks that attackers exploit by attacking the gaps; BlackBerry's managed XDR offering ingests whatever tools a customer already has and pairs human analysts with predictive AI.
- **Leadership risk.** Looking ahead, Richardson warns of technically gifted leaders whose character may not match their power; Valenzuela stresses human-machine teaming and starting from the organisation's mission.
