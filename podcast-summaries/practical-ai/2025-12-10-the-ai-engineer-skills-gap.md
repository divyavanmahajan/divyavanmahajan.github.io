## Thesis

Ramin Mohammadi, adjunct professor at Northeastern University and lead principal AI engineer at iBaseT, argues that MLOps and then generative AI have automated the routine tasks juniors used to learn on. Entry-level AI jobs now effectively demand mid-level engineers who can build, deploy and maintain systems. Universities teach theory well but move too slowly, so he wants project-based courses and more investment from industry.

## Key points

- **From notebook to pipeline.** Mohammadi traces the shift from the "sexiest job" data-science boom to MLOps (Docker, CI/CD, drift monitoring) and then generative AI. He cites an OpenAI and University of Pennsylvania study showing that repeatable junior tasks are highly exposed to LLMs.
- **Hiring for proven capability.** He says companies would rather hire five or ten people who have built complete systems than train 50 juniors. He has replaced intern work with AI himself, and entry-level postings now ask for about three years of experience.
- **The academic bottleneck.** He cites an MIT study: about 70% of AI PhDs go straight to industry, and 96% of major state-of-the-art systems come from industry labs. One of his courses took years to approve, and by then the tools had changed.
- **A project-based fix.** His Northeastern MLOps course has 150-170 students per semester building one real product in teams, ending with an MLOps Expo held with Google in Boston.
- **Parallel curricula cost money.** In his student survey, 60% take extra online courses, 82% join hackathons and 46% attend workshops. He warns that cloud costs make this self-learning inequitable and asks providers for credits.
- **Use it, don't lose it.** Whitenack describes using AI to write code for customer-lifetime modelling while he still directed the data science. Mohammadi warns that managers who only push juniors to ship with AI take away their chance to learn.
