## Thesis

Nathan Labenz interviews three TV writers during the 2023 Writers Guild of America strike. Trey Kollmer, Sophia Lear and Garrett Schabb describe the guild's AI demands as a defense of credit, pay and staffing. They do not see them as rejecting the tool. All three are curious about language models but doubt the studios will use them in writers' interests.

## Key points

- **The four demands.** Kollmer lists them: AI cannot write scripts, rewrite them or generate source material, and WGA-covered material cannot be used to train models. He fears a future with one or two showrunners and models producing first drafts, while most writers become cheap gig workers who polish and punch up. He adds that the contract only runs three years.
- **What GPT-4 can do.** Kollmer finds it good at medium-level scene structure but vague when jokes need specific language. Labenz cites GPT-4 being preferred over GPT-3.5 by only 70 to 30, and a 97% price drop for equivalent models in eight months.
- **The writers' room.** Lear says AI is decent at logline-level ideas. She argues that comedy depends on the conversation and "alchemy" of people working together, but she grants that studios may not care about quality. She is unsure contract language can help if AI truly matches writers.
- **The studio incentive.** Schabb calls it lucky that the negotiation coincided with ChatGPT's release. He says the guild believes studios want as little human contribution as possible. He mentions unconfirmed rumors that studios are generating scripts from public-domain books.
- **Personal practice.** Schabb uses GPT-4 as an assistant that keeps track of his outlines, but says it silently dropped scenes and changed details. He refuses to ask it for pitches; when he tested it anyway, it produced cliché ideas.
- **Utopian framing.** Asked about universal basic income, Schabb says he would happily give up paid writing if everyone had a high standard of living, but he doubts society will get there.
