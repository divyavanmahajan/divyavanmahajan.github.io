## Thesis

Kate Adamala, professor of genetics at the University of Minnesota, argues that mirror life (organisms built from mirror-image molecules) is one branch of the synthetic biology tech tree that should not be explored, because the risks outweigh any benefit. Nathan Labenz uses her shift from developer to opponent as a model for AI researchers: step back regularly, name the specific capabilities that would be hard to undo, and change course.

## Key points

- **Why synthetic biology.** Adamala says life uses a narrow chemical toolkit (22 amino acids, 5 nucleobases) and the field wants to expand it, both to replace petrochemicals and to understand disease and ageing. She cites Feynman: "What I cannot build, I cannot understand."
- **Building cells from both ends.** The top-down minimal cell from Craig Venter's project has 474 genes, a couple dozen of unknown function; her lab's bottom-up synthetic cell has about 85 to 90 genes and cannot yet undergo Darwinian evolution.
- **The core risk, at a high level.** The property that made mirror life attractive for drugs and contamination-free bioreactors, being invisible to immune systems, viruses and predators, is the same one that makes it dangerous. Labenz: "there's nothing more dangerous than something that nothing can eat."
- **A gradual change of mind.** Mirror life was one of Adamala's first grants as an independent PI; conversations with ecologists, immunologists and biosecurity people slowly convinced her to stop. She rejects containment as a fix because of accidents and deliberate misuse, and says most of the field expected a mirror cell within 10 to 20 years.
- **Coalition by invitation.** The group behind the Science warning asked experts to prove them wrong; none could, and many became co-authors. Her advice to AI: "carrot rather than a stick", win community buy-in before seeking regulation.
- **AI is different.** Adamala says AI is already out of the box and useful to her (she has not written code in over a year); her biggest AI worry is a model that can synthesise the literature to create new biological threats.
