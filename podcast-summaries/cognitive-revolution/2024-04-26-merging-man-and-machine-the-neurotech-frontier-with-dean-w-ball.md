## Thesis

Dean W. Ball, a Mercatus Center research fellow and author of the Hyperdimensional Substack, describes neurotech as advanced sensor fusion and signal processing. He thinks non-invasive brain reading is roughly where language models were at GPT-1 or GPT-2, and that cheap hardware plus a flood of new data could push it forward fast. Nathan Labenz and Ball agree that reading the brain will improve much faster than writing to it. Inducing complex thoughts probably needs invasive implants.

## Key points

- **EEG basics.** Ball says consumer EEG uses 2 to about 32 electrodes and lab rigs go up to 256. EEG samples about 1,000 times a second, while fMRI takes one image every 2 seconds at best. EEG only reads the cortical surface, roughly 1 to 3 mm deep.
- **Medical uses already work.** He cites EEG predicting epileptic seizures with nearly 100% accuracy up to an hour ahead, and early-onset Parkinson's signatures detected at 90%+ accuracy. He also mentions an Apple patent for electrodes in AirPods.
- **Old methods, little data.** Ball says the field still relies on Fourier transforms, CNNs and RNNs, with little transformer work. He blames academic conservatism. Labenz points to MindEye 2, which was trained on just 8 people with 30 to 40 hours each in an MRI.
- **Neuralink.** Ball calls Neuralink an invasive version of the same idea. Its first patient, a quadriplegic, used standard Windows to play chess and stayed up all night playing Civilization 6. Ball says the robotic surgeon may matter most because it lowers costs.
- **Writing to the brain.** Ball says transcranial focused ultrasound (TFUS) can change mood or focus, and inhibitory uses have mostly targeted pain. He doubts a Matrix-style skill download is possible without implants.
- **Policy.** Ball focuses on courts: lie detection would change what being under oath means, and he believes some deception is an important part of life. He expects some "digital Amish" who opt out, and says people who want to enhance their cognition should be free to do so.
