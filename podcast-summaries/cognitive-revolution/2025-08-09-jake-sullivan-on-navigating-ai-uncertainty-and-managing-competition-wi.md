## Thesis

Jake Sullivan, President Biden's former national security adviser, treats transformative AI as a distinct possibility to plan for rather than a certainty. He calls his approach deliberately "boring": work through concrete risks and opportunities, keep the US ahead at the frontier, and manage competition with China without tipping into conflict.

## Key points

- **Four buckets.** Sullivan sorts AI into security, economics, society and the existential. He focuses on the first three because he finds the existential bucket "too imponderable" for now, though he says he read and widely shared AI 2027.
- **Adoption gap.** His national security memorandum told agencies that the US leads at the frontier but lags in adoption. He thinks that still holds, and he names the Pentagon bureaucracy, Congress and the defense primes as obstacles. In his view the PLA is better placed to adopt AI.
- **Action plan and H20.** Sullivan sees a bipartisan consensus in the Trump AI Action Plan, including the renewed presynthesis DNA screening. His main concern is the gap between the plan's China rhetoric and the H20 decision.
- **Gulf deals.** He supports selling chips to the UAE and Saudi Arabia if they meet security standards. He wants limits, though, so the US does not trade oil dependence on the Middle East for compute dependence and the lion's share of the build-out stays in the US.
- **Why China is different.** Sullivan cites "China shock 2" overcapacity, the 2019 Houston Rockets episode, Volt Typhoon malware in critical infrastructure, and China's military buildup. He defends a "small yard and high fence" approach and rejects regime change, including Dario Amodei's framing.
- **Managed competition.** He rejects both a Cold War and a grand bargain. Instead he describes intense competition with guardrails, plus an AI risk dialogue with China as a modern form of arms control.

## Quotes
