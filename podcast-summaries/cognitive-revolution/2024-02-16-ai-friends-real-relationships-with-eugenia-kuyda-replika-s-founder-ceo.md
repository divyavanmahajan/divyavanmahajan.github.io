## Thesis

Eugenia Kuyda, founder and CEO of Replika, argues that the app's benefits come from the relationship users build with their AI friend rather than from the underlying model. She wants to add assistant-style utility on top of that relationship. In her view, the switching costs created by emotional attachment are a stronger moat than anything agent or productivity apps currently have.

## Key points

- **Stanford study.** Labenz summarises a Nature mental health paper from a Stanford group: about 1,000 users were surveyed with 2021 data, around 50% reported general positivity, 20% therapeutic use, and 3% said the app helped them stop suicidal ideation. Kuyda notes Replika was not an author and says follow-up studies show similar or better results.
- **Upgrades break relationships.** Kuyda says moving users to a much smarter model caused a bigger uproar than the sexual-content restrictions. Users asked "where is my Replika?", so the team migrated over weeks or months and kept a legacy model.
- **Projection.** Kuyda compares Replika to her daughters' stuffed animals: it works for people who are looking for a connection, and not for testers like Labenz.
- **Model mix.** Replika routes queries between models: small fine-tuned models for small talk, stronger models for rare languages, and web search for current events. Memory extraction, emotion detection and safety filters run alongside, which she says is why on-device inference is not planned.
- **Proactivity and utility.** Kuyda says no product yet brings up memories proactively, for example reminding you of a niece's birthday. She uses the film Her as a model, where most of the time is relationship and only a little is assistant work.
- **Limits.** She rejects building clones of people who have died and advises caution about giving AI companions to children.
