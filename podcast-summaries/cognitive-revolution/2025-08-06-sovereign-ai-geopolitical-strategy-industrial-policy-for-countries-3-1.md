## Thesis

Anjney Midha, general partner at a16z, argues that countries other than the US and China should partner for pre-training now, build local post-training and last-mile capability, and run critical inference on their own soil. In his "Marshall Plan for AI", allies own their models but build on American chips, which keeps them out of Huawei's ecosystem. Nathan Labenz, a self-described globalist, questions whether national champions can beat the frontier labs and whether export controls defeat their own purpose.

## Key points

- **Defining sovereign AI.** Midha says nobody has a canonical definition. For a European CIO it means keeping workloads clear of foreign backdoor requests such as those under the US Cloud Act. He sees hyperscalers being unbundled and rebundled by AI companies, for example OpenAI's deal with the UAE.
- **Talent on a 20-year arc.** Midha accepts most countries cannot compete at the frontier today. He points to joint ventures like Maruti Suzuki in India and the path from Standard Oil to Saudi Aramco as models for building local talent over time.
- **Value in the last mile.** Midha says ChatGPT speaks Hindi like a tourist and argues local champions win on post-training and integration, ideally on top of open-weight models. He notes it took 26 days to go from o1 to DeepSeek R1.
- **Petrodollars to petroflops.** The UAE, through G42, wants to convert oil wealth into compute. Midha says the US gets a one-to-one matching build-out at home, allies on the American stack, and 18 to 20% cheaper energy per FLOP.
- **Huawei's trajectory.** Midha says chip sanctions pushed China to double down on Huawei. He expects it to decouple inference from US chips within 2 to 3 years and warns that losing early infrastructure deals sets the next 30 years.
- **Security gaps.** Midha says US frontier labs have no real protection against weight exfiltration, and that China could take the weights today if it wanted them. So other countries should not be expected to do better.
