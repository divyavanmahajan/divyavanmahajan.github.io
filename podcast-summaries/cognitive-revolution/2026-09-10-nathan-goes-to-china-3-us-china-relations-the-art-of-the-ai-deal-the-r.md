## Thesis

In this solo finale of his China series, recorded ahead of the Trump-Xi summit, Nathan Labenz argues that US policy towards China has no stated goal beyond "winning". He proposes one: a "Pax Machina" or "Pax Robotica" in which both countries share AI's benefits without building a new AI sword of Damocles. Drawing on two weeks of meetings in China, he argues that China's rise is a return to its historical norm that containment can only delay.

## Key points

- **China has arrived.** Labenz cites PPP GDP (about $45 trillion against $30 trillion for the US) and cheap high-speed rail, and counts as many near-frontier labs in China as in the US. He also lists real constraints: an ageing population, no succession plan, an untested military and low global trust.
- **The view from Beijing.** The "century of humiliation" makes technological dependence intolerable, so the chip ban and the "coalition of democracies" in *Machines of Loving Grace* read as "they're trying to do it again." Labenz notes he never heard anyone in China frame AI as a race.
- **Distillation and model bans.** He calls it "pretty rich" for US labs to object to distillation after training on humanity's shared heritage. He opposes banning Chinese open models, proposing that insurance price the risk instead.
- **Export controls audit.** Labenz says the controls have some effect, but their rationale keeps shifting and Meituan has trained on Huawei Ascend chips. Above all they have chilled US-China AI safety collaboration.
- **The deal.** Trade chips for Chinese know-how in solar, batteries, EVs and robotics, with local manufacturing. Use formally verified security to give Huawei a route back, and clarify that safety research collaborations are allowed.
- **Keep the tech trees entangled.** Labenz's biggest fear is that diverging hardware and paradigms will make each side's capabilities illegible. He floats joint compute, an auditor's bill of rights and a convention against training AI to win wars.
