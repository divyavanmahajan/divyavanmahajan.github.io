## Thesis

Responding to the leaked "no moats" memo, Nathan Labenz tells co-host Erik Torenberg that AI inference will probably split like the cloud market: a big-tech oligopoly with about half, and a long tail of open source, on-prem and on-device. He sees OpenAI 18 months to 2 years ahead of open source and walks through nine moats. (The published transcript's speaker labels appear swapped; the Waymark and moats material is Labenz's, per the show notes.)

## Key points

- **Start with GPT-4.** At Athena, where he advises on AI, Labenz says every new automation starts with GPT-4. Cost rarely matters for task automation: turning a call transcript into a client profile costs about a quarter and replaces hours of work.
- **Value, brand, feedback.** His first three moats are GPT-3.5 Turbo's price ($2 per million tokens), corporate trust ("nobody got fired for going with IBM"), and the usage feedback loop. He contrasts ChatGPT with Bing's launch problems.
- **Pricing, compute, GPT-4.** At that price, he calculates, a rival must serve 100 billion tokens to pay one $200,000 employee. Compute is scarce, and GPT-4 itself can clean data and automate interpretability work. OpenAI has also stopped returning logits, which makes imitation harder.
- **Talent, distribution, weak lock-in.** He cites Satya Nadella asking how OpenAI did this with a couple of hundred people, and customers such as Morgan Stanley, Shopify and Khan Academy. He rates network effects weakest, since switching to Claude takes a few lines of code.
- **Safety and regulation.** Torenberg asks whether "it's not a race" talk is partly strategic. Labenz reads Sam Altman's call to regulate only the largest models, and his low-key GPT-4 launch tweet, as sincere.
- **What's investable.** Labenz expects incumbents such as Salesforce and Adobe to add AI before startups catch up. He sees AI companions growing fast but has doubts about their social effect.
