## Thesis

Nathan Labenz and BabyAGI creator Yohei Nakajima watch and pause Nakajima's TEDAI talk. Nakajima argues that identity is defined by the many overlapping groups we belong to, and that Eastern thought, which treats the mind as a tool rather than the self, makes AI feel less threatening. He hopes AI can serve as a lens for mutual understanding.

## Key points

- **Building by watching.** Nakajima improves BabyAGI, originally 105 lines of code, by running it repeatedly and noting what he would do differently. Self-reflection was added this way: reflections are embedded and the five most relevant guide the next task list.
- **Model choice.** He builds mostly on GPT-3.5 because it is cheaper and faster, treating GPT-4 as an upgrade. He stopped OCR work on pitch decks in anticipation of GPT-4V and had prototypes ready when it shipped.
- **Knowledge graphs.** His autonomous CRM prototype, tested on Game of Thrones episode summaries, dedupes nodes by having an LLM compare new nodes with the 5-10 most similar existing ones. For time, he applies a decay factor to older items in retrieval.
- **GPT store.** His VC associate GPT reached about 7,000 chats at no cost to him, because users pay OpenAI $20 a month. He remains cautious, noting OpenAI might shut it down as it did plugins.
- **East and West.** Raised in Seattle in a Japanese household, Nakajima sees Japan as more collective, so tools become part of a shared identity. He compares human society to an ant colony whose trails and tools are part of its intelligence. He and Labenz discuss weaker non-English performance, tokenization costs and national models.
- **Bridging divides.** He cites AI-written letters explaining Israeli and Palestinian grievances from the other side; Labenz describes his own fitness-chat and neighbor-dispute mediator experiments.
