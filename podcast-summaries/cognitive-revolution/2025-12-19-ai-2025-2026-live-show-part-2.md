## Thesis

The second half of the year-end live show, co-hosted by Nathan Labenz and Prakash, turns to AI politics. Assemblymember Alex Boris defends targeted catastrophic-risk regulation, Dean Ball argues the coalitions around AI are still forming as coding agents reach a new level, and forecaster Peter Wildeford argues that cheaper, more capable models will make 2026 a year of more autonomous agents and hotter politics.

## Key points

- **RAISE Act in negotiation.** Boris says the bill is in New York's "chapter amendment" process with the governor, with pressure to align it with California's SB 53. It targets extreme harms only: 100 deaths or $1 billion in damage via weapons of mass destruction or fully automated crime.
- **Super PAC backfire.** Boris says the $100 million Leading the Future PAC (backed by Andreessen Horowitz, Greg Brockman and others) named him its main target, raising its planned spend from $2 million to $10 million, which he says has boosted his donations and volunteers.
- **Coalitions in flux.** Ball, formerly AI policy adviser at the White House, sees AI safety, pro-AI and an emerging anti-AI camp competing for kid-safety and safety voters. He says Trump personally backs federal preemption and that the US is in a structural decoupling from China.
- **Rent, don't sell.** Wildeford argues Huawei covers only 1-4% of Chinese demand and Beijing will push Nvidia out once it can. He proposes renting compute (for example Oracle-run data centres in Malaysia) so access can be cut off in a conflict.
- **Revenue beat forecasts.** Wildeford says his 2025 revenue forecast failed because company projections ignored AI progress; coding tools like Claude Code and Cursor drove growth, while general computer-use agents lagged. He cites a 390x cost improvement on the ARC-AGI leaderboard.
- **2026 outlook.** Wildeford expects METR-style task horizons to grow from about two hours to a day or two, a possible "ChatGPT moment" for agents before the midterms, and affordable robot butlers only by the late 2030s.
