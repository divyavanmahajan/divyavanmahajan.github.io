## Thesis

Nathan Labenz talks with Helen Toner of CSET, the former OpenAI board member. Toner argues that new AI capabilities are expensive when first built and then get cheaper fast, so trying to stop them from spreading would need ever more invasive controls. She says society should treat the gap as an "adaptation buffer": time to build defences and resilience for a transition period, rather than a permanent lockdown.

## Key points

- **OpenAI board.** Toner says the rumour that a "Q Star" breakthrough letter drove the board's 2023 decision was false. The board knew about the reasoning research that later shipped as o1 and o3.
- **Why nonproliferation fails.** She compares the idea to nuclear nonproliferation, which works because enriched uranium is scarce. If bombs needed less and less material, inspectors would end up checking farmhouses. DeepSeek, in her view, matched US models months later and at lower cost.
- **Resilience beyond technical fixes.** She argues much of the misuse defence has nothing to do with frontier models: wastewater monitoring, vaccine capacity, FBI detection of plots, and easy-to-deploy cyber tools for water and power operators. She calls it naive to assume AI will simply favour defenders.
- **Policy building blocks.** Toner has not seen a full regulatory regime she likes. She supports disclosure of test results, safety plans and model specs, whistleblower protections tied to those disclosures, Dean Ball's private-regulator proposal, and more technical capacity in government. She warns of a Three Mile Island-style overreaction after an accident.
- **US-China.** She explains the hostility as the failure of the effort to bring China into the rules-based order. She thinks CEOs stress "beat China" because it is the path of least resistance to funding, contracts and light regulation.
- **Military decision support.** A CSET paper led by Emmy Probasco looks at uses from viewshed analysis to LLM "battle buddies" from Palantir and Scale AI, judged on scope, data and human-machine interaction. Toner doubts "AlphaGo for war" is close, because war is far messier than any game.
