## Thesis

Stephen Parker and Josh Rubin, the creative leads at Waymark, made The Frost, a 12-minute short film built from DALL-E 2 stills. They argue that AI image generators behave like an artist you do not fully control. Getting a coherent film out of one depends on filmmaking craft, contextual prompting and a strong human vision. Rubin says the process was harder than traditional animation, not easier.

## Key points

- **Images came before the story.** Rubin says Parker's first 20 or 30 DALL-E images of faces, mountains and gear suggested a world. The team then built a narrative around them, which took about three and a half months.
- **Contextual prompt wrapper.** Parker prompts by imagining how a real image would be captioned: "portraits from the film Tundar", then the subject, then a named cinematographer or director, plus fixed colours such as blue and gray to keep the look consistent.
- **Continuity through archetypes and setting.** Faces were hard to keep consistent, so recurring characters such as Dr. Ulrich were written as familiar archetypes (a gray-haired mad scientist in a lab coat). Rubin says a consistent voice, the music and the all-snow setting let viewers accept faces that change from shot to shot.
- **An active storyboard.** The team could ask for a new shot, such as a hand gripping a rope, and get it back in about 20 minutes. Most shots were also fixed with inpainting and outpainting, and a transponder prop was composited into scenes as a recurring object.
- **Motion and emotion are the limits.** Animator Matt Sessions puppeted stills in After Effects, using deliberately sluggish movement inspired by Kurosawa's "The Blizzard". Both guests say a romantic comedy would be far harder because it depends on subtle facial expression.
- **Frost 2 tools.** Rubin is using Runway Gen-2, whose output grew from 4 to 18 seconds. He says AI voices failed for part 1 but now work in a dramatic context.
