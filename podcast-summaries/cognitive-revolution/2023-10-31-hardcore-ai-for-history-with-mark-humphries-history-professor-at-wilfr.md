## Thesis

Mark Humphries, a historian at Wilfrid Laurier University, argues that the main bottleneck in history is processing vast, unstandardized archives, and that large language models, especially fine-tuned ones, have just crossed the threshold from promise to actually accelerating that work. He and Nathan Labenz also agree that AI output is becoming the minimum bar for students and knowledge workers, which raises hard social questions.

## Key points

- **Archives at scale.** Humphries led a project that digitized about 12,000,000 pages of mostly handwritten records; the First World War records in Ottawa alone would stretch about a kilometer end to end, far more than anyone can read.
- **Cost of transcription.** He compares Transkribus at about 10 cents a page with paying students $20 to $30 an hour to transcribe a few dozen pages, and expects API prices to keep falling.
- **GPT-4 Vision's quirk.** In his tests it struggles to transcribe old handwriting exactly, tending to summarize the document instead, though the summaries are good.
- **Privacy limits.** Many pension records fall under research agreements with Veterans Affairs Canada and the Privacy Act, so he cannot send them to commercial APIs and looks at models like Llama 2.
- **Fine-tuning as behavior.** Since fine-tuning arrived in August, Humphries has used contrastive examples (teaching that "my girl" in Wentzell's records means wife) and chain-of-thought tuning; untuned GPT-4 with graded relevance labels left about 75% of returned results irrelevant.
- **Teaching and history.** Humphries takes a permissive classroom policy and sees institutional costs, such as Copilot at $30 a month for 30,000 students, as the real barrier. He points to the industrial revolution and the enclosure movement as warnings about leaving displaced workers worse off.
