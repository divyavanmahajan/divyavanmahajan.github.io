## Thesis

Anastasis Germanidis, co-founder and CTO of Runway, argues that video is a more general training modality than text and that models like Gen-3 learn physics and 3D structure purely from data. He rejects the "AGI lab" label, framing Runway's goal as building better world simulators that extend human creativity. Talking with Nathan Labenz and co-host Stephen Parker of Waymark, he holds that what you train a model to do matters as much as how much compute you spend.

## Key points

- **Emergent physics from scale.** Germanidis says the jump from Gen-2 to Gen-3 came largely from compute, producing surprisingly accurate liquid simulation and 3D consistency with no physics priors built in, though gravity and a bouncing ball remain hard.
- **Data over architecture.** He thinks the field over-focuses on architectures, warns that papers on alternatives such as linear attention often compare unfairly on compute, and prefers careful data and training-regime choices over inductive biases.
- **Not an AGI lab.** He calls AGI a poorly defined milestone and says better models also get better at novel, out-of-distribution combinations, which suits surreal creative work; audio is planned but visual quality comes first.
- **Shipping and staying small.** Germanidis describes continuous shipping as core to Runway's culture since it was three people; the company deliberately stays smaller than needed, and leadership stays technical. Its 48-hour film competition drew around 1,800 teams.
- **Production use.** Studios and ad agencies use Gen-3 for storyboarding and B-roll. He finds the Doom game-engine paper a less efficient game engine without generalisation, and is more excited by Genie, which generates controllable platformer games and generalises to new environments.
- **Competing with giants.** Against well-funded rivals, he argues task choice can save compute; Runway's API already serves Canva and is expanding, which matters for Labenz's image-to-video use case at Waymark.
