## Thesis

In this crossover, Nathan Labenz is the guest on the Nick Halaris Show and gives a broad overview of AI for a general audience. He describes himself as an "adoption accelerationist and hyperscaling pauser": use current models such as GPT-4 to cut the cost of medicine, law and other services now, but put fewer resources into scaling and more into interpretability before building systems more powerful than humans.

## Key points

- **Waymark as a case study.** Labenz says generative AI doubled Waymark's business in 2023 while breaking even. A business URL plus a short prompt now produces a finished 30-second commercial in about 30 seconds; before, the same work took about an hour plus days of waiting for voice-over.
- **Upside by sector.** He cites blind studies where AI outperforms human doctors on diagnosis through chat at well under one percent of the cost, AlphaFold 3, a Google shipping tool that needs 15% fewer ships, and a food-plating robot that has packed some 20 million meals.
- **Social contract.** Labenz calls the question of displaced workers under-theorised, notes Sam Altman's personally funded UBI experiments, and warns that disruption will now happen within careers rather than across generations. He gives at least a 10% chance that progress stalls.
- **Why worry.** He explains how RLHF could teach models to separate the truth from what earns a high score, along with instrumental convergence and Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude steering demo. He also cites OpenAI's failure to give the superalignment team the 20% of compute it had promised, at the same time as a planned $100 billion data center.
- **China and chips.** Labenz thinks China is not far behind, says the chip ban gives Chinese chipmakers a captive market, and argues that an AI arms race would be more dangerous than the nuclear one.
- **Proposed path.** He favours domain-specific work such as OpenAI's custom model for Harvey in law over raw scale-up: "$100 billion in projects" for medicine, law and shipping instead of one data center.
