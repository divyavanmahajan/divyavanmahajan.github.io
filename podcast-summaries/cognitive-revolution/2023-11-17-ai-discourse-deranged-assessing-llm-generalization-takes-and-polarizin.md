## Thesis

Nathan Labenz argues that two recent online debates went wrong because people mixed up what is true about AI with what should be done about it. He says a narrow Google DeepMind study was turned into a viral claim that LLMs cannot generalize, and that the hostile reaction to voluntary responsible-AI commitments from venture firms works against the tech industry's own interest. Erik Torenberg puts the case for the critics, who see the commitments as a wedge for regulators.

## Key points

- **Accelerationist credentials first.** Labenz cites a Waymo and Swiss Re study: over 3.8 million rider-only miles with zero bodily injury claims, against a human baseline of 1.11 per million miles. He also backs wider use of GPT-4V, citing a Harvard study where it outperformed human respondents on 934 image cases.
- **What the paper showed.** Labenz says the paper, "Pretraining Data Mixtures Enable Narrow Model Selection Capabilities in Transformer Models", trained toy models on lines and sine curves. A reproduction by Samuel Muller found that adding noise to the training data let the model handle the combined case.
- **Critics of the hype.** He disputes takes from Naveen Rao, Arvind Narayanan, Jim Fan and Replit CEO Amjad. He argues the training data holds far more than any human can extract, citing GPT-4's command of the world's languages as one example of superhuman ability.
- **Scout versus soldier.** Borrowing from Julia Galef, Labenz credits more careful readings from Ethan Mollick, Meg Mitchell and Lee Sharkey.
- **The VC commitments.** A nonprofit co-founded by Hemant Taneja gathered 35+ VC firms and 15+ companies behind five commitments. Labenz calls them common sense. Torenberg relays the view of critics such as Balaji, who frame them as capitulation and point to social media's experience with regulators.
- **Product liability challenge.** Labenz says only 5 to 10 companies face the executive order's reporting rule, and he asks critics why AI products should be exempt from existing product liability law.
