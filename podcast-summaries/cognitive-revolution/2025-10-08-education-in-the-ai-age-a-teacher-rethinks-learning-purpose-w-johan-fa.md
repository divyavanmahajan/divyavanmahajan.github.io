## Thesis

Johan Falk, a former teacher who led AI work at Sweden's National Agency for Education, tells Nathan Labenz that the pace of AI change is the central problem for education systems, where changing a curriculum takes five years. He argues the most urgent task is teaching students about AI, while using AI as a learning tool should stay a teacher-by-teacher choice until the evidence improves. He also suggests that the age of grades is ending.

## Key points

- **Three purposes, four buckets.** Falk describes education as serving the labour market, citizenship and personal growth. He sorts AI in education into AI for learning, teacher productivity, teaching about AI, and system-level change.
- **Thin evidence.** He says studies are often two years old on publication and small, such as one based on 25 adults in Nigeria and another on 18 people writing essays. He names South Korea, Singapore, Estonia, China (Squirrel) and Khanmigo as notable approaches.
- **Grading with care.** Falk calls AI detectors a dead end. If teachers use AI grading anyway, he recommends rubrics with numeric scales, checks for bias against groups such as non-native speakers, an appeal route and transparency, which he thinks would also satisfy the EU AI Act.
- **What students need.** His urgent topics are using AI for learning and understanding AI companions, which he would prohibit for under-18s. Labenz adds that "just predicting the next token" and outdated hallucination concerns lead people to underestimate the technology.
- **System change.** Falk expects AI tutors to turn teachers into mentors, as at Alpha School, and points to vanishing entry-level jobs and possible UBI. He doubts society will invest in finding the top 3% of talent.
- **Habits of mind.** He tells teachers to make more mistakes and to use AI to save five minutes a day.
