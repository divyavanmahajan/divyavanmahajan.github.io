## Thesis

In the second half of a live show co-hosted with Prakash (@8teAPi), Nathan Labenz asks three guests how close AI is to a tipping point. All three see real progress, but each points to something that holds it back: costly real-world validation in medicine, deep disagreement about automated AI R&D, and fragile physical infrastructure in US-China competition. Labenz closes by noting how little consensus exists among even well-informed experts.

## Key points

- **AI for medicine is bottlenecked by validation.** Abhi Mahajan of Noetik AI argues the most clinically valuable problems lack cheap, verifiable ground truth, so the RL gains seen in maths and code will not transfer easily. Noetik trains a self-supervised model on multimodal tumour data to predict which patients respond to treatment. He cites the figure that 97% of oncology trials fail, and argues that better patient selection matters more than generating more candidates.
- **No consensus on automated AI R&D.** Helen Toner describes a CSET workshop whose report, "When AI Builds AI", failed to reach a consensus and concluded that automated AI R&D is a source of strategic surprise. She argues the key disagreements are whether AI fully replaces human researchers and how soon other bottlenecks bite (an Amdahl's law argument). Her own default is a transformative but not unlimited "superhuman plateau".
- **Policy asks.** Toner welcomes state transparency laws (she cites SB 1047 and New York's RAISE) but wants independent audits, continuous rather than release-based risk evaluation, and investment in societal resilience. She also says assumptions about open models staying close behind closed ones should be revisited.
- **Infrastructure is the real chessboard.** Jeremie Harris of Gladstone AI argues that security debates ignore physical "bones": Chinese-made grid components, a TSMC that would not survive an invasion, and data-centre "one-way doors" such as who reviews site plans. He believes export controls have worked, doubts a trustworthy US-China treaty, and calls for credible deterrent options.
- **Timelines and p(doom).** Harris gives a 10-90% range for loss of control and says 2027 is plausible enough to plan around. Prakash argues adoption is moving faster than expected because small capability gains suddenly "clear the hurdle".
