## Thesis

In this weekly highlights episode, partly narrated in his cloned voice, Nathan Labenz reports from The Curve under Chatham House rules. A senior frontier-lab executive told him there is likely a level of intelligence we should not pass, and called a cap on pretraining compute "reasonable". Guest segments argue that AI already changes chip design, software work and training data. Labenz closes by arguing that "it only works for verifiable tasks" is no longer a safe assumption.

## Key points

- **Notes from The Curve.** By Labenz's account, insiders say pretraining keeps delivering via synthetic data and that models may have research taste that RL has not yet drawn out. When he floated a cap of around 10^27 FLOPs, an executive said it could be reasonable. Attendees saw two or three labs at the frontier, with RL-environment vendors acting as an indirect distillation channel, and talked about "next year" rather than 2028. Co-host Prakash Narayanan argues a cap would mean limiting compute build-out.
- **The memory wall.** Positron co-founder Thomas Sohmers says memory prices rose 4.5x in a year. He claims GPUs use 30-40% of memory bandwidth during decode, against 93% for Positron's first chip. Asimov will hold up to 2.3 TB per chip, and he says looped transformers save memory capacity rather than bandwidth.
- **Tokens versus salaries.** Sohmers says agents now run closed-loop verification on Cadence Palladium emulators. Token spend became Positron's largest non-manufacturing cost, briefly exceeding salaries and peaking above $100,000 a day.
- **Software after agents.** Sean Wang (swyx) says plugged-in engineers are in more demand than ever, but staff producing unreviewed "slop" are under performance review. He reports a CPU shortage among companies he interviews. His Kill My SaaS bounty led him to replace a subscription of more than $40,000 a year. He argues the market for custom software is not fixed.
- **Unowned risks and training data.** Evan Miyazono of Atlas Ignota proposes cryptographic attestation of inference providers to trace accidental agent swarms, and expects a "shock and awe" moment in software verification. Edward Hu of Mercor describes "scattergunning", where agents hedge with many answers to game a rubric, fixed in APEX 1.1.
- **Beyond verifiable tasks.** Narayanan says Claude Fable composed a music track by playing instruments in a digital audio workstation. Labenz takes this as evidence of strong generalisation beyond domains with verifiable rewards.
