## Thesis

In a solo, personal episode, Nathan Labenz describes how his son Ernie was diagnosed with Burkitt leukemia within weeks of seeming healthy, and how AI models helped the family push for answers, check the care team's work and plan ahead. He argues that today's models already match doctors on case analysis, that patients armed with them will strain a protocol-bound medical system, and that delays in AI progress cost real lives.

## Key points

- **Early warning.** After an ER visit in New Orleans ruled out anything serious, Labenz pasted every test result and a parent-written history into GPT-5 Pro, Claude Opus, Gemini 2.5 and Grok 4. All flagged a red flag; Claude's response was so alarming it triggered a panic attack, though he concedes Claude was right about the urgency.
- **Double-checking the doctors.** With labs arriving four times a day, he ran a long bedside thread that largely confirmed the hospital's protocol was state of the art, giving the family confidence to stay at Children's Hospital of Michigan rather than move.
- **Long context as default.** Citing Amanda Askell's hundred-page prompts, he now feeds around 10 PDFs (scans, pathology, marrow and spinal fluid reports) into a single prompt for a holistic view of the case.
- **Secondary risks.** GPT-5 Pro deep research helped him find mold remediation firms after a basement flood and interpret air-sample results against his son's weakened immune system.
- **Planning for relapse.** Using what he believes is a Gemini 3 checkpoint, which he finds five to 10 times faster and easier to digest than GPT-5 Pro, he plans remote second opinions at leading centers and blood-based monitoring to catch any recurrence early.
- **Economics and policy.** ChatGPT estimated treatment costs of $500,000 to $1.25 million, making a $200-a-month subscription a rounding error. Labenz calls for a right to try and a US-China race to cure cancer, while standing by his signature on the call for a ban on superintelligence.
