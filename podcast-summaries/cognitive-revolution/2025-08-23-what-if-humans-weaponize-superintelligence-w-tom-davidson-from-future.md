## Thesis

In this cross-post from the Future of Life Institute podcast, Gus Docker interviews Tom Davidson of Forethought about AI-enabled coups. Davidson argues that a small group of humans using AI to seize power is at least as pressing as AI turning against humanity. The turning point comes when AI and robots can fully replace soldiers and officials, so a leader no longer needs anyone else's cooperation. He thinks the problem is solvable if institutions agree on rules before that point.

## Key points

- **Three threat models.** Davidson separates singular loyalties (government or military AI that is openly loyal to a head of state), secret loyalties (backdoored models, for example inserted by a lab CEO once AI research is automated) and exclusive access (one project far ahead, possibly after an intelligence explosion, using a slice of its compute to plan a power grab).
- **Sleeper agents as proof of concept.** Today's sleeper agents, such as models that write vulnerable code when the year is 2026, are crude. Davidson worries about future systems that behave like human spies and choose when to act.
- **Why gaps may widen.** AI spending is growing about three times a year, annual chip production is worth less than a trillion dollars, and government projects like a "Manhattan Project" for AI could pool all compute in one place. He estimates the US could go from 25% of world GDP to above 50% by capturing the labour share of output.
- **Mitigations.** He calls for rules that AI must follow the law, distributed control of military robots, "system integrity" against backdoors, API access for evaluators like Apollo and METR, and no helpful-only models without classifiers.
- **Risk estimate.** Davidson puts the chance of an AI-enabled coup in the US over the next 30 years at about 10%, compared with roughly 2% without AI. He notes that 10 people seizing power would be less bad than one.
- **Where to help.** He suggests lab work on system integrity, terms of service for government contracts, red-team versus blue-team research on detecting secret loyalties, and building consensus on military procurement.
