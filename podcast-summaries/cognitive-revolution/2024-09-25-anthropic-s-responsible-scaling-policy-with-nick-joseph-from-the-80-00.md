## Thesis

In this crosspost from the 80,000 Hours Podcast, Rob Wiblin interviews Nick Joseph, head of training at Anthropic, about the company's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP). Joseph argues RSPs separate the question of whether a model is dangerous from what to do about it, so sceptics and worriers can agree on them. Wiblin presses on trust, vague wording and enforcement, and concludes that such policies should eventually move into legislation and external audit. Nathan Labenz's intro adds that the public deserves to see frontier labs' safety plans.

## Key points

- **How the RSP works.** Joseph describes AI Safety Levels: red-line capabilities (CBRN misuse, large-scale cyberattacks, autonomous replication), conservative "yellow line" evaluations well short of them, and required precautions such as stronger security and intensive red teaming before deployment. Claude 3 was rated ASL-2.
- **Incentive alignment.** Joseph says reaching ASL-3 without safeguards would block revenue, so safety teams feel product-style pressure; about 8% of Anthropic staff work on security.
- **Safety buffer.** Evaluations rerun roughly every 4x jump in "effective compute"; Joseph says the jump from Claude 2 to Claude 3 was bigger than that gap. Critics think the buffer is too thin.
- **Trust and enforcement.** Wiblin cites OpenAI's board episode as a reason not to rely on internal structures. Joseph points to board approval, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, employee buy-in and whistleblowing, but agrees RSPs are no substitute for regulation and argues a blanket pause would adversely select for the most careful labs.
- **Biggest worries.** Joseph names under-elicitation of capabilities and unknown unknowns; outsiders can help by building evaluations and threat models.
- **Careers.** Joseph moved from a GiveWell internship to Vicarious, was rejected twice by OpenAI's safety team, then joined Anthropic. He urges strong engineering over research prestige; on his team 60-70% lean engineering.
