## Thesis

Logan Kilpatrick argues that the DevDay launches lower the barrier to building AI products rather than killing startups, and that OpenAI ships products like ChatGPT, GPTs and the Assistants API so society can adapt to AI gradually. Nathan Labenz probes the details: pricing, fine-tuning, modalities, auth and safety.

## Key points

- **Thin wrappers were never startups.** Kilpatrick rejects the "how many startups did OpenAI kill" meme, saying DevDay probably created thousands of new startups and gave existing builders lower prices and more features.
- **Assistants stop short of agents on purpose.** GPTs and assistants have no self-planning or background actions yet; Kilpatrick says this reflects both unsolved reliability problems and a deliberate choice to give people time to adjust.
- **A ladder of customisation.** GPT-3.5 Turbo fine-tuning now supports 16k context, GPT-4 fine-tuning is an experimental programme open only to people who have used 3.5 fine-tuning, and custom models start at 2 to 3 million dollars with OpenAI researchers involved end to end.
- **Model and modality details.** The GPT-4 1106 "preview" label means an early release candidate, expected to go general in weeks. Low-res vision resizes images to 512 x 512; Labenz notes text-to-speech is about an order of magnitude cheaper than 11 Labs, and Whisper Large v3 targets non-English languages.
- **Developer features.** Seeded, reproducible outputs with a system fingerprint aim to make testing possible; log probabilities for output tokens are coming. Actions in GPTs add hybrid auth and a "consequential" flag that prompts users before an action runs.
- **Safety and trust.** Kilpatrick repeats that OpenAI does not train on API data, describes Copyright Shield as a way to reassure legal departments, and points to the 18-page GPT-4 Vision system card as public evidence of red-teaming work.
