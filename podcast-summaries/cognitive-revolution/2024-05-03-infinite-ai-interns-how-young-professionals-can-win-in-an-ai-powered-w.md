## Thesis

In a talk to the Texas State University Computer Science Club, Nathan Labenz argues that AI is closing in on expert performance on routine tasks, which gives every company "infinite interns" and makes junior hiring less attractive. His answer for students is to lean into what mid- and late-career workers will not do: learn the tools, automate real business processes, and become the person who can measure whether AI is working.

## Key points

- **State of the art.** Labenz cites GPT-4 at 86% on the MMLU-style exam benchmark versus 90% for domain experts, and Med-PaLM 2 at 86% on medical licensing questions, beating doctors on 8 of 9 criteria as judged by doctors. AI still produces "precious few eureka moments".
- **Tale of the cognitive tape.** He scores AI ahead on breadth, speed (at least 10 times faster and cheaper), parallelism and bedside manner, and humans ahead on depth, breakthrough insight, memory and adversarial robustness, citing Far AI's attack on a superhuman Go AI.
- **Three modes of production.** Copilot mode (real-time help you must check), delegation or task automation mode (a narrow, tested process you no longer review item by item) and agent mode, which he expects the next big OpenAI release to unlock.
- **Practical tips.** Pay for the best models (GPT-4, Claude 3, Gemini 1.5), try Cursor, Claude for Sheets, Anthropic's meta prompt and Devin, and use "prompt before app" when automating a process with no-code tools such as Zapier.
- **Evals as a career edge.** He demos a Claude for Sheets eval at Waymark that flags video scripts with misplaced contact information, and describes a 1,000-employee firm that moved half its internal Q&A to a chatbot fed 250 documents.
- **Audience Q&A.** He advises students to pass their classes but not to fear using AI at work, says Bloomberg found GPT-4 beat its own model, and recommends giving Claude 3 many examples before reaching for fine-tuning.
