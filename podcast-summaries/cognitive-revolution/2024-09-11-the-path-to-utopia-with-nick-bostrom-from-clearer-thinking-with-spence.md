## Thesis

In this cross-post from Clearer Thinking, Spencer Greenberg interviews Nick Bostrom about his book Deep Utopia. Bostrom argues that if AI goes well, we could reach a "post instrumental" condition where nothing truly has to be done by humans. That removes the "exoskeleton" of necessities that gives our lives shape, and forces a rethink of what makes a life valuable. He insists he is not selling utopia, only trying to take its implications seriously.

## Key points

- **Why utopias are hard to imagine.** Bostrom notes most people can name dystopias (Brave New World, 1984) but not a single utopia; piling up improvements soon feels artificial, like plastic surgery taken too far.
- **Abundance went into consumption.** Citing Keynes' essay predicting people would be 4 to 8 times richer and barely work, Bostrom says we work maybe 25% less, while much of the extra wealth goes into positional goods such as ever-bigger yachts.
- **Layers of value in a solved world.** Bostrom builds up from pleasure, which he says is underrated, to "experience texture" (taking pleasure in beauty or truth), then artificial purpose such as games, then real purposes that come from other people's preferences and traditions.
- **Quiet values.** He compares subtle values (aesthetics, honouring ancestors, upholding traditions) to stars hidden by daylight: they may only become visible once urgent problems are solved.
- **AI companions.** Greenberg says consciousness of a conversation partner matters to him; Bostrom suggests people may stop caring about the question and warns against stopping at the initial "yuck reaction".
- **Governance and surveillance.** Bostrom separates the alignment, governance and digital-minds problems. He says current AI could already enable mass sentiment analysis of what people write, and calls himself agnostic on whether more transparency would be good.
