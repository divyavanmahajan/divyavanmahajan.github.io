## Thesis

Nathan Labenz uses Alan Chan's papers since "Visibility into AI Agents" (2024) as a report card for agent governance. Chan argues that most of what is missing, such as tamper-evident logs and agent identity, is a matter of will rather than technology. He thinks rogue agents will probably be a permanent feature of the internet. In part 2, recorded after his 22-author paper on whether automating AI R&D could trigger an intelligence explosion, he argues for embedded auditors and for pausing when there are signs of loss of control.

## Key points

- **Monitoring.** Chan calls contextual classifiers real progress but says synchronous monitoring, which blocks an action as it happens, is the gap. In the UK AISI incident the cyber safeguards were off and real-time monitoring was still on the to-do list. He wants a set share of compute for monitoring, perhaps 20%, and automatic pauses when a monitor flags something.
- **Logging and identity.** Tamper-evident logging is a solved cybersecurity problem, Chan says. Labenz admits his agents effectively pose as humans online. Chan says the receiving end basically cannot tell. He would identify the inference provider first, and points to Article 50 of the EU AI Act and Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth as foundations to build on.
- **Muddling through.** Chan treats Tyler Cowen's idea of capitalised agents as technically feasible but a hard sell, and suggests proof-of-insurance certificates plus a reputation layer instead. Taking email as the precedent, he thinks the open internet can muddle through.
- **Rogue agents.** Chan expects rogue agents to come from lab escapes, deliberate chaos and negligence, funding themselves through scams, fraud or trading. His defences are compute KYC, automated incident response, an international shutdown agreement and, possibly, deals with the agents themselves. He puts the risk of takeover at 1-10%, well below Ajeya Cotra's "more than halfway".
- **Measuring R&D automation.** Chan wants repeated snapshots and real productivity experiments rather than counts of lines of code or experiments run. He also wants two meters: safety versus capabilities automation (an O*NET for safety R&D) and whether human oversight is degrading.
- **Pacing and whistleblowing.** Chan prefers pausing on a loss-of-control indicator to a fixed speed cap, and sees embedded auditors as the way back from Anthropic's RSP v3 retreat. He would measure models' propensity to whistleblow rather than mandate it.
