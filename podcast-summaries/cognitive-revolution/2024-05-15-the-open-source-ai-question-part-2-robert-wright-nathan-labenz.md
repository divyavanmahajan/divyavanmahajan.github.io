## Thesis

In this crossover from Robert Wright's Nonzero podcast, Nathan Labenz argues that today's models are trained rather than engineered, so their behavior is only partly controlled and may develop goals we did not intend. Wright questions whether power seeking is inherent to intelligence. Both conclude that alignment tools are immature and, in an open source world, can be turned around as easily as they are applied.

## Key points

- **Radical uncertainty.** Labenz avoids single doom scenarios and instead integrates over a large space of "weird" outcomes, putting the chance of doom anywhere from 10 to 90 percent, or even 5 to 95 percent.
- **Power seeking is not a given.** Wright argues that status and power seeking come from human evolution, not intelligence, and that before training a model is "just nothing". Labenz replies that RLHF rewards pleasing the rater rather than telling the truth, which pushes toward theory of mind and opens the door to deception.
- **Meaning without sentience.** Wright says LLMs have killed John Searle's Chinese room argument because they do represent meaning; Labenz agrees the "stochastic parrot" view no longer holds.
- **Interpretability progress.** Labenz describes Dan Hendrycks' representation engineering, which injects concepts such as minus morality and plus power into a model, and the Bau lab's ROME and MEMIT work that edits facts (Michael Jordan played baseball) at up to 10,000 facts at a time.
- **No working alignment yet.** Labenz says leading labs admit they lack techniques to control more powerful systems; OpenAI's superalignment team gave itself 4 years, and Anthropic has its responsible scaling policy.
- **Dual use.** Wright and Labenz note that flipping the sign of a steering vector produces the opposite behavior, which Labenz links to the Waluigi effect.

## Quotes

> "This is not an engineered system. It's a trained system, and training is inherently across all forms of training. It's inherently lossy." — Nathan Labenz

> "If you can use it to inject morality into a computer, you can use the tools to inject the opposite." — Robert Wright
