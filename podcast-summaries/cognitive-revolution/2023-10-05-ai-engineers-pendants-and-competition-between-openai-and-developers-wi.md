## Thesis

Swyx argues that the "AI engineer", a software engineer specialising in the emerging AI stack rather than training models, is becoming a distinct full-time role because demand far outstrips the supply of ML researchers. With Nathan Labenz he also debates agent timelines and OpenAI's growing overlap with the developers who build on it, which Swyx sees as normal platform tension that startups survive by shipping fast.

## Key points

- **Supply and demand.** Swyx estimates there are perhaps 5,000 good LLM researchers in the world, so most companies will rely on engineers who apply models via APIs; his free seven-day email course (Latent Space University) covers the baseline skills.
- **Buy first, then build.** He advises starting with tools such as Humanloop, Guardrails and LangChain, and ripping them out once you understand your domain, noting JSON output validation became obsolete when OpenAI shipped its own.
- **Narrow agents.** Swyx is uninterested in open-ended agents like Auto-GPT's "increase my net worth" prompt, and points to Calendly-style scheduling and Code Interpreter as agents that already work.
- **Issue-to-PR bet.** Swyx puts reliable code agents about five years out, defining success as a non-technical person managing a project by filing issues; Labenz expects it in two to three years, citing MedPalm 2 reports preferred over radiologists 40% of the time.
- **Sherlocking.** Swyx says every large platform eventually competes with its developers; he doubts OpenAI's promise not to compete is ironclad and says startups should prove they are strong engineering teams.
- **AI bundle and pendants.** Labenz pitches a $100-a-month bundle of 1,000 AI apps, noting Waymark spends about $0.15 per free user; Swyx compares it to Setapp and warns SaaS firms want to own their customers. Swyx says he would wear an AI pendant.
