## Thesis

James Yu, co-founder of the fiction-writing assistant Sudowrite, argues that language models are most useful to writers as a supportive partner that keeps the human in charge, not as a replacement. The hard engineering problem, in his view, is giving the model the right slice of a story's context for each task. Nathan Labenz uses the conversation to pull out prompting techniques and to discuss the backlash Yu faced after a product demo went viral.

## Key points

- **Started on DaVinci.** Yu and co-founder Amit built Sudowrite as a toy in late 2020 on the first GPT-3. Its first feature, Wormhole, offered alternative next paragraphs as cards to consider, and hallucinations were treated as a feature for fiction.
- **Less lonely writers.** Early users told Yu the tool helped them get unstuck and made them feel less lonely, which shaped the homepage pitch of a "non-judgmental" partner willing to read 30 drafts.
- **Story Engine and model mixing.** Yu describes a hierarchical system from a 50,000-foot view down to scenes, with vector search to pick context. He says GPT-4 follows rules but sounds stiff, Claude writes better prose, and Sudowrite still uses DaVinci models; some authors "kitbash" outputs from several models.
- **Prompt tips.** Yu puts style instructions at the end of long prompts, avoids "don't" instructions (his "pink elephant syndrome"), keeps temperature relatively high, and targets authors using about one in two outputs.
- **The backlash.** Yu's launch video reached more than 8 million people amid the Writers Guild strike. He says about 99% of viewers watched only the first 5 seconds and would now lead with a customer showing their own workflow.
- **The business.** Sudowrite raised a small round in 2021, has 7 people including 4 developers, and has been profitable since March. Yu says AI startups still face the usual SaaS problems of pricing, churn and distribution.
