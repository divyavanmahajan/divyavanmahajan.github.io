## Thesis

Neal Khosla, founder and CEO of Curai Health, argues that medicine has barely changed in 60 years because it still rations doctor time, and that large language models now make it possible to rebuild care around data and computing. In his view the core language capability already exists; the remaining work is safety engineering, testing, doctor oversight and fitting into health systems and regulation.

## Key points

- **Medicine runs on opinion.** Khosla cites a review finding about 11% of clinical guidelines rest on Grade A evidence, with doctors following guidelines about half the time, and wants longitudinal patient data to inform treatment.
- **Regression tests for clinical AI.** Curai runs large sets of clinical test cases against its models, Khosla says, so that non-deterministic and changing models do not quietly get worse at, for example, antibiotic cases.
- **Better benchmarks and agents.** Curai stripped the multiple-choice options from USMLE-style questions and used GPT-based scoring of open-ended answers. Its Dialogue-Enabled Resolving Agents paper pairs a "decider" and a "researcher" instance of GPT-4 in Socratic dialogue and reports state-of-the-art results.
- **OpenAI and general models.** Khosla calls OpenAI's lead an "inconvenient truth" and argues domain-specific models are overstated; his example is a patient whose lung problem traced back to dust at Burning Man, which needs world knowledge.
- **Go-to-market.** Curai offers a $14.99-a-month consumer app but focuses on health plans and provider systems. AI handles intake and triage, doctors make the decisions, and the language model was then reaching about 5% of patients in a slow rollout.
- **Regulation and global access.** Khosla wants doctor-supervised AI treated as decision support, with autonomy only after FDA-style evidence (citing IDx-DR). He expects poorer countries to adopt faster, notes local drug supply chains as a gap, and sees a moral imperative where care is absent.
