## Thesis

Ben Buchanan and Tantum Collins, co-authors of *The Bitter Struggle*, defend the Biden-era plan: use the allied chokehold on compute to build a lead over China without racing, then "spend that lead on safety". Nathan Labenz asks whether the lead can actually buy safety and whether cooperating with China would be better.

## Key points

- **How they became scaling-pilled.** Buchanan points to Dario Amodei's "Big Blob of Compute" essay, Rich Sutton's "The Bitter Lesson" (2019) and the release of GPT-3 in May 2020. Collins says working at DeepMind felt like Severance, because friends outside told him he worked at a cult.
- **Individuals matter.** Buchanan arrived believing in structural forces and left struck by how much a handful of people decided. He compares their role to Alexander Sachs, who carried the Einstein-Szilard letter to Roosevelt.
- **The scorecard.** Buchanan estimates the US and its allies still hold roughly a 100:1 advantage in producing compute and 15:1 in access to it, despite weak enforcement under Trump. What has been missing, he says, is the conviction that the technology matters.
- **Engaging China.** Buchanan describes the Woodside summit, the May 2024 Geneva talks and the Lima agreement on keeping AI out of nuclear weapons decisions. He would not sell GPUs as a precondition to talks. Collins says being a China hawk and favouring a US slowdown go together.
- **Regulating the labs.** Buchanan cites 1870s railroad standards as proof that regulation can make an industry safer and faster. His preferred blunt instrument is a ban on closed-loop recursive self-improvement. He warns that a pause tied only to a date risks a compute overhang.
- **Politics and the Bitter Temptation.** Buchanan says 56% of voters called AI a crucial or major factor in 2026 and predicts it will be the top issue in 2028. Collins warns that AI could make governments far more capable while elections stay a low-bandwidth form of oversight. His answer is to expand popular control as state capacity grows.
