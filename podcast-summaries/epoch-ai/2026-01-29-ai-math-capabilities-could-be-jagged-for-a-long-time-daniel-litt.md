## Thesis

Daniel Litt, a mathematics professor at the University of Toronto, argues that AI's mathematical ability is jagged in the same way it is jagged everywhere else. Models handle contest-style problems and lower the cost of trying things, but they cannot yet work on a problem for months, learn new objects, or come up with new techniques. Epoch AI's Greg Burnham and Anson Ho press him on timelines and on how benchmarks such as FrontierMath and the new Open Problems set should measure progress.

## Key points

- **Current ceiling.** Litt places today's best systems at roughly mid- to low-tier IMO level, around a problem a strong high-school student or undergraduate could solve in a couple of hours. He expects mildly interesting but neglected conjectures to keep falling over the next year.
- **Limited uplift so far.** He says literature search beats Google Scholar but adds perhaps a percent or two to productivity. Models help most where code helps, such as inequalities, and least in algebraic geometry.
- **Learning is the bottleneck.** Litt says humans "make friends" with new objects by playing with them over time, while a model needs retraining to learn something like Peter Scholze's Gestalten. He sees the obstacles to autonomous math research as the same ones that block automating any long task.
- **Junk papers.** He counted 12 or 13 arXiv algebraic geometry papers on the Hodge conjecture in September and October; all but one were nonsense and, judging by style, LLM-written.
- **Forecasts.** On his bet with Tamay Besiroglu, Litt still puts about 25% on AI producing Annals-level papers by around 2030, with a marginal cost of about $100,000. He gives the Riemann hypothesis roughly 15% in ten years and expects no Millennium problem to be solved autonomously by AI in that time.
- **What benchmarks measure.** Litt argues benchmarks mostly test knowledge. Burnham reports that Gemini 2.5 Deep Think's FrontierMath scores fall as background and execution ratings rise but show no correlation with creativity ratings.

## Quotes

> "So I think right now, at least where I expect AI to have a significant impact is that the marginal cost of, like, trying something is getting very small." — Daniel Litt

> "a lot of the jaggedness we see in math, I think it's just the same jaggedness we see everywhere. I don't think there's anything special." — Daniel Litt
