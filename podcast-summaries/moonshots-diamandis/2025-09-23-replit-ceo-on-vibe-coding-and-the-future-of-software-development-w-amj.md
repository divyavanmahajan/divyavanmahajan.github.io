## Thesis

Amjad Masad, co-founder and CEO of Replit, argues that programming in plain language is a shift as big as the compiler or the stored-program computer. He thinks that when anyone can build software and agents, talent will be found through what people build rather than their credentials. In his view, firms will shrink and entrepreneurship will become the default career. Peter Diamandis, Salim Ismail and Dave Blundin push on what this means for companies, competition and AGI. The conversation was recorded live at Imagination in Action.

## Key points

- **Origin story.** Masad describes writing software for an internet cafe in Jordan, building a browser-based coding sandbox that Codecademy built on, and working on React Native at Facebook. He says Replit got into Y Combinator after Sam Altman and Paul Graham found it on Hacker News, despite earlier rejections and a Rickrolled application video.
- **History of coding.** Masad traces programming from rewiring machines through von Neumann and Grace Hopper's push to program in English. He argues that critics have called every higher-level abstraction too high-level, and they have always been wrong.
- **Permissionless innovation in firms.** Masad cites a Zillow product manager whose vibe-coded work raised conversion and led to 500 licences. He says Replit uses Replit internally, for example sales staff analysing call transcripts. Ismail calls this permissionless disruptive innovation.
- **Agents and the end of Coase.** Masad previews Agent 3, which can build other agents and test its own code. He says Replit ran it for four and a half hours. Masad and Ismail argue that if agents lower transaction costs, full-time employment looks like "a bug" and companies will shrink.
- **Competition.** Masad says Replit differs from front-end-only tools because it provisions databases and handles deployment. He calls the market "total war" and notes that Google is both a partner and a competitor.
- **Models and AGI.** Masad says Replit may return to training its own models, citing a computer-use architecture he describes as three times faster and 15 times cheaper than frontier labs' versions. Masad and Blundin suggest AGI may already be here, while Ismail calls that unmeasurable.
