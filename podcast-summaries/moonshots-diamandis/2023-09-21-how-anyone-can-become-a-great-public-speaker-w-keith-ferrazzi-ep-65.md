## Thesis

Peter Diamandis and executive coach Keith Ferrazzi, author of "Never Eat Alone", treat keynote speaking as a skill anyone can learn through practice and as a business built on a credential, usually a book, and on personal relationships. Diamandis credits a lunch with Ferrazzi a decade earlier with launching his own paid speaking career. Ferrazzi also frames speaking as a way to stay relevant well into retirement.

## Key points

- **Fear yields to practice.** Diamandis describes being terrified of public speaking at MIT. Ferrazzi says he choked on live television on Good Morning America, and a former business partner then ambushed him with a flip camera until he got used to it. Both say modelling great speakers (Tony Robbins, Ferrazzi's Harvard professor Len Schlesinger) helped.
- **Structure and delivery.** Ferrazzi opens with a personal story, builds talks around three sections and rewrites his bullet points on recipe cards until he needs no notes. Diamandis uses image-heavy slides as prompts, avoids podiums and varies his energy ("go to 11 and go to two") with pauses.
- **Research the audience.** Ferrazzi asks clients what the audience should do differently months later, then talks to attendees at the bar and at breakfast. He says about 25% of a talk takes shape that way.
- **The business.** Both advise against going exclusive with one speakers bureau, citing commissions of 25-30%. They say you build relationships with individual agents rather than bureaus, and both describe sending thanks and gifts. Ferrazzi's own fee tiers run roughly $15k, $30k, $50-60k, $75k and $100k, and he says price should be used to cap your days.
- **A book as credential.** The two agree that a book (or an established brand) is close to a prerequisite for paid keynotes. They also recommend a speaker reel, flat travel buyouts and selective free talks (TED/TEDx, alumni reunions, audiences that buy speakers).
