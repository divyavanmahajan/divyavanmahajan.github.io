## Thesis

Bill Gross, founder of the Idealab incubator, walks Peter Diamandis through the lessons from roughly 5,000 ideas and 175 companies. His argument is that the idea is about 1% of the work. What decides success is timing, how fast a team iterates, how much it cares about the problem, and whether it can get through the "swamp of despair" in the middle. Since anything worthwhile will be hard, he says founders may as well pick something bold.

## Key points

- **The secret moon mission.** In 1999 Idealab funded Blastoff, a private lunar lander company that Diamandis ran. James Cameron was involved as director of photography. Gross put in the first $12 million. The dot-com crash killed it, and Diamandis says the plan later became the $30 million Google Lunar XPRIZE.
- **How Idealab filters ideas.** Gross puts each idea through gates: novel IP, whether the passion lasts, a leader who will "sell their house in a day", and outside investors willing to join. Founding teams end up with about 50% of the equity, and shared back-office staff handle admin.
- **Timing beats almost everything.** Gross says Uber worked because GPS phones and a recession arrived together. Idealab's online entertainment company, which had content deals with Chris Rock and Adam Sandler, shut down in 2003, just before Flash, mass broadband and YouTube. He advises spending slowly and finding interim revenue to survive until the market arrives.
- **Iterate and be remarkable.** Startups win on "learning per time" and a willingness to fail that reputation-conscious big companies lack. He quotes the line "marketing is a tax on being unremarkable" and gives Gmail's free gigabyte as an example.
- **Team types.** Gross says entrepreneur, producer, administrator and integrator skills must work in harmony, and that such a culture can beat better-funded rivals.
- **Climate bets.** Almost all of his work is now in climate, with about 20 companies. They include gravity storage that lifts 40-ton compressed-dirt blocks up hillsides, thermochemical hydrogen, and self-powered buildings with Skidmore, Owings & Merrill. He aims for about 3 cents per kWh all-in, including storage.
