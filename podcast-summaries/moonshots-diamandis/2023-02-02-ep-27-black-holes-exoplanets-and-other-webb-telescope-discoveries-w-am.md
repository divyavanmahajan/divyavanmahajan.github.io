## Thesis

Amber Straughn, Deputy Project Scientist for the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) at NASA Goddard, describes the telescope as a 25-year moonshot that needed technology which did not exist when the project began. She argues that its first six months have already challenged models of early galaxy formation and opened up spectroscopy of exoplanet atmospheres. Peter Diamandis presents it as a model for how moonshots work.

## Key points

- **Building the impossible.** Straughn says engineers had to invent 10 new technologies, and that Congress at one point cancelled the mission. Diamandis notes it was conceived in 1996, launched in 2021 and cost about $10 billion, compared with about $2 billion for Hubble.
- **Origami deployment.** Straughn says the telescope stands almost four stories tall and has a sunshield the size of a tennis court. Unfolding it took two weeks, with more than 300 single points of failure. An efficient Ariane 5 launch left propellant for over 20 years.
- **Why infrared and L2.** Straughn says the telescope sits a million miles away, at the second Lagrange point, so it stays cold enough to detect faint infrared light. She rates it about 100 times more powerful than Hubble.
- **Early galaxies.** In the deep fields, early galaxies are more numerous, brighter and more evolved than theory predicted. Straughn rejects claims that this disproves the Big Bang. She suggests dark matter halos may have played a bigger role in the early universe.
- **Exoplanets and life.** JWST studies starlight filtering through planetary atmospheres and has made the first definitive detection of carbon dioxide on an exoplanet. Straughn thinks life is ubiquitous, but expects that confirming it will probably need the next large telescope.
- **Open data.** Observing time is allocated through dual-anonymous annual proposals, and the data becomes public after any proprietary period. Diamandis expects AI tools to boost citizen astronomy.
