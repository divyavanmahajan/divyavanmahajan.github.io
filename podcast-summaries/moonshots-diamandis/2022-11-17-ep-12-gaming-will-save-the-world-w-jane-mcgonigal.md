## Thesis

Jane McGonigal, game designer and director of games research at the Institute for the Future, argues that the habits games teach (trying again after failure, collaborating, chasing ambitious goals) carry over into real life. Her future-simulation games ask people to imagine crises and how they would help others, and she says players come out more hopeful and with a sense of purpose, not more anxious. Peter Diamandis ties this to his own abundance and moonshot mindsets.

## Key points

- **Imagining how to help.** McGonigal's "pack your bags for the future" exercise lists your skills, knowledge and communities before a scenario is presented. In a 2008 pandemic simulation set in 2019 she imagined game releases keeping people at home, which she says the industry actually did in 2020.
- **Urgent optimism.** McGonigal says players of games about disasters, run for six to ten weeks with up to about 20,000 people, end up clear-eyed about risks but full of ideas for solutions. She calls this balance "urgent optimism".
- **Curiosity practices.** Her "flipping the world upside down" habit means listing facts you think will stay true, reversing them, and then searching for research that points the other way. At home her family keeps a "question wall".
- **SuperBetter.** After a severe concussion in 2009, McGonigal built a free app that treats a health challenge as a quest with power-ups, allies and "bad guys". She says randomized studies found benefits for depression, anxiety, chronic pain and concussion.
- **Designing for abundance.** She points to Fortnite, where you can rejoin straight away, and Pokemon Go's shared rewards as examples of designed abundance. She contrasts these with Web3's artificial scarcity. Diamandis compares school grading, where marks only go down, with game scores that only go up.
- **Moonshots.** McGonigal's 2008 goal of a game designer winning the Nobel Peace Prize was meant as a provocation, not a prediction. Her own moonshot is an annual "Super Bowl Sunday" game in which millions simulate a crisis. Her X Prize pick is long COVID and post-viral illness.
