## Thesis

Dave Asprey, founder of Bulletproof and CEO of Upgrade Labs, Danger Coffee and 40 Years of Zen, argues that biohacking means measuring what works for one individual rather than following epidemiology. In his view, precise signals delivered by technology beat brute-force effort. Peter Diamandis pushes back on some of Asprey's claims and pitches his own vision: an AI system that personalises health recommendations from a person's full data.

## Key points

- **Smarter, not harder.** Asprey says he trains for only 20 minutes a week. He argues the body responds to how fast stress comes on and how fast you recover, not to total volume. He says Upgrade Labs, a franchise he puts at 30 US locations, uses AI-driven machines and claims a 12% VO2 max improvement from 15 minutes a week.
- **Personalisation over averages.** Asprey criticises the Blue Zones diet advice from Dan Buettner. He says epidemiology can offer clues, but people should rely on mechanistic studies and their own measured outcomes.
- **AI-optimised health stacks.** Diamandis describes his moonshot at Fountain Life: an AI that takes genomics, metabolomics and biomarkers plus a person's goals and pill budget, then outputs the optimal regimen whichever physician you see. He expects this within two to five years. Asprey says Upgrade Labs already uses wearable data to make similar recommendations.
- **Regulation and risk.** Both criticise FDA limits on unapproved treatments, and Asprey describes a compassionate exemption case from his earlier lab-testing company. Diamandis proposes an "accredited patient" model, similar to accredited investors. They also discuss the FDA turning down MDMA for PTSD.
- **Neurofeedback as a business.** Asprey says 40 Years of Zen, a five-day program in Seattle, has trained 1,500 executives. It combines QEEG scans, neurofeedback and optional psycholytic ketamine under medical supervision. He treats P300 response latency as a marker of brain ageing.
- **Bias and AI.** Diamandis hopes for a Jarvis-style AI that gives a "bias alert" when he weights data poorly. Asprey argues the brain's "more is better" shortcut is its most harmful bias.
