## Thesis

Brett Adcock, founder of Figure (and earlier Archer Aviation and Vettery), argues that because the physical world is built for humans, a general-purpose humanoid can be slotted into the economy without changing the environment. He expects the first deployments in structured, labour-short logistics and manufacturing jobs, with homes, elder care and space to follow. Peter Diamandis, whose fund Bold Capital is an investor in Figure, presses him on timelines, costs and founder lessons.

## Key points

- **Market size.** Adcock says half of global GDP is labour and that there could eventually be up to 10 billion humanoids. Diamandis cites a Goldman Sachs estimate of $154 billion in robot revenue within 15 years.
- **Figure 01 specs.** The robot is 5 ft 6 in, about 61 kg, with roughly a thousand parts. It targets five hours per charge, around 20 kg of lift and vision-only perception, with no lidar. Adcock suggests a cost of about $30K, or a lease of around $500 a month.
- **Easy problems first.** Adcock compares humanoids to self-driving cars: moving known bins in a mapped warehouse is "highway driving", while cooking or elder care is "city driving". He cites a client with 140% annual warehouse turnover, and expects paid commercial work as early as 2024 and homes around the end of the decade.
- **AI approach.** Figure plans to train vision-language models on robot sensor data rather than build its own LLMs. Adcock argues that fleet data means a skill learned by one robot is learned by all.
- **Hardware is hard.** Adcock says he found no adequate off-the-shelf actuators, batteries, control software or middleware, so Figure builds most of them in-house.
- **Founder lessons.** He self-funded the early stages of both Archer and Figure, wrote a 10-year master plan and a culture document, and puts shipping product above PR.
