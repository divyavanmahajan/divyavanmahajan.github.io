## Thesis

In this stage conversation, recorded at the 2025 Abundance Summit, UC Berkeley sleep scientist Matt Walker and Peter Diamandis treat sleep as the base layer for every other longevity effort. Walker's argument is that consumer wearables measure sleep well but leave users unsure what the numbers mean. His new company, Nightfall IQ, applies AI analysis to that data to explain which habits predict a person's best and worst nights. The session is general discussion, not medical advice.

## Key points

- **Generations of sleep drugs.** Walker describes sleep medication in "web" generations. In his view the older sedative classes give sedation rather than natural sleep. He says a newer class, DORA drugs, works differently, and he cites a study on overnight clearance of Alzheimer's-linked proteins. He notes they cost up to about $350 a month without insurance.
- **Habits over hacks.** Walker's top habit is regularity: the same bed and wake times every day. He adds evening darkness (dimming half the lights an hour before bed), care with caffeine and alcohol timing, and a wind-down routine.
- **Phones as attention devices.** Walker says the research on blue light is now mixed. He argues phones mainly hurt sleep because they are built to capture attention, and suggests using them in the bedroom only while standing.
- **Nightfall IQ.** Walker says the service is device-agnostic and compares a user's data with age- and sex-matched norms drawn from millions of hours of sleep data at his centre. It then clusters nights with AI to find personal predictors and next-day effects. Pricing he quotes: about $4,000 for a one-off report and about $15,000 for a year of quarterly reviews.
- **Diamandis as case study.** Diamandis shared his Oura ring data. Walker reports deep sleep scores well above average for his age, a REM shortfall during the LA fires and a roughly 27% rise in heart rate variability. He credits Diamandis with very regular bedtimes.
