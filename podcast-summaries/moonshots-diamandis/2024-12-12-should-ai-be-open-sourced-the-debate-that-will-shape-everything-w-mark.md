## Thesis

Mark Surman, president of the Mozilla Foundation, argues that open source works best as a complement to commercial innovation, not a replacement for it. In his view the foundational layers of AI will become commoditized, and they should end up as a shared "LEGO kit". Peter Diamandis plays the sceptic throughout, putting the case for for-profit meritocracies and the speed of entrepreneurs.

## Key points

- **What open source means.** Surman defines it by four freedoms: use, study, modify and share. He cites a Harvard study that puts the value open source created over 20 years at $8 trillion. Motivations range from scratching a personal itch, through companies pooling infrastructure costs (Linux), to business models such as Red Hat's.
- **Is Llama open source?** Surman reads Meta's Llama strategy as a "Linux play" to commoditize infrastructure, noting reports that Nvidia is putting 200 engineers into it. He says the licence cap at 700 million users means Llama is not truly open source.
- **Mozilla's history.** He tells how Firefox grew out of the Netscape code to break Internet Explorer's dominance. His lesson is that a mission only matters if the technology also stays excellent, and Mozilla did not always manage that.
- **Commercial dominance vs innovation.** Surman says the market alone will not deliver broad, transparent safety work or access for smaller players. Diamandis counters with SpaceX's disruption of incumbent defence contractors and asks whether open source can match that speed.
- **Public AI.** Surman's paper rests on public use, public orientation and public goods. He points to the Allen Institute for AI as a possible "Linux Foundation" of the era and argues for shared infrastructure across democracies rather than purely national stacks.
- **Policy asks.** His three asks: antitrust that keeps room for entrepreneurs, use-based AI and privacy guardrails (he credits Europe with partly getting this right), and public innovation money spread beyond California and Washington state. On privacy, he cites Flower AI, backed by Mozilla Ventures, as an open alternative to on-device Apple Intelligence.
