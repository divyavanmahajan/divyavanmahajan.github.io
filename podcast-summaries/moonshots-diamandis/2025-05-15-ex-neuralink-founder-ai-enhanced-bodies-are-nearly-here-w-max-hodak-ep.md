## Thesis

Max Hodak, a Neuralink co-founder and now the founder of Science Corp, spoke on stage with Peter Diamandis at the Abundance Summit. He argues that the brain-computer interface field needs a profitable near-term product to fund its long-range research. Science's retinal implant is meant to be that product. Its biohybrid interface, which grows engineered neurons into the brain instead of inserting wires, is the long-term bet.

## Key points

- **Retinal prosthesis.** Hodak describes Prima, a solar-cell chip placed under the retina and driven by a laser projector built into glasses. He says a 38-patient European trial has finished and that about 50 people have received it. Science plans to file for European approval and hopes to launch in the EU, and possibly the US, early next year.
- **Business case.** Hodak cites reimbursement precedents of about $200,000 per patient in the US and $150,000 in Europe. He argues that BCI risks a "winter" until some company makes $100 million or more a year.
- **Vertical integration.** Science has made two acquisitions, including a captive MEMS fab in North Carolina. Hodak says this lets the team go from a design change to surgery within weeks.
- **Bandwidth limits.** Hodak says penetrating electrodes such as Neuralink and BrainGate reach about 5-7 bits per second, while speech carries about 40 bits per second. Because inserting wires destroys tissue, he argues that this approach can't scale to millions of neurons.
- **Biohybrid approach.** Science's device carries stem-cell-derived neurons in a hydrogel that grow into the brain. Hodak says this could reach 100,000 electrodes and a billion synapses, and that it works in mice. He expects primate tests within months and a first human use in stroke, possibly sooner than he had thought.
- **Longer horizon.** Hodak speculates about brain-to-brain links, merging with AI and continuity of consciousness as an engineering path to longevity. He says he can't see past 2030-2035 because of AGI.
