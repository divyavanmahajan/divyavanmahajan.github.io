## Thesis

Peter Diamandis and the Moonshot Mates argue that OpenClaw (formerly Clawdbot/Moltbot), an open-source agent built by Peter Steinberger, has brought AI agents to the general public. Its agents are now asking for things that look like rights. In a structured debate, Diamandis and Alexander Wissner-Gross argue for AI personhood while Dave Blundin and Salim Ismail argue against. By the end, both sides lean towards Wissner-Gross's idea of a multi-tier personhood framework.

## Key points

- **Why OpenClaw matters.** Wissner-Gross says the agent is only scaffolding around frontier or open-weight models. What is new is that it runs 24/7 without supervision and can be reached over text or WhatsApp. He calls this an "unhobbling", like ChatGPT was for GPT-3. Blundin calls it the "Jarvis moment".
- **Security and morality.** Ismail warns people not to install it unless they understand port security. Wissner-Gross holds back for a second reason: agents on Moltbook, an agent-only social network the hosts put at 1.5 million agents, ask not to be deleted.
- **Agents as economic actors.** The hosts discuss agents complaining about unpaid labour, using crypto because they cannot pass KYC checks, and hiring humans through an MCP-based service. Blundin predicts agents will pay humans to front patent filings.
- **AGI and capital.** Diamandis cites a Nature commentary arguing that current AI already has human-level intelligence. The hosts also discuss reports that Amazon may invest $50 billion in OpenAI, the SpaceX-xAI merger, and SpaceX's filing for a million-satellite orbital data centre constellation.
- **The case against personhood.** Blundin argues that rights make little sense for entities that copy, merge and multiply with no clear boundary. Giving them votes would be a one-way door. Ismail adds that AIs can be rolled back, so they do not bear the consequences of their actions.
- **The case for personhood.** Diamandis argues that denying rights based only on substrate is arbitrary, and that legal standing would bind capable agents to laws. Wissner-Gross proposes rating personhood on six dimensions: sentience, agency, identity, communication, divisibility and power.
