## Thesis

In a fireside chat recorded at Peter Diamandis's Abundance360 summit, Harvard geneticist David Sinclair presents his "information theory of aging": aging is less wear and tear than cells losing the ability to read their genes correctly. He claims a recoverable backup of that information exists and can be switched back on, and argues that AI-driven research plus new investment will move age reversal from a scientific backwater to a major pharmaceutical market. These are Sinclair's claims and forecasts, much of it based on animal work.

## Key points

- **The CD analogy.** Sinclair compares DNA to the music on a compact disc and the epigenome to the reader; aging is like scratches, and his January Cell paper, he says, shows the scratches can be "polished off" in mice.
- **From lab to company.** Three gene regulators (OSK) are being developed by Boston-based Life Biosciences. Sinclair reports promising non-human primate results and predicts a first human age-reversal trial, aimed at blindness, within 18 months to two years.
- **Engineered on/off switch.** The therapy is delivered by an AAV virus co-developed with George Church and stays inactive until triggered by doxycycline, which Sinclair says nobody else has in the clinic. He also describes unpublished mouse work on reversing brain aging, with hearing and dementia as later targets.
- **From gene therapy to pill.** Gene therapy will be expensive, so with more funding Sinclair would screen millions of molecules for a pill that mimics the effect, a market he sees spanning cosmetics and big pharma.
- **AI and capital as accelerants.** Sinclair says his lab already uses AI to work through a literature volume (he cites some 7,000 papers a day) no human can read, and to look for molecules that mimic OSK. He points to rising investment, mentioning Jeff Bezos.
- **Access and economics.** Diamandis cites a study claiming a one-year gain in global healthy age adds $38 trillion to the economy; Sinclair says broad affordability is his goal.
