## Thesis

In a Thanksgiving episode, Peter Diamandis asks Salim Ismail, Dave Blundin and Alexander Wissner-Gross six questions about setbacks, mentors and gratitude. Their shared argument is that the moments that felt like the end (a near-expulsion, a collapsing company, a rejection letter) built the grit and range behind their later work, and that luck deserves open acknowledgement.

## Key points

- **Two near-expulsions.** Diamandis tells how he took a fetal pig from a Hamilton College lab after missing weeks with chickenpox, then confessed and was forgiven by Professor Frank Price. At Harvard Medical School, Dean Tosteson let him graduate while running International Space University and a launch company, on condition he never practise medicine.
- **A board that nearly fired him.** Blundin recalls his first company, Data Sage, stalling when an Amazon deal went quiet; his board questioned him as CEO. A Walmart-Amazon lawsuit turned out to be the cause, and once it settled the company reached a billion-dollar liquidity event.
- **Collapse as a credential.** Ismail says the implosion of PubSub led to CEOs seeking his advice on difficult boards, then to Yahoo's incubator, and through a chance coffee with Chris Messina to Singularity University.
- **Rejection as a detour.** Wissner-Gross was turned down by an MIT summer programme for AI research around 1998, switched to nanotechnology, and won the Intel Science Talent Search. He credits that detour with his physical-world range.
- **Adversity data.** Blundin cites figures that foreign-born people are heavily over-represented among tech CEOs by market cap; Ismail adds a Google study that he says tied employee success to overcoming early adversity.
- **XPRIZE funding.** Diamandis says the $10 million prize was rejected around 150 times over four and a half years before Anousheh Ansari backed it.
