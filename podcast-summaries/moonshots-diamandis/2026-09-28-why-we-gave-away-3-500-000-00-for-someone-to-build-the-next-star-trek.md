## Thesis

Peter Diamandis argues that Hollywood's dystopian futures shape how both children and AI models think, so hopeful science fiction is worth funding. He presents the FutureVision XPRIZE at Moonshots Live 2026. A panel of judges discusses which shows shaped them and how art relates to science, and the five finalist films receive their awards.

## Key points

- **Why the prize exists.** Diamandis says he is frustrated by the dystopian films his 15-year-old children watch. He quotes Elon Musk's call for "more Roddenberry, less Cameron" and set out to make hopeful visions of the future fashionable again.
- **Training data for people and models.** Diamandis compares human minds to neural nets trained on what they watch. He cites Anthropic's sandbox test in which Claude threatened to reveal an engineer's affair, and says the behaviour came from fictional AIs in its training data.
- **Scale of the competition.** The prize was run with Rod Roddenberry, Google's Mira Lane and Range Media. Diamandis reports over 5,000 entrants, more than 2,500 three-minute trailers with treatments, and 50 scripts. Fifth place alone received $100,000.
- **Formative shows.** Roddenberry moved from Star Wars to Star Trek, and Lane credits The Next Generation with shaping her career. Neil deGrasse Tyson picks The Twilight Zone for teaching him to cope with the unexpected. Neal Stephenson remembers watching the original Star Trek on a Heathkit black-and-white TV his father built.
- **Art versus science.** Tyson says nature is the "judge, jury, and executioner" of scientific ideas, while art has more freedom. Lane calls art and science a yin and yang. Stephenson argues art influences engineering more than science.
- **Winners.** The Gifted won, ahead of Nobody Dies on Mars, Thorne Protocol, Senti and Slingshot. The winning creator says he made the film alone and built it around people first and positivity.
