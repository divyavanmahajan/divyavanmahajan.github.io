## Thesis

Harvard geneticist David Sinclair tells Peter Diamandis that aging should be treated as the root cause of most chronic disease rather than an inevitability. He frames it as "99% a software problem" that his lab can already partly reset in animals, and both argue that, like earlier technologies, longevity treatments will start expensive and then get cheap enough to deliver very large economic gains.

## Key points

- **Aging as information loss.** Sinclair says there is no biological limit to human lifespan and describes his lab's 2020 finding of a "backup copy" of the body's original epigenetic software that can be reset; in mice they restored vision in a week and, in unpublished work, reset the eye more than once.
- **Cost curves and democratization.** Diamandis compares longevity tech to early mobile phones; he describes a PCSK9 cholesterol vaccine, moving from primate studies to humans, that he says could cost about $50 a year versus far pricier monoclonal antibodies. Sinclair says biological-age tests could drop by two to three orders of magnitude via Tally Health, a company tied to his student.
- **The economic case.** Sinclair cites work with Andrew Scott estimating one extra healthy year is worth $86 trillion to the US alone, and 10 years $365 trillion, and calls the claim that slowing aging bankrupts society false.
- **Timelines.** Asked when accessible age-reversal treatments will exist, Sinclair answers 10 years; Diamandis notes Ray Kurzweil gave a similar figure and George Church about 15.
- **Research momentum.** Sinclair says more than $10 billion has recently been committed to aging research and drug development, that his lab uses AI to triage papers, and that 18-30 year olds have become the field's most passionate supporters.
- **Measurement and ethics.** Sinclair calls the annual physical "medieval" and argues for a continuous body dashboard feeding AI; on ethics, he argues people should have the right to choose how long they live.
