## Thesis

Regenerative medicine researcher Doris Taylor, speaking on stage at Peter Diamandis's Abundance360 summit, argues that the bottleneck for building personalised replacement hearts is no longer the science but manufacturing. By automating her process with Dean Kamen's ARMI BioFab institute, she says she can get bioengineered hearts into patients within five years. Diamandis frames the conversation around operationalising ideas at scale.

## Key points

- **The problem with transplants today.** Taylor cites about 10 US heart transplants a day against a waiting list of 2,700, and anti-rejection drugs costing up to $32,000 a month. She says personalised hearts would remove the need for those drugs and cut lifetime transplant cost by 49%.
- **Scaffold, not 3D printing.** Her method washes cells out of a donor heart to leave a "ghost heart" scaffold that keeps the natural blood-vessel network, which she says 3D printing cannot yet reproduce. The scaffold is then reseeded with cells grown from the patient.
- **Automation as the unlock.** Taylor says manual work took 14 years to double the size of a heart she could build, a pace that would need 42 years to reach a child-sized heart. Automating cell production and robotic injection at ARMI in Manchester, New Hampshire, cut that to under a year, she says.
- **Infrastructure for a stalled field.** Taylor describes ARMI as a Department of Defense-funded initiative created because tissue engineering has produced few marketed products, partly due to contamination and the lack of closed, automated facilities. She plans a "heart hotel" of 40 hearts and works with robotics firm Advanced Solutions.
- **Funding model.** Taylor says the company has run mostly on non-dilutive grants, her own money and family donors, and estimates it needs $300 million to reach approval. She is seeking partners for what she calls a long-horizon return.
- **Stem cells and ageing.** Taylor also shares her view that ageing and chronic disease reflect declining stem-cell-based repair, which she links to inflammation and stress.
