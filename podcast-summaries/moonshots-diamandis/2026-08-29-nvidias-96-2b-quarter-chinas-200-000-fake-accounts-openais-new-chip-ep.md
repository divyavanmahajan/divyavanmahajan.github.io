## Thesis

Peter Diamandis and the Moonshot Mates read NVIDIA's $96.2 billion quarter as proof of an AI economy that increasingly trades with itself. Dave Blundin argues every economy is circular, so this is not a warning sign. Alexander Wissner-Gross is less sure: he wants to know how much demand NVIDIA finances itself. The hosts also argue that inference is moving to custom silicon, and that the data so far does not show AI wiping out jobs.

## Key points

- **NVIDIA and circularity.** Diamandis cites revenue up 106% year on year and guidance of $108 billion. Blundin calls TSMC NVIDIA's biggest vulnerability. Wissner-Gross separates trade between companies from balance-sheet financing of a company's own customers, and says the second looks "bubbly". At worst he expects a "mini winter".
- **China's world models.** Diamandis cites a Runway co-founder's claim that video makes up about 70% of AI token use in China. Wissner-Gross's explanation is that American labs maximise revenue per token and Chinese labs do not.
- **Custom inference chips.** OpenAI's Broadcom-built Jalapeno chip claims 1.5-1.9x more work per watt than NVIDIA's GB200/GB300. Blundin says inference is moving off NVIDIA, while training and the Mellanox interconnect remain its moat. Wissner-Gross wonders whether OpenAI could launch its own compute cloud.
- **Influence ops and data centres.** The hosts discuss X's report of a bot farm of about 200,000 suspected Chinese accounts, some posting that data centres raise electricity prices. They contrast this with CNN's report that poverty in Quincy, Washington fell from 29.4% to 6.2%.
- **Jobs data.** Salim Ismail cites a Principal Financial Group survey of small and mid-sized firms: 4% expect AI to reduce staffing, 31% expect increases. Wissner-Gross argues that agency is now the trait that gets rewarded.
- **Surveillance.** On Flock Safety's 20 billion monthly vehicle scans, Ismail warns that cheap pattern matching erodes civil liberties. Diamandis defends trading some privacy for safety. Wissner-Gross mentions David Brin's idea of giving everyone access.
