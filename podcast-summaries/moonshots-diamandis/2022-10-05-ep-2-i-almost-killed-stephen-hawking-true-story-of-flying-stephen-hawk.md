## Thesis

Interviewed by his producer Nick, Peter Diamandis tells the story of founding Zero G, his parabolic-flight company, and of flying Stephen Hawking into weightlessness. He presents it as a lesson in persistence: a massive transformative purpose, opening the space frontier, kept him going through more than a decade of regulatory refusals. He also makes the case for why space exploration matters.

## Key points

- **From rejection to a company.** Diamandis says he was refused a seat on NASA's zero-g aircraft and started Zero G in 1993 instead, reasoning that other people would want to fly too.
- **An 11-year FAA battle.** He describes the FAA's position as all downside and no upside for approving something new. The agency wanted passengers to wear parachutes during "aerobatic" flight. He says approval came in 2004, after he told Administrator Marion Blakey he was taking US entrepreneurs to Moscow to fly instead.
- **Nearly bankrupt on day one.** Zero G's first paid job, filming for Richard Branson's show Rebel Billionaire, almost collapsed when the 727 needed a part flown in overnight. Diamandis admits the first flight may have broken the daylight-only rule.
- **Flying Hawking.** The FAA said its rules allowed only "able-bodied" passengers. Diamandis got doctors to certify Hawking, covered their malpractice insurance, and ran test flights with a high-school stand-in. The plan was one parabola; they flew eight.
- **Why space.** Diamandis gives two reasons: existential risks such as pandemics and asteroids, which mean humanity should become multiplanetary, and inspiring people. He says space spending is tiny, with US government spending at about a quarter of a percent today compared with 2% at the height of Apollo.
- **Zero G today.** According to Diamandis, the company flies 70 to 80 flights a year at around $7,500 to $8,000, with 15 parabolas including lunar-gravity ones.
