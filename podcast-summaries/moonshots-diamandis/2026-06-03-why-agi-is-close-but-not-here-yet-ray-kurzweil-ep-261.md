## Thesis

At a live session at MIT with the Moonshot Mates and Steven Kotler, Ray Kurzweil defends the timeline he set in 1999: AGI by 2029 and the singularity by 2045. He says two gaps remain, AI's understanding of physics and affordable household robotics. He argues that most people, and most universities, are not planning for how fast things are about to change.

## Key points

- **Two missing pieces.** Asked about Demis Hassabis's 50/50 view on needing another breakthrough, Kurzweil says AI still does not really understand physics, and he expects a Google project on that to take until 2029. He adds that robotics trails language models: people "can't spend $100,000" on a robot to clean up after dinner.
- **The compute curve.** Kurzweil says hardware price-performance has improved 75 quadrillion-fold since relay computers in 1939, and that software adds roughly another millionfold. On his account, that is why language models only recently became effective.
- **Prediction record.** Kurzweil recalls a Stanford conference after The Singularity Is Near, where AI experts agreed human-level AI would come but put it 100 years out. He scores his own predictions strictly: a forecast that is off by a year or more counts as wrong.
- **Personhood and governance.** Kurzweil calls consciousness a non-scientific question. Wissner-Gross argues for recognising several forms of personhood and for economic rights for AIs, such as opening bank accounts. Ismail sees AI-driven policy and Dubai's agentic-government plans as a large opportunity.
- **Education.** Kurzweil says AI already teaches subjects better than universities do, so their remaining role is socialisation. Ismail argues education should flip from supply-side skills to the problems students want to solve. Blundin wants MIT to open up its curriculum much more.
- **Background and advice.** Diamandis tells how Larry Page offered to buy Kurzweil's startup Patterns Inc. during a pitch for a $10 million investment. Kurzweil says he plans a "selfbot" of himself for his upcoming book, and his advice to founders is agility first, because plans that once held for years may now last weeks.
