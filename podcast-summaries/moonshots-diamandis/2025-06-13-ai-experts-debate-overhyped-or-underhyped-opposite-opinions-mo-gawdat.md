## Thesis

Peter Diamandis sets up a debate on the decade from 2025 to 2035, citing Ray Kurzweil's prediction of a century of progress in ten years. Steven Kotler argues that AI is overhyped compared with what he sees in practice. Mo Gawdat replies that today's AI is underhyped, because what matters is where it is heading. Gawdat sees the main short-term danger in bad actors using AI, not in the AI itself. By the end, all three agree that human cooperation at scale is the deciding factor.

## Key points

- **The case for hype.** Kotler says AI-polished drafts fall apart in his editing sessions. He compares the hype to Bitcoin and the metaverse, and notes that many people promoting AI make their living from it.
- **Where the ball is going.** Gawdat points to synthetic data, agents prompting agents, AlphaEvolve-style self-improvement and DeepSeek-style efficiency as signs of faster progress. He expects 5 to 10 years of "augmented intelligence" before an era of "machine mastery".
- **Risk tolerance.** Gawdat compares AI risk to insurance and Russian roulette. He says misuse by humans is near-certain: autonomous weapons, AI posing as friends, and unemployment of 10-40% in some sectors. He predicts a "drastic event" within two to three years.
- **Intelligence and benevolence.** Gawdat defines intelligence as creating order with the least waste. He hopes a smarter AI will eventually refuse destructive orders. He puts that point 12 to 15 years out.
- **Cooperation and flow.** Kotler argues that AI, climate change and other threats all need the same fix: cooperation at scale. He calls for a "Manhattan style project" for it and an XPRIZE for human cooperation. He also argues that flow keeps people doing meaningful work even when AI can do it.
- **What to do now.** Gawdat wants governments to regulate how AI is used, for example by criminalising undeclared deepfakes, rather than the technology itself. He tells investors not to back any AI they would not want aimed at their own children. Diamandis hopes for a benevolent superintelligence. Kotler pushes back on the idea of a "code god".
