## Thesis

On a stage in Saudi Arabia, Peter Diamandis asks a16z's Anjney Midha, Hong Kong Exchanges CEO Bonnie Chan and Link Ventures' Dave Blundin how the AI buildout gets paid for. The panel agrees that demand for capital is effectively uncapped and that traditional venture cannot cover it. Midha and Chan add that energy is the binding constraint, and that the wealth being created is concentrated in private hands.

## Key points

- **Venture is too small.** Diamandis cites about $1 billion a day going into AI in the US, which he expects to rise to $3 billion a day by 2030. Blundin compares that with roughly $200 billion a year of US venture and says strategic money such as Nvidia's fills the gap. Midha says all of a16z's funds are now effectively AI funds.
- **A stack of scarcity.** Midha describes cash converted into GPUs and GPUs converted into tokens. In his view, high-quality foundation-model tokens are now the scarcest input for application companies. He argues that energy, permitting and data-centre retooling, not chips, now set the pace.
- **Public markets.** Chan says Hong Kong tops the global IPO league table this year, with about 80 deals done and 300 in the pipeline, roughly half of them AI-related. She points to China's grid and storage work and to data-heavy sectors such as drug discovery.
- **Vertical startups.** Blundin says MIT and Harvard teams going after vertical use cases have so far had close to a 100% success rate in his portfolio. He cites Mercor's rise from a $30 million to a $10 billion valuation in two years.
- **Risks.** Midha warns of civil backlash, of IT-services-heavy economies such as India, and of a public left out of private AI gains. He wants sovereign and pension funds to invest. Blundin fears capital-intensive side bets such as fusion, robotics and quantum could cause a 2001-style loss of confidence. Chan worries that retail investors could be the last ones in at inflated valuations.
