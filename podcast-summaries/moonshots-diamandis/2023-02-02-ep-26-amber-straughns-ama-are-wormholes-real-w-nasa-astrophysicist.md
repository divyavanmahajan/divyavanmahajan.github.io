## Thesis

Amber Straughn, deputy project scientist for the James Webb Space Telescope, takes Twitter questions from Peter Diamandis's audience. Her consistent message is hopeful but grounded: wormholes are allowed by physics but not usable now, humans will probably return to the Moon by 2030, and the next generation of NASA observatories is aimed squarely at finding life beyond Earth. Diamandis frames several topics as possible XPRIZE challenges.

## Key points

- **Wormholes and the Moon.** Straughn says wormholes are theoretically possible but not something humans can use for travel today. She gives a 90% chance of boots on the Moon by 2030, citing the Artemis test and Starship, and agrees with Diamandis that they could be Chinese boots.
- **Propulsion and debris.** She argues interplanetary travel needs a revolution in propulsion, with nuclear and solar-sail options still early. On debris, she notes NASA already has to manoeuvre the International Space Station around it; Diamandis mentions a space-debris XPRIZE in development.
- **Planetary defence.** Diamandis points to a school-bus-sized asteroid spotted only days before passing inside geostationary orbit. Straughn calls the DART mission, which hit an asteroid to change its path, a successful first proof of concept.
- **Next telescopes.** The Nancy Grace Roman telescope, Hubble-sized but with a much wider field of view, is due in 2027 and will study exoplanets and dark energy. The 2020 decadal survey prioritises a Habitable Worlds Observatory to survey about 100 nearby star systems, followed by large X-ray and far-infrared telescopes, all designed to be serviceable.
- **Data and prizes.** Straughn names climate change as her grand challenge. She expects Roman and the ground-based Rubin telescope to produce more data than astronomers can yet analyse, which is driving machine learning work and could suit citizen-science competitions.
