## Thesis

Peter Diamandis and Strategic Coach founder Dan Sullivan describe entrepreneurship as a lifetime commitment, not a career choice. They argue that it rests on an obsession with a problem, a focus on your own "unique ability" with others covering the rest, and getting paid by real customers as early as possible.

## Key points

- **Definitions.** Sullivan traces the term to Jean-Baptiste Say in 1804: someone who moves resources from lower to higher productivity. He links the rise of entrepreneurs to James Watt's improved steam engine. Diamandis defines an entrepreneur as someone who "finds a juicy problem and solves it".
- **Cash flow first.** Sullivan says he went bankrupt twice in the late 1970s and early 1980s because of receivables. Since 1984 he has insisted on payment up front, and he says Abundance 360's first event was paid for before anything was presented. He and his wife Babs take 15% of gross off the top.
- **Funding choices.** Diamandis contrasts bootstrapped companies built on early sales, which he finds the most fulfilling, with moonshots that need outside capital and dilute founders to 10-20%. He says early investors bet on the person, because a great entrepreneur will rework a mediocre business.
- **Founder vs CEO.** Diamandis prefers the founder or executive chairman role with a hired CEO. Sullivan says Babs runs Strategic Coach "backstage" under a rule to "free up Dan" for front-stage creative work.
- **Education and networks.** Sullivan says few of the roughly 22,000 entrepreneurs he has coached credit formal education. Diamandis says his networks, such as the student space group SEDS, came from outside MIT and medical school, and cites Alexandr Wang leaving MIT to found Scale AI.
- **Rapid-fire advice.** Their advice: choose co-founders with complementary abilities, make your first hire an artist (Sullivan), pick a problem you are obsessed with, and earn the first dollar quickly through an MVP. Sullivan says only a check writer can tell you an idea is good.
