## Thesis

Recording at Figure's San Jose headquarters, Brett Adcock tells Peter Diamandis and Dave Blundin that Figure now runs its humanoids entirely on neural nets. In his view general-purpose robotics is limited by data, not hardware, and the companies that matter will be the few that can show long, uncut, closed-loop autonomy in places the robot has never seen.

## Key points

- **Helix 2 drops the last code.** Adcock says Helix 2, released the day of recording, removed the remaining 109,000 lines of C++ by adding a learned whole-body controller (S0). The result is room-scale autonomy such as kitchen work.
- **Demos are mostly not autonomy.** He argues that much humanoid video elsewhere is teleoperated or open-loop replay ("karate" routines), and says he knows of nothing else running continuous neural-net control for more than a minute. Figure's logistics policy, he says, once ran 67 hours before an error.
- **Vertical integration.** Adcock says off-the-shelf actuators, hands and robots could not do this work, so Figure designs and assembles in-house. He expects almost no China supply chain by summer, but sees China as the only real competitor, and predicts consolidation to "far less than 10" companies globally.
- **Manufacturing and deployment.** The BotQ facility has room for four lines of about 12,000 units each, so a little under 50,000 robots a year. Adcock plans to put robots on those lines this year, and to scale leased Figure 3 fleets with commercial customers, building on six months at BMW.
- **Specs and compute.** He gives four to five hours per charge from a roughly 2 kWh pack, about 2 kW inductive charging through the feet, a 20 kg payload, and a new cluster of 3,000 NVIDIA B200s used for pre-training.
- **Scale and the home.** Adcock imagines tens of billions of humanoids, with robots building robots within about 24 months. On Blundin's capital question, he notes a billion robots at $20,000 each means $20 trillion of working capital. Home deployments would follow from next year.
