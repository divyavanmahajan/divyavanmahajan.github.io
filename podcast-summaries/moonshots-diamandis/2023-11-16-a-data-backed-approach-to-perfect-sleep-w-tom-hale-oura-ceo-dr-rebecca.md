## Thesis

Peter Diamandis, Oura CEO Tom Hale and Brigham and Women's / Harvard Medical School researcher Rebecca Robbins frame sleep as a foundation of health and performance. Robbins argues that behaviour, not medication, gives the best long-term results. Hale argues that a wearable which learns each person's own baseline is what makes that behaviour change stick.

## Key points

- **The case for sleep.** Robbins says most adults do best on 7 to 9 hours, and cites data that only about 35% get enough. Hale cites figures linking sleep loss to more car crashes, workplace accidents and heart attacks.
- **Behaviour over pills.** Robbins says trials comparing sleep drugs with cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia show similar short-term results, but behaviour wins long term. She is cautious about the surge in largely unregulated melatonin use; Hale calls melatonin a "starting gun" for sleep, not a sedative.
- **Data as feedback loop.** Robbins encourages "n of one" experiments, such as dropping caffeine or alcohol for a few days and checking tracker data. Diamandis describes "gamifying" his sleep score and reports his first 97.
- **Routine and environment.** The guests describe circadian timing, a cool, dark, quiet bedroom and a consistent wind-down. Hale mentions using the Timeshifter app for jet lag, while noting it is not a perfect fix.
- **Inside the ring.** Hale lists the hardware: red and green LEDs, temperature and SpO2 sensors, accelerometers, 16 MB of memory and 5 to 7 days of battery in a ring of about four grams. He claims the finger gives a signal 50 to 100 times better than the wrist, and says Oura was about to launch a new sleep-staging algorithm.
- **Personal baselines.** Hale says Oura is less about gamification than about measuring deviations from each user's baseline, for example in heart rate variability. In his view there is no universally "good" HRV.
