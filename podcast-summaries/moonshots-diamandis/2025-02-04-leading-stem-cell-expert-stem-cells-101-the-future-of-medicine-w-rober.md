## Thesis

Robert Hariri, founder and CEO of Celularity, tells Peter Diamandis that stem cells act as the body's repair kit and that this supply declines with age. He argues that placental cells, normally discarded at birth, are an abundant, off-the-shelf source that avoids the old embryo controversy. His main business point is that the US regulatory model, built for drugs, is too slow and costly for cell therapy, and he calls for provisional approvals backed by large real-world data sets. Diamandis, who discloses he is Celularity's vice chairman, largely agrees.

## Key points

- **Access gap.** Diamandis notes that Americans travel to Panama, Costa Rica, Mexico or Bali for stem cell treatments that are not approved at home. Hariri describes the FDA as very conservative and says the US needs more courage to accept that the benefits outweigh the uncertainty about long-term safety.
- **Declining reserve.** Hariri presents age-related loss of stem cells, which he puts at roughly a thousandfold in bone marrow from infancy to age 80, as a hallmark of aging. He frames supplementation as the commercial opportunity.
- **Placenta as a source.** Hariri says one placenta can yield billions of cells, against a dozen from an embryo, and that placental cells behave as universal donor cells. Celularity's LifebankUSA unit banks newborn placental cells, and its facility in New Jersey also makes surgical tissue products for wound care and orthopaedics.
- **Clinical trial economics.** According to Hariri, no large companies do stem cell clinical development, and small biotechs cannot fund the multi-year trials regulators expect. He proposes provisional approval for products that meet GMP and safety standards, starting with conditions such as ALS.
- **Data and AI.** Hariri argues that real-world use across 10,000 patients, not 10 or 100, would give machine learning the data it needs. He expects Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to be receptive to new therapeutic models.
- **Diagnostics plus therapeutics.** The two describe Fountain Life, which they co-founded, as aiming to pair early detection with regenerative treatment. They also cover Hariri's early patent on repopulating decellularised organ scaffolds, an approach that Dean Kamen and Martine Rothblatt are now using.
