## Thesis

In this audience Q&A at the 2022 Abundance360 summit, hosted by Peter Diamandis, Astro Teller explains the management mechanics behind Alphabet's X. His recurring argument is that a moonshot factory has to protect intellectual honesty from emotion and money, so that teams can kill weak ideas quickly and cheaply through tight learning loops.

## Key points

- **Pre-mortems.** About two months before Wing's first public launch, Teller asked the team to imagine it had failed badly and write down why in two minutes. The team then spent two months fixing those issues ahead of time.
- **Killed projects come back.** Teller says X has killed roughly 2,000 projects and keeps a graveyard. Foghorn, which turned seawater into methanol, was shut down because fuel could not get below about $15 per gallon equivalent; a later green-hydrogen breakthrough may revive the idea.
- **No OKRs, no early equity.** Teller calls OKRs a poor way to drive moonshots, preferring a small set of non-negotiables plus audacious goals. X staff get no equity until a project graduates as an Other Bet, because stock in a "darling" makes it hard to kill.
- **Capital allocation.** X once had almost a third of its resources on one project, which Teller calls dangerous. Teams are now deliberately under-resourced to push them away from conventional approaches. He also says tight learning loops make much healthcare work a poor fit for X.
- **Inside large companies.** Teller advises putting a meaningful barrier between a moonshot group and the core business. He says X's first real satellite office, in Tel Aviv, worked because a committed leader opened it herself.
- **Google Glass.** Teller says Glass was presented as a product before it was one, but it continues in business-to-business settings such as doctors' offices.
