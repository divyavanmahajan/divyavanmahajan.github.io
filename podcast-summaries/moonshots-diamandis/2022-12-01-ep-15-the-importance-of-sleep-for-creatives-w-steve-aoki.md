## Thesis

DJ and producer Steve Aoki describes how decades of extreme touring left him with very little REM sleep, and how health data (brain imaging, neurofeedback and his Oura ring) finally pushed him to change. Peter Diamandis argues that measurement matters because the body hides problems until late, and that prioritising sleep is the single most important habit.

## Key points

- **Life on the road.** Aoki holds the Guinness record for the most travelled musician in a calendar year and says he once played 300 shows a year on two to four hours of sleep a night. He now says he is trying to slow down.
- **Brain imaging.** Aoki recounts a blood-flow brain scan with Dr Amen that showed a dent in his prefrontal area, which he links to old snowboarding and extreme-sports injuries, plus temporal-lobe findings that lined up with neurofeedback results on memory and tinnitus. He has lived with tinnitus since 2007.
- **Preventive, not reactive.** Diamandis argues that the body compensates for problems until they become serious, so regular imaging lets people track their health preventively, citing his own scans through Fountain Life.
- **The REM problem.** Matt Walker, author of "Why We Sleep", reviews Aoki's Oura data weekly and told him his REM levels were among the lowest he had seen. Aoki says he typically gets 20 to 30 minutes of REM even on a full night and is working with Walker on medication and routine changes; on one tour run he slept about six hours across three days and noticed himself forgetting lyrics on stage.
- **Sleep as priority.** Diamandis says he aims for eight hours, in bed by 9:30, and calls sleep a superpower; Aoki repeats Walker's framing of sleep as the real version of the "Limitless" drug.
- **Cold plunges.** Aoki, inspired by the Wim Hof method and Laird Hamilton, converted his home water feature into a group cold plunge and usually stays in four to five minutes. He lists cold, heat and fasting as his core longevity principles and adds sleep as the fourth.
