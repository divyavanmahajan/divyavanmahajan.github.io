## Thesis

Recorded at the White House, Peter Diamandis interviews Michael Kratsios, Director of the Office of Science and Technology Policy, about the Genesis Mission and the "Science in a New Golden Age" report. Kratsios argues that scientific productivity has declined despite bigger budgets because the way science is funded and run has not changed, and that AI, faster grant mechanisms and bold national missions can reverse this.

## Key points

- **Born free or born in captivity.** Kratsios sorts technologies into two buckets: those like the 1990s internet, where government should stay out, and those like commercial drones or AI medical diagnostics, which need active regulatory change before they can be deployed.
- **Genesis Mission and national missions.** The stated goal is to double US scientific productivity within a decade. Kratsios cites goals of a crewed Moon landing and a space nuclear reactor by 2028, a scientifically useful quantum computer this term, and about $5 billion in recent Genesis grants. Pressed by Diamandis, he says the target should probably be 10x.
- **Why science stalled.** Kratsios points to an NIH budget near $45 billion, a National Academies figure that about 45% of researcher time goes on grant administration, and a median age of 71 for intramural NIH scientists.
- **New funding mechanisms.** The report proposes five-year grants funded up front, short fast-track grants reviewed within a month, "golden tickets" that let one reviewer fund a risky idea, meta-science units at NIH and NSF, and incentive prizes, which Kratsios thinks could reach about $100 million.
- **AI stack and open source.** Kratsios says the US must lead in both closed and open models, admits Chinese open-weight models are currently the cheap default, and describes an American AI Exports Program offering turnkey stacks backed by export financing.
- **Autonomous labs and public data.** He backs self-driving labs and making 70 years of national-lab data AI-ready as a public good, comparing it to NOAA weather data.
