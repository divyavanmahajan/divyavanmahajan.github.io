## Thesis

Ege Erdil and Tamay Besiroglu, formerly of Epoch AI and now co-founders of Mechanize, argue that AI still lacks many capabilities that jobs require, so a drop-in remote worker is likely decades away. They reject a software-only intelligence explosion but expect AI that can substitute for human labour to drive growth of around 30% a year. Dwarkesh Patel pushes back on each step.

## Key points

- **Timelines.** Erdil puts a remote worker that can do literally everything at around 2045, and Besiroglu shades that about five years earlier. Besiroglu says past capability unlocks came roughly every three orders of magnitude of compute, and that perhaps three or four orders of scaling remain.
- **Moravec's paradox.** The guests argue that AI masters skills that are recent in evolutionary terms, like chess and maths, but not the agency and perception animals already have. Erdil notes that Claude still got stuck in Mount Moon for 48 hours while playing Pokemon.
- **No software-only singularity.** Besiroglu says software progress tracks hardware: about 30% a year for traditional software, in line with Moore's law, and innovations such as the transformer and flash attention came from GPU-rich labs. Dwarkesh cites Daniel Kokotajlo's survey suggesting researchers would still make a third of their usual progress with a thirtieth of the compute.
- **The growth arithmetic.** Besiroglu argues that an H100 does roughly as much computation as a human brain, costs about $30K and could earn $50-100K a year, which implies the economy doubling about once a year. Erdil sees regulation as the strongest objection and gives global coordination to stop it about a 10% chance.
- **Takeover and speed.** Erdil sees little evidence that AIs become misaligned, and Besiroglu argues that misaligned goals alone rarely cause conflict. Erdil says each year of delay costs perhaps 100 million lives, and that pausing at a fixed compute level would teach us little about alignment.
- **Humility over plans.** Erdil notes that Britain expected more bombing deaths in the first weeks of WWII than it suffered in six years, and argues flexibility matters more than detailed plans.
