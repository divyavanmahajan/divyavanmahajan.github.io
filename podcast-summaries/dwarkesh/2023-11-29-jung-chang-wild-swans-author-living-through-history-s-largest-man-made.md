## Thesis

Jung Chang, author of Wild Swans and Mao: The Unknown Story, argues that Mao kept control of China through information isolation, terror and organisation reaching down to the grassroots, not through ideological conviction. She holds that the Great Leap Forward famine, with nearly 40 million deaths, followed from Mao's decision to seize grain and export it in exchange for Soviet and Eastern European weapons and industry. Dwarkesh Patel pushes on why elites, Deng Xiaoping included, never broke with Mao.

## Key points

- **Personal history.** Chang was born in Sichuan in 1952. Her father, an official, was purged after writing to Mao to criticise his policies. Her mother endured over a hundred denunciation meetings. In 1978 Chang was one of the first 14 people sent to study in Britain, and in 1982 she earned a York doctorate in linguistics.
- **How the brainwashing worked.** Chang says it took her eight years from first disliking the society to blaming Mao, because isolation and fear made him beyond question.
- **Origin of the Cultural Revolution.** In her account, Mao launched it in 1966 mainly as revenge on Liu Shaoqi, who had used a party gathering to halt the food-export policy and end the famine from 1962.
- **Economic ignorance.** Chang describes backyard steel furnaces that stripped mountains of trees and produced mostly useless metal, and the campaign to kill sparrows that let pests flourish.
- **Mao's legacy today.** She calls Deng's refusal to denounce Mao a big mistake, probably driven by fear for party rule. She says that since Xi took power, banned books bring a total clampdown, and she rejects the claim that Nixon and Kissinger opened China, dating the opening to after Mao died in 1976.
- **Self-criticism as a weapon.** Chang argues that forced confessions, quotas of "rightists" and the charge of "warm feelingism" broke individuals and turned people against each other so that no opposition could organise.
