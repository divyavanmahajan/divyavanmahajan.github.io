## Thesis

Grant Sanderson of 3Blue1Brown argues that AI's progress in math is spiky even within the field: models already draw "lightning bolts" between known areas, but inventing new definitions and theories resists benchmarks and RL. Dwarkesh Patel adds that math advances fast because it is not just verifiable but "grindable", since rollouts can be replayed in parallel. Both expect human roles to shift toward curation, explanation and teaching.

## Key points

- **IMO gold was just another benchmark.** Sanderson notes AI brute-forced olympiad geometry in nineteen seconds as early as 2024 and still struggles with combinatorics. He cites OpenAI's disproof of the unit distance conjecture and Erdős problem 1196 as examples of AI linking two fields.
- **Century-long verification loops.** Using Lagrange, Abel and Galois, Sanderson shows group theory went from rejected prison manuscripts to Liouville and Jordan over decades, long before Gell-Mann used it to predict quarks. He suggests rewarding compressed, elegant concepts, perhaps via Kolmogorov complexity.
- **Grindability over Lean.** Patel argues computer use lags because websites can't be replayed a thousand times, and that Lean mattered little to recent results. Sanderson counters that Lean could let an AI extend Mathlib unattended for years, and spare mathematicians from checking papers where even 1 in 100 is wrong.
- **Escaping context.** Sanderson suggests parallel agents with deliberately different contexts, for example one proving and one disproving, while Patel proposes giving agents different research heuristics.
- **Writing and theory of mind.** They argue models explain well but lack the well-timed unpredictability and reader modelling of good writing, citing Andy Matuschak's failed attempts to train LLMs to write spaced-repetition prompts.
- **Advice for students.** Sanderson tells students to learn from good human-curated sources, to understand where their salary comes from, and to consider teaching, which he sees as durable.
