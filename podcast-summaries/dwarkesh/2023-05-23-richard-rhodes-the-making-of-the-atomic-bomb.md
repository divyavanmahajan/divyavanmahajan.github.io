## Thesis

Richard Rhodes, Pulitzer Prize-winning author of *The Making of the Atomic Bomb*, tells Dwarkesh Patel that the physics of fission was so obvious once published in 1938 that the bomb would have been built even without World War II, only more slowly. He argues the bomb's main unintended consequence was to put a ceiling on war between great powers, at the price of a permanent threat of catastrophe. Patel draws repeated parallels to today's AI labs, and Rhodes says AI will be at least as transformative.

## Key points

- **Material, not design.** Rhodes says the hard part is producing enriched uranium or plutonium; he claims Soviet scientists had a better design by 1947 but Beria ordered them to copy the American Fat Man bomb.
- **Bombs as firebombs.** Hiroshima looked much like cities already burned by incendiaries, Rhodes says, and because US planners counted only blast effects they demanded weapons such as 60 for Moscow, while the Air Force reached 47% of the defence budget by the mid-1950s.
- **Arms control.** Rhodes calls the Acheson-Lilienthal plan, shaped by Oppenheimer, the only workable idea for international control without world government, and says the Baruch revision doomed it.
- **Oppenheimer as director.** Edward Teller, Oppenheimer's adversary, told Rhodes he was "the best lab director I ever knew"; Rhodes credits him with winning openness inside Los Alamos against Groves's compartmentalisation.
- **Proliferation and risk.** Rhodes notes that Sweden and others explored bombs before the 1968 Non-Proliferation Treaty, warns that using nuclear arsenals to shield conventional war is a dangerous new doctrine, and puts the chance of a nuclear weapon used in anger within 50 years at probably above 10%.
- **Manhattan Project analogies.** Rhodes says the project was mostly engineering on settled science, kept secret across some 600,000 workers, so most "Manhattan Project for X" calls do not fit.

## Quotes
