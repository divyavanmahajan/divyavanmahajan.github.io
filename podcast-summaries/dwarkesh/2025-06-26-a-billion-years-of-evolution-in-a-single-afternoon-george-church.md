## Thesis

George Church argues that falling sequencing and synthesis costs, gene editing and AI-guided protein design have put biology on a curve at least as steep as Moore's law, with large payoffs in health and materials close at hand. He favours narrow scientific AI over a race to AGI, which he calls an artificial emergency, and wants stronger oversight of dual-use biotech. Dwarkesh Patel presses him on why the revolution has not yet shown up as many more drugs.

## Key points

- **Aging.** Church would not be surprised if around 2050 is the point where lifespan gains keep pace with time, though he stresses any estimate needs a big grain of salt.
- **Library-driven design.** He describes making huge libraries of variants, testing them and iterating, as evolution sped up; his 2004 chip-based gene synthesis paper in Nature was ignored for about a decade despite being far cheaper.
- **Biology as a materials platform.** Church contrasts the "1 nanometer" chip process (really about 40 nm spacing) with biology's 0.4 nm, three-dimensional precision, and expects nonstandard amino acids to open new materials, possibly a room-temperature superconductor.
- **Caution on AGI and dual use.** He would rather go slowly on AGI and ASI and wants an international consensus on safe AI. On biosecurity, which he discusses only in general terms, he says voluntary moratoria are not enough and calls for surveillance, consequences and whistleblower channels, citing the germline editing case.
- **Genetic counselling is underhyped.** Church says carrier screening, practised by Dor Yeshorim since 1985, costs about a hundred dollars per genome and gives at least a tenfold return. He advises his gene therapy companies to target common age-related and infectious diseases instead.
- **Why one lab spawned so many companies.** He credits Boston's density, an early commitment to linking basic science with societal needs, and hiring people who are nice and multidisciplinary.
