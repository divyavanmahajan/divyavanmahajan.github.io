## Thesis

Lewis Bollard, who runs Open Philanthropy's farm animal welfare program, argues that the end of factory farming is not inevitable, even with AGI. Chickens convert grain into meat so cheaply that alternatives struggle to compete, and cultural and political barriers remain. He says the most effective levers are corporate campaigns, government policy and humane technology, and that the field is badly underfunded.

## Key points

- **Hard cost target.** Bollard says the feed conversion ratio for chickens is 2x, so cultivated meat has to beat the price of grain times two. He does not think current venture funding puts it on that path, and notes cultivated meat is already illegal in seven US states.
- **Humane tech is starved of capital.** Bollard guesses humane technology gets less than $10 million a year in venture capital, compared with billions for alternative proteins. In-ovo sexing, kickstarted by about $10 million of public and philanthropic money, has already spared about 200 million male chicks and covers a third of the European egg industry.
- **Breeding drives suffering.** Chickens have seen a 4x gain in growth rates since the 1950s, says Bollard. Breeding birds are fed about 30% of what they would eat so they do not collapse. Higher welfare breeds, adopted in Denmark and pledged by France's LDC Group, cost slightly more.
- **Small budgets, large results.** Bollard estimates less than $300 million a year goes to all work on factory farming globally. Cage-free campaigns spare over 200 million hens a year; the US went from under 10% to 47% cage-free. Patel announces a $250,000 donation match through FarmKind.
- **Lobbying power.** Bollard says the pork industry, after losing at the Supreme Court over California's and Massachusetts' sales standards, is pushing farm bill language to override them through the Agriculture Committees. Patel cites the meat lobby's roughly $45 million per election cycle.
- **Corporate campaigns work.** Advocates have secured over 3,000 corporate pledges, Bollard says. Cage-free eggs cost 19 cents more per dozen to produce, yet Walmart and Kroger mark them up; Costco went 100% cage-free.
