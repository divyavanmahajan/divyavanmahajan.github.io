## Thesis

Sarah Paine argues that analysts must study the other side's culture instead of "mirror imaging", and uses Japan in the 1930s and 1940s as the case. Bushido's focus on honour, death, loyalty and willpower, she says, led Japan to equate operational wins with strategic success, neglect logistics and grand strategy, and fight on long after defeat was certain. In the Q&A, Dwarkesh Patel presses her on whether the US oil embargo and diplomacy made war avoidable; she is sceptical.

## Key points

- **Bushido as strategy.** Paine reads Yamamoto Tsunetomo's Hagakure, Nitobe Inazō and Miyamoto Musashi to show a literature about deportment and honourable death rather than winning, which she links to banzai charges, kamikaze pilots and surprise attacks that opened every Japanese war.
- **Resource gap ignored.** Japan never produced more than 1/13th of US steel and coal output; the US had about 18 support personnel per rifleman against Japan's roughly 1 to 1. Production figures were dismissed as propaganda and officers who knew better were not promoted.
- **Shipping and logistics.** She cites Prince Higashikuni naming the loss of transport shipping as the basic cause of defeat; Japan ended the war with one ninth of its shipping after US submarines cut its sea lanes.
- **Army-navy rivalry.** Separate, secret war plans from 1906, the Kwantung Army starting the Manchurian invasion, Guadalcanal, and the navy hiding the loss of four carriers at Midway for months illustrate in-group stovepiping.
- **The end and the peace.** Paine describes Hiroshima, the Soviet entry into Manchuria with 1.3 million troops, and Nagasaki within four days breaking the cabinet deadlock, followed by a generous peace and a constitution written in one week by MacArthur's staff.
- **Embargo debate.** Patel suggests the oil embargo and mistranslations made war avoidable; Paine counters that the Hawley-Smoot tariff set the conditions, that 600,000 casualties in China made withdrawal impossible, and that Roosevelt feared a Japanese attack on Russia.
