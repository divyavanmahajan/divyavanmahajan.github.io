## Thesis

Former GitHub CEO Nat Friedman tells Dwarkesh Patel that big opportunities often sit in plain sight because people wrongly assume someone else is already handling them. He applies this to the Vesuvius Challenge, the GitHub acquisition and Copilot, and argues that AI models will proliferate widely rather than concentrate in a few labs.

## Key points

- **Vesuvius Challenge.** With Daniel Gross, Friedman is funding a prize to read a carbonized Herculaneum scroll without opening it, building on Brent Seales' 8-micron particle-accelerator scans. Reading the 600-plus scrolls could roughly double surviving ancient text; he puts success this year at 50%.
- **Markets are inefficient.** Hedge fund traders told him that sharing month-old strategies for 10 minutes a month would cut profits by about 80%, which he sees as evidence the world is less efficient than assumed, and as a model for AI lab secrets.
- **Buying GitHub.** Friedman pitched Satya Nadella that developers now drive purchasing and a new generation had no affinity with Microsoft. As CEO he prioritised developer trust, and Microsoft pledged its patent portfolio to open source.
- **Copilot's origin.** After GPT-3, early prototypes (a Q&A chatbot, whole-function synthesis with multiple options) disappointed; Alex Graveley's idea of switching between line and block completion based on cursor position worked. Friedman says Copilot now writes over half of code and could reach 95%.
- **AI proliferation.** He argues training recipes are simple, data is public and hardware is commodity, so many models will exist; he also sees diminishing returns, guessing GPT-4 cost two orders of magnitude more than GPT-3 without being that much more capable.
- **Alignment.** Friedman notes there is no open source technical alignment community and cites Microsoft's Sydney as a warning.
