## Thesis

Researcher and designer Andy Matuschak tells Dwarkesh Patel that most readers fail to absorb hard material not because they forget it but because they never understood it, and never noticed. He argues that reading well is a skill built on asking questions, that memory is underrated as the basis of understanding and creative insight, and that learning tools work best when they take on planning and self-monitoring for the learner.

## Key points

- **Active reading.** Drawing on Adler and Van Doren's How to Read a Book, Matuschak says the first rule of skillful reading is asking questions and trying to answer them. Patel describes watching him spend about 15 minutes per page on a quantum mechanics textbook.
- **Outsourced metacognition.** When material is hard, planning and self-monitoring suffer, so Matuschak says syllabi and embedded prompts help. In Quantum Country, built with Michael Nielsen, review questions placed every 1,500 words or so also showed readers what they had skimmed past.
- **Memory matters.** Matuschak argues that following complex arguments and noticing surprising connections both depend on memory. At Khan Academy, memorised market figures let him judge strategy ideas on the spot. He says years of practice have not pushed out other memories.
- **Media and tacit knowledge.** He sees video (Grant Sanderson), games such as The Witness, Nand to Tetris and live-streaming (George Hotz) as partial models. He wants more designer streamers, because design is still taught through studio feedback.
- **MVPs and ideas.** Matuschak says building Orbit quickly, as a general platform, was the wrong test. One-off experiments would have taught him more, because strong interfaces come from new ideas, like the Photoshop layer.
- **Crowdfunding and Apple.** His Patreon loses about 2% of subscribers a month, and he says it pays somewhere between a grad student and a junior faculty member. He avoids marketing, which he sees as harmful to honest inquiry. At Apple, he says, decisions were heavily delegated: Craig Federighi focused closely on a few issues and left about 95% to others.
