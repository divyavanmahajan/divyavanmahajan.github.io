## Thesis

Journalist Bethany McLean, author of The Smartest Guys in the Room, tells Dwarkesh Patel that business failures like Enron, Theranos and FTX are rarely planned frauds: they are built on self-delusion, compelling leaders and cultures that reward the wrong things. She argues that regulation tends to fight the last crisis, that a visionary and a fraudster often differ only in whether the money keeps flowing, and that nobody is really assigned to watch the big picture.

## Key points

- **Legal fraud.** McLean says much of Enron's collapse came from abusing accounting rules rather than outright fraud, and that Sarbanes-Oxley and later Dodd-Frank were sold with near-identical promises that markets soon outran.
- **Too few short sellers.** She attributes the shortage to a view that shorting is un-American, four decades of falling interest rates, and the rare temperament needed to stay contrarian; she notes FTX and Theranos were private companies without public financials or short sellers.
- **Visionary or fraudster.** McLean argues Enron Broadband might have become a Netflix ahead of its time had Enron kept raising capital, and sees Elon Musk somewhere on that spectrum, citing the SolarCity acquisition.
- **Culture and deterrence.** She doubts prison sentences deter white-collar crime, because people do not see themselves as the next Jeff Skilling, and says culture can make smart employees believers.
- **Finance is too big.** Responding to Patel's figure of 9% of GDP, she says finance should be the lubricant for business, not the thing itself, and blames low interest rates for outsized private equity returns.
- **Systemic risk and journalism.** McLean says 2008 was caused by mortgages embedded in the plumbing of the system, says an internal Washington Mutual presentation on pushing riskier mortgages changed her view on borrower blame, and warns that the decline of local news reduces the chance of spotting the next crisis.
