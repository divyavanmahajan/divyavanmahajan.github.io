## Thesis

Former UK Prime Minister Tony Blair tells Dwarkesh Patel that elected leaders usually fail at change not because of a "deep state" but because government is a conspiracy for inertia and distraction, and because winning office and governing need different skills. He argues that governing is a professional discipline built on deep policy work, the right people and relentless focus. Above all, he says, leaders must now engage with the AI revolution, which he calls the biggest technological change since the Industrial Revolution.

## Key points

- **Persuader to chief executive.** Blair says he took office with zero ministerial experience; campaigning rewards "saying", governing rewards "doing". He only reformed healthcare, education and criminal justice in his second five years.
- **Ambitions are not policies.** His institute works in roughly 40 countries. Leaders typically list ambitions, not policies, and put politics first and policy second; time audits find some spend 4 percent of their time on their priorities. His "four Ps" are prioritise, policy, personnel and performance management.
- **Not ready for an AI crisis.** Asked about a "July 1914" moment for AI, Blair says governments today "wouldn't know where to begin". Experts and the private sector should set out the options, but politicians must choose, and "when you decide, you divide", as COVID showed with Sweden versus China.
- **Lessons from Lee Kuan Yew.** Singapore's three contested early decisions were English as the common language, importing the best talent and zero tolerance of corruption with well-paid leaders.
- **Leadership decides outcomes.** Blair cites Poland versus Ukraine, Rwanda versus Burundi and South Korea, once level with Sierra Leone in GDP per head. He urges developing countries not to copy Western systems and proposes a "reimagined state" using AI tutors and AI doctors, citing Khan Academy.
- **Rhythm of reform.** A reform is called a terrible idea at first and is hell while you do it; afterwards you wish you had done more. He recommends a Zen-like attitude to criticism and a half-page social media summary each morning.
