## Thesis

Kenneth T. Jackson, Columbia historian of New York and editor of *Robert Moses and the Modern City*, tells Dwarkesh Patel that Moses was the greatest builder in American history and that, without his public works, New York might have declined like Detroit or St. Louis. He calls Robert Caro's *The Power Broker* the best book he ever read and correct in broad strokes, but full of small errors. He also says Moses misread neighbourhoods and density, and that today's preservation culture would stop him.

## Key points

- **Scale and method.** Jackson lists the bridges (Triborough, Throgs Neck, Verrazano-Narrows), Jones Beach, more than 700 playgrounds, Lincoln Center and two World's Fairs. Moses held many posts at once, worked for a dollar a year, and used Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority bonds, with a chairman removable only for cause, as a self-renewing source of money.
- **Rise, not fall.** Jackson notes Pittsburgh, Detroit and St. Louis lost half or more of their population while New York grew. The New York area went from about 20% to 40% of US transit riders, so he argues Moses did not destroy transit; he just did not help it.
- **Disputing Caro.** Jackson says census tracts show Jews were already leaving East Tremont before the Cross Bronx Expressway, and that Caro ignored what Los Angeles and Chicago were doing.
- **Moses' blind spots.** Moses liked cities but not neighbourhoods or people, wanted an expressway through Greenwich Village, and never drove himself. Jackson sides with Jane Jacobs, who "saw the block". Patel cites an NBER digest by Morgan Foy finding 7 of the 10 highest-return US highway segments are in the New York area.
- **NIMBYism today.** Jackson argues that historic preservation and an "old is better" attitude make building harder now; he doubts Moses could repeat his record and names climate-change flood defences as a project a Moses-like figure could take on.
