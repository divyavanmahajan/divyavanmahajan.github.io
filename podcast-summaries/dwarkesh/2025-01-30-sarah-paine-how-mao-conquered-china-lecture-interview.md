## Thesis

Sarah Paine, drawing on the first eight volumes of Stuart Schram's edition of Mao's collected works, argues that Mao was both a military genius who reunified a shattered China and a psychopath unable to run a peacetime economy. She frames him through Clausewitz's idea of coup d'oeil and determination, as propagandist, social scientist, military leader and grand strategist. In the Q&A, Dwarkesh Patel presses her on how such a shrewd commander produced the Great Leap Forward, and whether the US could have saved the Nationalists.

## Key points

- **Propaganda first.** Paine notes that every Red Army unit had a propaganda team of about 20 people, and that foreign journalists such as Edgar Snow, author of *Red Star Over China*, acted as Mao's international broadcasting system.
- **Data-driven land reform.** Mao's rural surveys from 1926 to 1933 found that 6% of the rural population owned 80% of the land. Paine describes his land reform as a bait and switch: peasants received land to win their loyalty, then lost it to collectivisation.
- **People's war needs a big friend.** Mao's three stages run from strategic defensive to strategic offensive. Paine argues that stage three requires conventional arms from an industrial patron, which is why Mao retreated to Yan'an, near the Soviet Union. She adds that the Nationalists, not the Communists, did almost all the conventional fighting against Japan.
- **Catastrophe in peace.** Paine cites Yang Jisheng's figure of 40 million deaths in the Great Famine, the only nationwide famine in Chinese history, caused by communes, backyard furnaces and continued food exports. She attributes Mao's later blindness to purges that removed anyone who could argue back.
- **US policy and Taiwan.** Patel argues that the US underfunded Chiang Kai-shek, at roughly 1/100th of the Marshall Plan. Paine counters that nation-building in China was not feasible. She contrasts this with Taiwan, which by around 1990 had about 40% of the mainland's GNP with 1/50th of its population.
