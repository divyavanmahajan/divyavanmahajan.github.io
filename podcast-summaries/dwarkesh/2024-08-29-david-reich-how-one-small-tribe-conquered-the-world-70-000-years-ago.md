## Thesis

Harvard geneticist David Reich argues that ancient DNA keeps overturning textbook accounts of the human past. Almost every population is the product of repeated mixture, and who ends up on top looks less like innate superiority and more like a contingent process shaped by culture, demography and disease. Dwarkesh Patel presses him on what this implies for intelligence, and by analogy for AI.

## Key points

- **The standard model is full of epicycles.** Reich says the accepted split of modern humans from Neanderthals and Denisovans 500,000-750,000 years ago clashes with mitochondrial and Y chromosome data pointing to 300,000-400,000 years, and that very different models may fit better.
- **An archipelago of tiny groups.** Reich describes Africa and Eurasia as full of small, isolated bands that lost diversity and knowledge (the Indigenous Tasmanians losing fire) until one took off culturally. He suspects the expansion was cultural rather than genetic.
- **Plague as a hidden force.** Reich cites *Yersinia pestis* in 5-10% of randomly sequenced people from 4000-5000 years ago and suggests a quarter to a half of deaths may have been due to it, possibly opening Europe to steppe migrants.
- **Steppe replacement.** In Britain, within 100 years of the last big stones of Stonehenge going up, 90% of the farmers were gone, replaced by migrants with steppe ancestry. In Iberia the Y chromosomes were completely replaced. Reich describes a two-step spread via the Yamnaya and Corded Ware.
- **Recent selection targets metabolism and immunity.** Using about 8500 sequences in work led by Ali Akbari, Reich reports immune and cardiometabolic traits overrepresented by a factor of maybe four, with selection against body fat, while cognitive traits are hardly affected.
- **Frozen mixture in South Asia.** Reich explains that mixing between Ancestral North Indians and Ancestral South Indians froze 2000 to 3000 years ago as the caste system set in. His top research wish is old DNA from across Africa.
