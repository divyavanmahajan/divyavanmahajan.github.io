## Thesis

Jacob Kimmel, co-founder and president of NewLimit, argues that evolution put little effort into keeping us healthy for long, so ageing is a problem with low-hanging fruit rather than one already optimised. He expects no single cure, but medicines that add years of healthy life by resetting how cells behave, discovered with AI models trained on proprietary lab data and paid for through new reimbursement models.

## Key points

- **Why evolution let us age.** Kimmel gives three reasons: early humans rarely lived long enough for longevity to be selected for, an ageing population may have been a net cost to the group, and evolution's limited "updates" went mostly to fighting infectious disease.
- **No magic pill.** Because ageing has many causes, Kimmel expects first medicines to add healthy years without stopping decline entirely, and says treating even one organ, such as the liver, can benefit the whole body, citing transplant outcomes and Ozempic.
- **Too many combinations to test.** Kimmel contrasts Shinya Yamanaka's Nobel-winning discovery, where success was easy to see, with ageing, where roughly 10^16 combinations make AI models that predict outcomes from sparse experiments essential.
- **Eroom's Law.** Drawing on Jack Scannell's term, Kimmel explains that new drugs per billion dollars have fallen since the 1950s; he argues the field needs medicines for large populations and models that make each discovery feed the next. Patel compares this with LLM pre-training followed by task-specific tuning.
- **Paying for long-lasting drugs.** Since US patients switch insurers every three to four years, Kimmel proposes pay-for-performance and direct-to-consumer models like LillyDirect, and notes about a third of Medicare costs fall in the final year of life.
- **Industry structure.** Kimmel describes big pharma as acting like venture capital, with small biotechs originating around 70% of approved molecules while large firms run the costly trials.

## Quotes
