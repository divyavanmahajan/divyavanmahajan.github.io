## Thesis

Stalin biographer Stephen Kotkin argues that Stalin's regime came out of real tsarist injustice, sincere Marxist-Leninist belief and a peasant land revolution. Those forces produced a far worse tyranny, which Kotkin calls "perverse and unintended consequences". Stalin, he says, became Stalin only through holding power. Leninist systems survive by suppressing alternatives, but they cannot create legitimacy. Kotkin applies that to China today, while Dwarkesh Patel pushes back on how much credit the CCP deserves.

## Key points

- **Modernization as geopolitics.** Kotkin says autocracies modernize to compete with rivals but must repress the workers and engineers they need. That was tsarist Russia's dilemma, and he says Iran, Russia and China still face it.
- **Peasants and the left.** Russia's land-hungry peasant army seized land instead of putting down the urban left, which helped Lenin hold power. Stalin then re-enserfed roughly 100 million peasants and built a huge secret police in the process.
- **Belief, not just fear.** Kotkin says Marxism-Leninism appealed to impatient young people and gave intellectuals power. He argues that what needs explaining is less the psychology than why the system survived killing its own loyalists and police.
- **China's growth.** Kotkin argues society, not the Party, lifted people out of poverty. He credits Deng Xiaoping with one deliberate move: copying Japan's export model to the US market, using British Hong Kong, Taiwanese investment and most-favored-nation status from 1980. Patel argues policy deserves more credit.
- **Why nobody killed Stalin.** Stalin offered to resign six times between 1923 and 1928, and his colleagues begged him to stay. Kotkin points to their belief in the system, a collective action problem, and pervasive distrust of possible provocateurs.
- **Legitimacy and tech.** The Soviets hoped computers would fix planning. Kotkin expects China's bet on tech to hit the same limit: repression works until the repressors defect, like a political bank run.
