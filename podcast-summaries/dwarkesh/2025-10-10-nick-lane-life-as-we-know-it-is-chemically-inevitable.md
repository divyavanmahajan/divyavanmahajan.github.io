## Thesis

Nick Lane, an evolutionary biochemist at University College London, argues that early life was continuous with the chemistry of alkaline deep-sea hydrothermal vents, where proton gradients across mineral pores drive hydrogen and CO2 to react into organics. Because that chemistry is thermodynamically favoured, he expects similar simple life on many wet, rocky planets. The hard step, in his view, is the eukaryotic cell, which arose once on Earth about 2 billion years ago through the acquisition of mitochondria. Dwarkesh Patel repeatedly pushes back on whether evolution really has no other route to complexity.

## Key points

- **Vents as proto-cells.** Building on work by Bill Martin and Mike Russell, Lane describes vent pores with acidic ocean outside and alkaline fluid inside, lined with iron and nickel sulfides, producing Krebs cycle intermediates and, in his lab, fatty-acid membranes at 70-90°C.
- **Abundant simple life.** Lane estimates 20-40 billion wet, rocky planets or moons in the Milky Way, guesses roughly 50% could make nucleotides, and points to Enceladus plumes with organics, hydrogen and a pH of eight or nine.
- **Eukaryotes as the filter.** Large genomes need mitochondria, Lane says; giant bacteria instead rely on extreme polyploidy, up to 700,000-800,000 genome copies. Patel finds it hard to believe no other planet finds an alternative; Lane cites Orgel's second rule but asks for a concrete mechanism.
- **Mitochondria and two sexes.** Uniparental inheritance of mitochondria increases variance between cells so selection can purge mutations. Lane links this to protected oocytes versus mass-produced sperm and to the degenerate Y chromosome, and notes mitochondrial DNA fell from 3,000-4,000 genes to 37.
- **Sex versus lateral gene transfer.** Bacteria keep small genomes with a large pan-genome (E. coli: 3,000-4,000 genes, access to 30,000-40,000); Lane argues lateral transfer stops scaling once genomes grow, so eukaryotes needed systematic recombination.
- **Anaesthetics and feelings.** Lane reports early experiments suggesting anaesthetics act on mitochondria, possibly complex I, and speculates that feelings relate to fields generated by membrane potential.
