## Thesis

On his third appearance, Bryan Caplan tells Dwarkesh Patel that many popular positions survive because challenging them feels socially costly, not because the evidence supports them. He applies this to his book Don't Be a Feminist, to student loan forgiveness, to refugee policy and to taxing billionaires, while presenting his own method as weighing each issue separately and conceding points against himself.

## Key points

- **Defining feminism.** Caplan defines feminism as the view that society treats women less fairly than men. In the US he calls the objective measures close to a wash and says false accusations against men tip the balance. He concedes women are treated worse in Saudi Arabia and probably in India and China, citing female infanticide, and that paternal support has fallen from about 90% in 1940 to 60-70%.
- **Mentoring and misunderstandings.** Caplan says male managers now admit they hesitate to mentor women, and argues that siding with the most hypersensitive person harms workplaces and friendships.
- **Education as signalling.** Caplan calls student loan forgiveness a regressive transfer and blames credential inflation for wasted years in school. He reports a 23-for-23 record on resolved bets, including one that four-year college attendance would not fall by more than 10%.
- **Refugees in Poland.** After visiting Eastern Europe, Caplan says Poland grew its population by 10% in a month with Ukrainians allowed to work on arrival; he puts the US equivalent at 30-33 million people.
- **Decolonization and contingency.** Caplan argues decolonization should have followed a credible long timetable that prioritised human rights over democracy, as in post-war Germany and Japan. He calls India's partition badly botched and gives a 99% chance the Bolshevik takeover would not have happened without Lenin.
- **Billionaires and anarcho-capitalism.** Responding to Scott Alexander's case for higher taxes on billionaires, Caplan cites tournament theory: large rewards motivate everyone who might win. He also describes anarcho-capitalism, with competing police and courts, as his ideal long-term system.
