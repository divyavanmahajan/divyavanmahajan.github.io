## Thesis

Sarah Paine argues that clever decisions in Tokyo, not luck, explain how Japan defeated China and Russia to become Asia's dominant power: the Meiji generation Westernised its institutions, combined diplomacy, information, military and economic tools into one strategy, and ended the Russo-Japanese War before overreaching. She tests this against a counterargument (China's implosion) and a rebuttal built on Russia's expansion, and Dwarkesh Patel pushes back with Russia's 1905 revolution.

## Key points

- **Institutions before gadgets.** Paine says the Iwakura Mission of 1871 concluded Japan could not modernise technology without Westernising institutions; the Meiji reforms (1869-1890) brought compulsory education, a Bank of Japan, courts and a parliament, letting Britain end the treaty port system in 1894.
- **A closing window.** The Trans-Siberian Railway and the 1902 Anglo-Japanese Alliance gave Japan a short window; the railway moved only 20,000-40,000 men per month in 1904 versus 100,000 by the war's end.
- **Port Arthur and Tsushima.** Taking 203 Meter Hill cost General Nogi's assaults 45,000 dead but let Japan sink the fleet in harbour; the Baltic Fleet then sailed into the Battle of Tsushima, which Paine calls one of history's most lopsided naval battles.
- **Knowing when to quit.** Field Marshal Yamagata pushed for Roosevelt's mediation after Mukden, when Russia's army was three times Japan's; Paine says demanding more at Portsmouth would have restarted a war Japan would lose.
- **Pearl Harbor contrast.** Patel links the 1904 surprise attack to 1941; Paine answers that Nicholas II was a "cooperative adversary" while the United States was not, and that later leaders pursued regime change in China.
- **Institutions versus size.** Patel argues economic and population size matter most; Paine counters with Argentina and Russia as large, resource-rich countries held back by weak institutions.
