## Thesis

Lars Brownworth, author of The Sea Wolves and host of the 12 Byzantine Rulers podcast, argues that the Vikings were not mindless brutes. He describes them as pragmatic farmers and traders who used speed and terror as weapons. Within a generation or two they turned from raiders into state builders, and their destruction cleared the way for a stronger, outward-looking Europe, shielded in the east by Byzantium.

## Key points

- **Lindisfarne and terror.** Brownworth dates the start of the Viking Age to the raid on Lindisfarne in 793, which broke the medieval taboo against attacking churches. He says Vikings scouted ports as traders and then raided on holy days like Easter.
- **Ships as the secret.** Longships could cross the Atlantic yet had a draft under two feet and covered 70 to 120 miles a day. An English army managed 10 to 15. Ethelred the Unready's payments, including 7.5 million silver pennies in one year, only attracted more raiders.
- **Ragnar and Rollo.** Brownworth treats Ragnar Lothbrok as a partly mythical template: he sacked Paris in 845 and was paid about 7,000 pounds of silver. Rollo took Normandy by treaty in 911. Within a generation his descendants had dropped Norse names, language and gods, but kept their drive.
- **Religion and Valhalla.** Norse cosmology was a war between order and chaos that chaos eventually wins at Ragnarok. Valhalla meant daily combat for brave warriors, and Berserkers were Odin's chosen fighters.
- **West and East.** Erik the Red's naming of Greenland was, in Brownworth's words, a real estate scam. Leif Ericson's Vinland lasted about three years: the settlers refused to give up livestock farming, were too far from Norway to be resupplied, and faced constant native attacks. Swedish Varangians founded the Kievan Rus, lost to Greek fire at Constantinople, and became the emperor's guard.
- **Byzantium and human nature.** Brownworth traces Byzantine decline to the bureaucracy choosing weak rulers after Basil II died in 1025, which led to defeat at Manzikert in 1071. He holds to a great-man view of history and sees human nature as flawed and unchanging.
