## Thesis

Dan Reynolds, lead singer of Imagine Dragons, tells Lex Fridman that the core of good art is truth that the audience perceives as truth: people sense when a performer does not mean it. He traces his songs to lifelong depression, a crisis of faith after a Mormon upbringing in Las Vegas, therapy and ayahuasca, and ends on open-minded not-knowing.

## Key points

- **The bullshit indicator.** Reynolds says audiences judge whether an artist believes what they sing, citing Harry Nilsson's "Without You". Rick Rubin's main test in the studio was saying he did not believe Reynolds on a song, which was enough to drop it. They argued line by line over lyrics, including the "number one" line, which Reynolds kept.
- **Songwriting as a journal.** Reynolds writes lyrics, melody and music together with no theme in mind, finishes every idea, and releases a small fraction. He says the single "I Bet My Life" was overproduced and taught him not to release songs he does not want to hear himself.
- **Depression, fame and therapy.** He describes feeling nothing during a near plane crash early in his career, the post-show crash that pushes musicians towards drugs, and Charlie Sheen's early warning that the band's life was about to get very strange. He says delaying therapy almost cost him his life and recommends exercise, cutting alcohol and a non-religious therapist.
- **Faith and ayahuasca.** Losing his Mormon faith in his twenties left him angry at his family; Reynolds says a second ayahuasca session, following his wife's example, felt like communicating with something wiser and helped him forgive. He calls it the scariest experience of his life and does not push it on others.
- **LGBTQ advocacy and religion.** Reynolds links his advocacy to friends' struggles, a teenager's suicide at the Las Vegas Mormon temple and Prop 8. He credits Mormonism with his family's closeness, but says religion has also caused much pain.
- **Self-love and doubt.** Fridman asks whether self-criticism is necessary; Reynolds says some self-doubt pushes him, but young artists need enough self-love to believe their own songs.
