## Thesis

Peter Norvig, Google research director and co-author with Stuart Russell of "Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach", tells Lex Fridman that the earlier editions treated AI as maximising expected utility, while the fourth edition recognises that choosing the utility function is the hard part. He also argues that trust in AI should rest on validation, testing and conversation rather than single explanations, and that programming is becoming more about assembling components and modelling problems than writing code from scratch.

## Key points

- **How the book changed.** Norvig says the 1995 edition bet on probability over Boolean logic and machine learning over knowledge engineering, but missed big data and deep learning. The new edition has a deep learning chapter by guest author Ian Goodfellow and more on fairness, bias and inverse reinforcement learning.
- **Fairness trade-offs.** Using recidivism-scoring tools as an example, Norvig explains that equal calibration across protected classes and equal error rates cannot both be achieved, so humans must deliberate on the trade-off with the analysis in hand.
- **Trust over explainability.** He notes that a bank's explanation of a loan denial may not reflect the real reason; testing across all cases, back-and-forth dialogue and adversarial testing are needed. He describes adversarial images as showing that a cat is a thin path through a million-dimension space, not a region of a flat 2D map.
- **MOOCs and motivation.** From teaching an intro AI course to about 160,000 students, Norvig concludes motivation and community matter more than content.
- **Lisp, Python and code.** He says Lisp suited small skilled teams but did not scale to large ones; when looking for a language close to his textbook pseudocode, Python matched it almost exactly. He tells new learners modelling and problem solving matter more than syntax.
- **Search and risks.** As director of search quality (2001-2005), Norvig says Google faced a game-theoretic contest with webmasters. He worries less about Terminator scenarios than about employment disruption and inequality, and wants "did you mean" style assistants for code.
