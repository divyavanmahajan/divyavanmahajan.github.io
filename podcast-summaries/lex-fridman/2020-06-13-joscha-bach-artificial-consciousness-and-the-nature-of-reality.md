## Thesis

Joscha Bach, VP of Research at the AI Foundation, tells Lex Fridman that intelligence is the ability to make models, and that the mind is software that builds both a model of the world and a model of a self living in it. In his view a physical system cannot be conscious; only a simulation can. He applies the same lens to organisms, societies and governments, and is pessimistic about how long industrial civilization can last.

## Key points

- **Computation as foundation.** Bach says the constructivist turn in mathematics, which he traces through Gödel, Turing and Wittgenstein, shows that the parts of mathematics that work are computation, and that this has not yet fully reached philosophy or physics.
- **Self as a story.** Bach describes experience as a multimedia novel the brain keeps writing; colour and sound are representations, not features of the physical world. Identity is a software state, and he uses the Dalai Lama, who identifies as a form of government, as an example of an identity that outlives a body.
- **Consciousness and attention.** Bach calls consciousness a model of the contents of attention, which evolved because brains cannot afford slow, backpropagation-style learning. He argues transformer attention captures only one aspect: tracking identity across a text.
- **Spirit as operating system.** Bach redefines "spirit" as the operating system of an autonomous system such as a plant, a person or a society, and calls each neuron a small reinforcement learning agent.
- **Civilization and climate.** Bach argues the Industrial Revolution burned a hundred million years' worth of trees and that many equatorial regions may only be livable with a "closed cooling chain" within 100 years. Fridman counters that technology and the spread of ideas through platforms such as YouTube and Twitter could win the race; Bach says US technology has been largely incremental since the 1970s.
- **Emotion and happiness.** Bach calls emotions configurations of the cognitive system, contrasts American and Russian norms on positive affect, and says suffering comes from caring about things you cannot change.
