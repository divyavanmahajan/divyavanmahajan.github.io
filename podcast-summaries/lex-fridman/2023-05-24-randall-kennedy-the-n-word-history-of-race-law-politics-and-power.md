## Thesis

Randall Kennedy, a Harvard Law School professor and author of a book on the N-word, tells Lex Fridman that the word's meaning depends on context and that people should be educated about it rather than shielded from it. The same view shapes his positions on free speech on campus, racial profiling and affirmative action. He says his optimism about American race relations has been tested since 2016 but that he still counts himself among the optimists.

## Key points

- **The word and its history.** Kennedy says the word had become a slur by the early 19th century. He has been an expert witness in murder and employment cases, and says teachers are disciplined every year for handing out excerpts of his book, which he calls bad pedagogy.
- **Context and responsibility.** He points to Dick Gregory, Richard Pryor and James Baldwin as people who used the word to confront society. He grants that an elderly critic who said the book gives people permission to use the word had a point. His advice to white fans of rap is to be prudent and avoid it.
- **Speech on campus.** Kennedy opposes technology that blanks the word and objects to required DEI statements and positionality statements. He calls state bans on so-called critical race theory a frightening limit on freedom. He agrees with critical race theorists that racism is deeply embedded, but rejects Derrick Bell's view that racism is permanent and the movement's support for restricting hate speech.
- **Policing.** He describes two harms: Black Americans being under-protected and being discriminated against. He wants police paid more and held accountable, and argues that a Black man stopped because of crime statistics pays a "racial tax".
- **Affirmative action.** Kennedy sets out reparative, distributive and diversity justifications. He thinks the diversity rationale is often a pretext and admits the policy brings stigma and resentment, but believes the benefits outweigh those costs. He defends meritocracy against parts of Michael Sandel's critique.
- **Optimists and pessimists.** He puts Jefferson, Tocqueville, Lincoln and his own father in the pessimist camp, and credits teachers such as Jack McCune at St. Albans with his more hopeful view.
