## Thesis

James Sexton, a New York divorce trial lawyer of more than two decades and author of How to Stay in Love, argues that marriages rarely end because of one big event such as cheating; they end through disconnection that builds "very slowly and then all at once". He says couples should name small changes early, cheer for each other in public, and treat love as a deliberate practice. Lex Fridman pushes the romantic side: take the risk, and remember the good years even after a breakup.

## Key points

- **Granola and blowjobs.** Sexton's example of disconnection: a husband stopped buying his wife's favourite granola, she stopped giving blowjobs, and neither said anything. He says people wait for the affair or financial impropriety before asking where it went wrong.
- **Don't criticise your partner.** Sexton calls the cultural habit of mocking a long-term spouse toxic; the strongest couples he sees are "thick as thieves". Fridman cites Joe Rogan never speaking negatively of his wife.
- **Infidelity types.** Sexton describes the "soulmate" affair (often sparked on Facebook or Instagram), being pushed out of the closet, and the "mistake". He says he has rarely seen very happy couples cheat, and calls open marriages often a "Hail Mary pass".
- **Cost and prenups.** A "yellow pad" divorce agreed at the kitchen table costs about $3,500 to $5,000; contested cases can run to millions, with some clients paying him $2-3 million. He calls prenups "an excellent idea" (his most expensive cost about $3,000), favours a "yours, mine, and ours" structure, and discourages fidelity and sunset clauses. Kevin Costner's prenup, he says, worked.
- **Co-parenting.** Sexton warns that children get weaponised through subtle alienation. He wishes his ex-wife happy Mother's Day every year and drafted a clause requiring parents to help young children buy the other parent a gift; many lawyers struck it out.
- **Courtroom habits.** He uses an "8 Mile strategy": have clients own damaging evidence first. He has also floated guided psilocybin sessions for divorcing couples in mediation, while admitting it would be a legal nightmare.
