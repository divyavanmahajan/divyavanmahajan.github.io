## Thesis

Michael Paulson (ThePrimeagen) says there are no shortcuts. He applies this to his own life, which went from addiction and failing at school to a decade at Netflix, and to programming, where he thinks AI tools tempt developers to give up deep skill in exchange for speed. Lex Fridman broadly agrees, but argues that working with AI is itself a skill worth practising.

## Key points

- **Turnaround through grinding.** Paulson describes being exposed to pornography at four or five, losing his father at seven, then meth use and a suicide attempt. At about 19 he felt the presence of God, and he says he suddenly had a conscience. He failed pre-calculus twice and passed on the third attempt. Later he was the only one of 400 students to finish the differential equations final.
- **Netflix war stories.** Over 10 years at Netflix he found a Falcor flaw that was later named the "Repulsive Grizzly Attack": a single request could take down a single machine. Reversing the Black Mirror season order meant pulling together about 20 engineers for roughly three weeks.
- **AI and learned helplessness.** Paulson used GitHub Copilot from the early access Nat Friedman gave him until about six months before the interview. He stopped because he noticed he was "autopiloting" and adding bugs. He finds AI good at predictable work such as TypeScript but weak with less-documented languages like Zig. He worries that juniors who hand everything to AI will cap their own skill at the level of the models.
- **Asserts and simulation testing.** Fridman and John Carmack's conversation about asserts, plus TigerBeetle creator Joran, convinced Paulson to put asserts in every function and run deterministic simulation tests. Doing so surfaced bugs he says he would never have found.
- **Setup and focus.** He uses one monitor, a Kinesis keyboard, Neovim and his Harpoon plugin, and maps each place he works to a single key press. For ADHD, he credits maturity (rereading a page until he understands it) and his wife's support.
- **Leaving Netflix.** Streaming started with an Extra Life charity stream in June 2018. Years later, after the Streamer Awards, he left to stream full-time in April, less than a year before this conversation. He says the work can be lonely.
