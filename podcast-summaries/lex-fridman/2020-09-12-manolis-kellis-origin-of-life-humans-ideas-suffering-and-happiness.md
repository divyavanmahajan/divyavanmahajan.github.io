## Thesis

Manolis Kellis, professor at MIT and head of its Computational Biology Group, tells Lex Fridman that life is a chain of transformations: from self-reinforcing chemistry, to specialised cells and bodies, to brains that can override what evolution programmed them for. He carries the same idea into human life, arguing that meaning comes from the journey and its struggles, not from milestones or constant happiness.

## Key points

- **Selection at many layers.** Kellis argues evolution selects not only organisms but whole ecosystems, which he offers as one explanation for altruism and cooperation.
- **What life needs.** He lists three ingredients: metabolism, compartmentalisation (a notion of self) and some form of self-reinforcing replication. He says early life likely ran on RNA alone, with DNA and proteins added later for efficiency, and would bet on the ocean floor near hydrothermal vents as the starting point. He expects life on Europa and says it will not be DNA based.
- **Life fights physics.** To detect alien life, Kellis would look for chemical signatures that physics alone does not predict. He adds that the brain in turn can override life, for example by choosing not to have children.
- **The rat race.** Quoting Christos Papadimitriou's joke about academics working "like a rat", Kellis describes a midlife crisis at 42, which led to his Meaning of Life symposium. He prefers a view of life focused on the path, citing a Greek poem about Ulysses's voyage to Ithaca.
- **Embracing the bad.** Kellis says Hollywood's happy endings leave people unprepared, and he reads his children sad Greek stories to widen their emotional range. He tells his mother he aims to be fulfilled, not happy.
- **Ageing and MIT.** He describes his thinking moving from speed at 20 to creativity and big-picture guidance at 40, quotes an advisor's advice to "let others write the second paper", and closes by reading two poems he wrote as a teenager.
