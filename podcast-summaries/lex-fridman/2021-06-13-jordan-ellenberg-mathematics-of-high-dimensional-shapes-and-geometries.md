## Thesis

Jordan Ellenberg, a mathematician at the University of Wisconsin and author of Shape and How Not to Be Wrong, tells Lex Fridman that much of modern mathematics turns on one question: when should two things be considered the same? Symmetry, topology and unusual notions of distance all answer that question in different ways. He argues that the goal of mathematics is understanding, with theorems as the benchmark, and he favours complication over the search for simple answers that Fridman finds appealing.

## Key points

- **Symmetry and AI.** Ellenberg defines a symmetry as any transformation, such as a stretch he calls a "scronch". Fridman links this to recognising MNIST handwritten digits; Ellenberg doubts that known symmetry types capture how people tell a two from a three.
- **Higher dimensions.** Ellenberg explains that Poincaré needed six-dimensional phase space to study the three-body problem, the first example of chaotic dynamics. He describes the Poincaré conjecture using loops on a mug, and Perelman's proof, building on Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow, as studying the space of all three-dimensional geometries.
- **Holes in a straw.** In Ellenberg's account, people split into zero-, one- and two-hole camps. His answer draws on homology: a straw has two holes, one the negative of the other, and holes can be added like numbers.
- **Distance is a choice.** Ellenberg describes 2-adic distance, where numbers are close if their difference is divisible by a large power of two, and notes that the deformation arguments in Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem use this kind of distance. He compares it to the learned distances in word embeddings.
- **Primes as random.** Ellenberg says number theorists expect infinitely many twin primes because primes behave as if randomly scattered, even though they are deterministic. He covers Fermat's primality test and its pseudoprimes, such as 341.
- **AI and learning.** Ellenberg recalls tasks that once made a PhD thesis and now take a few lines in Macaulay, Sage or Magma, and he cites Lisa Piccirillo's nine-page solution of the Conway knot problem. His advice for learning maths is to let a problem you care about drive it.
