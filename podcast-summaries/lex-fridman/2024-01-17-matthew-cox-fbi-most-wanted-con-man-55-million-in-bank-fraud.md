## Thesis

Matthew Cox, who served 13 years in federal prison for bank, mortgage and passport fraud, describes a slide that started with whiting out one late rent payment and ended with him on the Secret Service's most wanted list. He says need was the first motive and ego the second, and argues that nearly every criminal cooperates with prosecutors in the end. Lex Fridman keeps pushing back on that view of loyalty.

## Key points

- **From whiteout to fraud mill.** Cox says his manager at Eagle Lending in Tampa told him to white out a borrower's 30-day late rent. Once he ran his own brokerage, he estimates 60-70% of its loans involved fraud.
- **Synthetic people.** He used children's Social Security numbers and later talked the Social Security office into issuing new numbers. Secured cards got these "phantom borrowers" credit scores near 700 within six months. He named them after Reservoir Dogs characters, a choice he now calls stupid.
- **Fake comparables.** In Ybor City he bought houses for about $50,000, recorded the sales at $200,000 to create comparable sales, then had synthetic borrowers refinance. The FBI counted 109 houses and about $11.5 million.
- **On the run.** After he fled in 2006, he says he held over 50 identities and 27 state-issued driver's licenses from seven states, many built from details he got by "surveying" homeless men. Banks such as Washington Mutual often took repayment rather than call the FBI.
- **Caught and cooperating.** Cox was arrested in Nashville after a friend of his girlfriend called the Secret Service, and was sentenced to 26 years and four months. Cooperation, plus filings by fellow inmate Frank Amodeo, cut 12 years off.
- **Regret.** He says he would give it all up for an ordinary life. His son will not speak to him, and he got out in time to spend his mother's last year and a half with her.
