## Thesis

Neil Adams, a 1981 world champion, two-time Olympic silver medallist and now the "voice of judo", argues that a great champion shows himself in defeat, not victory. He traces how his two Olympic final losses haunted him, fed a period of heavy drinking, and later shaped the coach and commentator he became. He also argues that correct technique, a cooperative training partner and a lot of randori matter more than drills done for the clipboard.

## Key points

- **1980 and 1984 finals.** Adams says dropping to the 71 kg category for Moscow was his worst decision. With poor nutrition and an eight-hour wait before the final, he lost to Ezio Gamba on a split decision. In Los Angeles he was favourite, but arrived tired and was thrown by Frank Wieneke's drop seoi-nage while leading.
- **Winning in 1981.** At 78 kg, with no weight cut, he won the world title by armlock on a Japanese opponent. He argues judo groundwork is won in the quick transition from standing, unlike jiu-jitsu, which has more time to build.
- **Losing and drinking.** Adams describes being a "functioning drunk" who still trained most days, a recurring dream about the 1984 final, and quitting on the spot after a 20 December incident while working for Belgian judo.
- **Learning from defeat.** After being caught in a triangle in 1978 he vowed never to lose on the ground again, and says he never did. In Japan he kept going back to a dojo to beat "Gold Tooth", an older student who had thrown him repeatedly when he was 16.
- **Greats.** He calls Teddy Riner the greatest judo winner of all time, but thinks Yamashita would beat him, and notes that Nomura never lost an Olympic fight.
- **Training.** Adams trained twice a day, with a separate supervised skills session. He says Japanese players do 50-60 randori a week and that many countries now do too little. He coached Jimmy Pedro, and says his juji-gatame details passed on to Kayla Harrison and Ronda Rousey.
