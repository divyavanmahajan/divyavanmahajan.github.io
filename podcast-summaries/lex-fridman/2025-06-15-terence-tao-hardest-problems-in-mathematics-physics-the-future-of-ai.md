## Thesis

Fields Medallist Terence Tao takes Lex Fridman through the hardest open problems he has worked on, from Navier-Stokes blowup to the twin prime conjecture. A recurring idea is that you cannot rule out a "conspiracy" that only special cases trigger. Tao argues that formal proof assistants like Lean, combined with AI tools, will enable large-scale, trustless collaboration and more experimental mathematics, though current AI still lacks a sense of when a proof has gone wrong.

## Key points

- **Navier-Stokes and a water computer.** Tao's 2016 paper engineered finite-time blowup in an averaged version of the equations, which rules out some proof approaches. He describes a speculative route to real blowup: a self-replicating "fluid computer", inspired by Conway's Game of Life.
- **Cheating strategically.** His working method is to switch off nine of ten difficulties, solve the one that remains, then combine. He compares this to Hong Kong action films where the hero fights one goon at a time.
- **Lean and crowdsourcing.** Formalising a proof takes about 10 times as long as writing it, but when a constant improved from 12 to 11 the Lean proof was updated in a day or two. His Equational Theories Project checked about 22 million implications among some 4,000 algebraic laws with about 50 contributors, and all but two are settled.
- **AI's limits.** Tao says AlphaProof's silver-medal IMO result needed about three days of Google server time per problem. He finds LLM proofs can look flawless but hide subtle, basic errors, and that literature searches often return mostly hallucinated papers. He predicts research-level human-AI collaborations by 2026.
- **Primes and conspiracies.** The twin prime conjecture is hard because deleting a tiny fraction of primes could remove every twin. Known results show infinitely many prime pairs at most 246 apart, but the "parity barrier" blocks further progress. His Collatz result shows that almost all starting numbers drift down.
- **Recognition.** Tao discusses Perelman turning down the Fields Medal, says the medal made him "part of the establishment", and warns against crediting only the person who completes the last step.
