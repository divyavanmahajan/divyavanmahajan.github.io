## Thesis

Jed Buchwald, a historian and philosopher of science at Caltech and a former research assistant to Thomas Kuhn, tells Lex Fridman that paradigm shifts happen, but not as neatly as Kuhn described. In his view a new theory takes over mainly because it gives scientists the tools to make new devices and findings, not because the old one fails outright. He uses Isaac Newton's life and work to show how experiment, mathematics, rivalry and personality shaped that progress.

## Key points

- **Disagreeing with Kuhn.** Buchwald says the wave theory of light, developed by Thomas Young and Augustin Fresnel, did not win because Newton's particle theory could not explain diffraction. It won because its mathematics let younger physicists generate new effects and devices, for example with polarization.
- **Not a realist.** Buchwald calls himself a materialist but not a realist in the philosophers' sense. He disagrees with Steven Weinberg that physics has reached the depths of reality, and argues that what we know always comes through our senses and instruments.
- **Newton's prism.** Buchwald describes how the young Newton calculated the angle at which a prism should keep the sun's image circular, found a long image instead, and concluded that sunlight contains different kinds of light. Newton set the angle by turning the prism in his hand, not with a measuring device.
- **Perception and instruments.** Buchwald says Robert Hooke's eye test was right about static vision but wrong to doubt Hevelius's star catalogue, because the eye judges motion much better. Halley confirmed Hevelius's accuracy.
- **Rivalry drives work.** Buchwald covers the disputes between Newton, Hooke and Leibniz over credit, and how Arago backed Fresnel to undercut Biot. He says competition is usually good for science, even when it is painful for the people involved.
- **Data, alchemy and religion.** Buchwald notes that averaging repeated measurements came only after Newton's time; Huygens published the single value he trusted most. Newton spent more of the late 1670s on alchemy than on anything else, and held deeply religious but anti-Trinitarian views.
