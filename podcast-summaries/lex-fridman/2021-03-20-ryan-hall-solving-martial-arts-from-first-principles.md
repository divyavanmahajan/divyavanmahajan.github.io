## Thesis

BJJ black belt and UFC fighter Ryan Hall argues that most practitioners are "end users" of systems they do not understand, which makes them predictable. He says that understanding how games are structured, and repeatedly asking why, lets a fighter set traps where every choice the opponent makes still leads to a loss. Lex Fridman links the idea to mechanism design in game theory.

## Key points

- **The illusion of choice.** Hall demonstrates a finger-picking game in which every path ends on the middle finger, and says good strategy means asking the right questions so the opponent has no correct answer. He contrasts this with fighters winning a run of coin-flip exchanges and building a story of control around luck, a point he credits to Firas Zahabi.
- **Skills before wins.** Hall says trading damage to land shots is a bad bet, and points to Floyd Mayweather, Stephen Thompson and teammate Tang Le as fighters who do not get hit. He traces his MMA path from a 2006 loss as a blue belt, through wins over Gray Maynard, BJ Penn and Darren Elkins, to a hip flexor tear before a planned bout with Dan Ige.
- **Junk in jiu-jitsu.** Hall calls the shrimp a movement he would no longer teach, questions closed guard and the guard itself, and says leg locks such as his 50/50 heel hook exposed a large blind spot. He describes his seatbelt-versus-"Merkel" back control experiments as an example of reversing an accepted position.
- **Learning to learn.** Training under Marcelo Garcia taught Hall that understanding and performance are separate; Josh Waitzkin told him he was missing Garcia's genius. Hall advises asking "why" repeatedly while being a partner people want to train with.
- **Free will, society and AI.** Hall favours "facilitative beliefs" such as belief in free will, and leans libertarian; Fridman argues individuals also owe empathy to others. Both discuss how like-and-share incentives make social media divisive. On AI, Hall worries about extreme power differentials, while Fridman says greater power need not mean hostility and points to value-alignment research.
