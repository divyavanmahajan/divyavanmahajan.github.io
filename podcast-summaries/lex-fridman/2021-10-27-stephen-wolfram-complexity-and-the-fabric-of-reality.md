## Thesis

Stephen Wolfram tells Lex Fridman that complexity in nature comes from simple programs, and that the Wolfram Physics Project models the universe as a rewritten hypergraph of "atoms of space". His central claim is that the laws we observe, from general relativity to quantum mechanics, follow from what kind of observer we are: computationally bounded, with a single thread of experience. He extends this "multicomputation" idea to mathematics, chemistry, immunology and economics.

## Key points

- **Complexity from simple rules.** Wolfram recounts finding around 1980 that cellular automata such as Rule 30 produce complicated behaviour from one black cell, leading to his idea of computational irreducibility: you cannot jump ahead of the computation.
- **Space, time and quantum mechanics.** In the model, space is a network of perhaps 10^400 atoms of space with an elementary length around 10^-100 metres, and time is the irreducible updating of that network. Wolfram explains time dilation as a moving object spending computation on recreating itself rather than ticking. He suggests dimension fluctuations in the early universe or rapidly rotating black holes as possible tests.
- **Consciousness as a step down.** Wolfram defines intelligence as computational sophistication and calls consciousness a narrower thing, limited by computational boundedness and a single thread of time. Fridman questions whether consciousness could be more fundamental.
- **The ruliad and why the universe exists.** Wolfram argues the universe runs all possible computational rules; this entangled object, the ruliad, is a necessary formal object like 2+2=4, and observers occupy a place within it. Fridman presses on what lies outside it, and Wolfram admits parts of the argument may be missing.
- **Meta-mathematics and applications.** He compares axioms to molecules beneath the "fluid" mathematics that mathematicians work in, and describes using the same ideas for molecular computing (including computing primes in the Emerald Cloud Lab), immune system networks, and a distributed, multi-ledger alternative to blockchain. He also mentions cellular automaton NFTs on Cardano.
- **Ruliology.** Looking back at the roughly thousand complexity institutes since he helped seed the field, Wolfram says applications absorbed the pure science, and proposes "ruliology", the basic science of what simple rules do.
