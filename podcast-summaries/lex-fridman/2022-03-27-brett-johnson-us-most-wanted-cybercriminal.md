## Thesis

Brett Johnson, who built the ShadowCrew forum and was placed on the US Most Wanted list in 2006, tells Lex Fridman that cybercrime is mostly social engineering built on trust, both between criminals and between victims and their technology. He traces his crimes to a childhood of abuse and family fraud in Eastern Kentucky, but insists the adult choices were his. He credits his recovery to prison therapy and to people who believed in him.

## Key points

- **Trust is the product.** Johnson says successful cybercrime needs three roles (gathering data, committing the crime, cashing out) that one person rarely covers. ShadowCrew's real innovation, he argues, was giving criminals reviews, vouching and escrow so they could trust each other.
- **Low sophistication, high manipulation.** He cites the figure that 90% of attacks use known exploits, and describes phishing around 200,000 E-Trade accounts and how business email compromise uses look-alike Unicode domains. He treats SolarWinds as the exception: catastrophic because attackers could read source code.
- **The crimes themselves.** Johnson describes a fake Beanie Baby sale, reselling satellite TV cards, and filing fraudulent tax returns for dead people from the California Death Index, which he says earned $160,000 a week. He admits he did not care about his victims.
- **Collapse and capture.** Albert Gonzalez, arrested at an ATM, helped the Secret Service run a VPN that led to 33 arrests in six countries in 2004. Johnson was arrested in 2005, kept committing tax fraud while working as a Secret Service informant, fled, and was caught in Orlando.
- **Prison and change.** He describes solitary confinement, prison riots and violence at Big Spring, Texas, and says a nine-month cognitive behavioural therapy program and two and a half years of reflection made him accept responsibility. After reoffending once, he contacted FBI agent Keith Mularski, who helped him into consulting work, including for Microsoft.
- **Advice for defenders.** Johnson urges companies to report and prosecute fraud, share information as criminals do, fix known vulnerabilities and make attacks too costly. He also criticises ID.me's role in government identity checks on privacy grounds.
