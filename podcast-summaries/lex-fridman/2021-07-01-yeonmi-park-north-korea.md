## Thesis

Yeonmi Park, a North Korean defector and author of In Order to Live, tells Lex Fridman about growing up believing she lived in a socialist paradise, escaping to China at 13 and rebuilding her life. Her central argument is that political systems, not culture or intelligence, separate North and South Korea, and that the outside world, including the US, is choosing not to act. Fridman shares her concern about the regime's cruelty but pushes back on casting China as an enemy, favouring appeals to the Chinese people.

## Key points

- **Life without concepts.** Park says North Korea had no internet, no 24-hour electricity and no words for fashion, romantic love, human rights or stress. She describes reading Animal Farm in South Korea as the moment she understood what had happened to her country.
- **Escape and trafficking.** Park says she and her mother were sold to China in 2007, and she credits the trafficker with saving her life. She says she felt numb in China, and that healing began when her son was born.
- **Caste and famine as policy.** Park describes the songbun system of three categories split into about 50 classes, and argues that after Soviet aid ended in 1991 the regime chose to keep Pyongyang fed while starving the countryside. She says the Sunshine Policy's money was used to build nuclear weapons.
- **Who is responsible.** Park holds the Chinese Communist Party largely accountable for sustaining Kim Jong-un, and calls Kim, who was educated in Switzerland, pure evil. Fridman argues that demonising China, as in the Cold War, makes leaders harden rather than reform.
- **Freedom and speech in America.** Park says she self-censored at Columbia and sees echoes of North Korean collective guilt in US identity politics. Fridman calls it a pendulum that swings back over time.
- **Personal risk.** Park says South Korean intelligence told her at 21 that she was on Kim Jong-un's kill list, and that Penguin verified her book with recorded witness accounts after North Korean threats.
