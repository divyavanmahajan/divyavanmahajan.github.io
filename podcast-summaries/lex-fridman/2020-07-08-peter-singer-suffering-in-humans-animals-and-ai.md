## Thesis

Peter Singer, Princeton bioethicist and author of Animal Liberation, tells Lex Fridman that suffering is a conscious state we would rather not be in, and that its moral weight does not depend on the species of whoever suffers. The same test applies to machines: robots deserve rights only if they become conscious. Fridman, who eats a mostly carnivore diet and has programmed Roombas to scream, pushes back on whether displayed suffering might be enough.

## Key points

- **Defining suffering.** Singer separates objective suffering (constant hunger, cold, pain) from relative forms such as boredom in affluent societies, and says the objective forms are more severe and should come first.
- **Capacity for evil.** Drawing on losing three of his four grandparents in the Holocaust, Singer estimates fewer than 10% of people could truly say they would not have taken part in Nazi crimes, and admits he cannot be sure he would have resisted.
- **Robots and consciousness.** Singer says current AI, such as Google Maps, has no subjective experience. Fridman argues that a convincing display of pain may make suffering real; Singer likens this to Gilbert Ryle's behaviourism, which he says few now accept.
- **Speciesism.** Singer credits the term to Richard Ryder and frames it as a bias analogous to racism and sexism, behind factory farming and non-essential animal research. Under uncertainty, he gives the benefit of the doubt to octopuses and, to a degree, insects.
- **Utilitarianism and AI risk.** Singer holds that extreme suffering sits further below neutral than bliss sits above it. He supports some research on AI control but questions treating extinction risk as infinite, noting even Nick Bostrom's estimates are vast but finite.
- **Effective giving.** The Life You Can Save, free online, proposes a progressive donation scale from 1% up to 33 and a third percent of income; Singer also points to 80,000 Hours for career choices and cites research that givers are more satisfied with their lives.
