## Thesis

Ginni Rometty, CEO of IBM from 2012 to 2020, tells Lex Fridman that the hard part of running a large company is changing it: deciding what must endure and changing how work gets done. Drawing on her book "Good Power", she argues that power is needed to do good, and that leaders should be judged on how they achieve results, not only on what they achieve.

## Key points

- **Change the how, not just the what.** Rometty says she cut management layers by about half and trained hundreds of thousands of employees in agile working. When she took over, only two in 10 employees had skills for the future.
- **Skills first.** After a high school and community college programme produced hires IBM could not find elsewhere, she concluded that "aptitude does not equal access". She now values willingness to learn above all, and co-chairs OneTen, which aims to bring 1 million Black employees into the middle class without requiring college degrees.
- **Long-term trade-offs.** Rometty describes 22 quarters of revenue decline as the largest reinvention in IBM's history: 50% of the portfolio changed, $10 billion of businesses divested, chip manufacturing separated from R&D, and Red Hat bought for $34 billion as the basis for hybrid cloud.
- **PwC Consulting.** She led the 2002 purchase of PwC's consulting arm for $3.5 billion, which she says grew to $19.5 billion in revenue and $2.7 billion in profit. Her negotiation rule is to leave both sides slightly unsatisfied rather than push too far.
- **Watson and AI ethics.** Rometty says Watson's main obstacles were people, trust and processes rather than the technology, and that starting with very hard problems in healthcare was a lesson learned. She favours regulating uses of AI rather than the technology, and transparency about who trained a model and with what data.
- **Personal roots.** She credits her mother, grandmother and great-grandmother for her work ethic, and her husband for the advice "growth and comfort never coexist".
