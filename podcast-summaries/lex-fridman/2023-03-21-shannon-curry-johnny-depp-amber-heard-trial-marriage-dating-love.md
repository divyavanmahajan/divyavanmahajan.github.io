## Thesis

Clinical and forensic psychologist Shannon Curry tells Lex Fridman that early romantic infatuation is largely chemistry that fades, but that long-term passion is achievable through a set of skills most people were never taught. She draws on the Gottman Method for couples and on Ty Tashiro's research for choosing a partner, then describes her role as an expert witness in the Johnny Depp and Amber Heard trial and her work with veterans.

## Key points

- **The 5-to-1 ratio.** Curry cites the Gottmans' finding that satisfied couples have roughly five positive interactions for each negative one, mostly small responses to a partner's "bids" for connection rather than grand gestures.
- **Four horsemen.** She describes criticism, defensiveness, stonewalling and contempt as predictors of divorce, calling contempt the strongest; flooding above about 100 beats per minute shuts down thoughtful reasoning.
- **Choosing a partner.** Citing Tashiro, Curry says only about 35% of married people are happy, and that happy couples rate partners high in conscientiousness, low in neuroticism and moderate in adventurousness.
- **Infidelity and honesty.** Curry's personal view, which she separates from her clinical one, is that someone who has ended an affair should carry the guilt rather than disclose it; she notes her husband puts truth first.
- **The trial testimony.** Curry explains she was court-ordered to evaluate one party and give diagnostic impressions; she says her findings supported borderline and histrionic personality disorder diagnoses for Heard, that the MMPI-2 (567 yes/no questions) is one piece of evidence, and that the court did not allow collateral interviews.
- **PTSD in veterans.** She says only about 14% of people exposed to severe trauma develop PTSD, and criticises outsourced one-hour assessments for Vietnam-era veterans as too short.
