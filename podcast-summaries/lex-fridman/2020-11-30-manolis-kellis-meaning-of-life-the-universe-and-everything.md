## Thesis

For episode 142, Lex Fridman and MIT professor Manolis Kellis take on the meaning of life, after a long opening on the songs that shaped Kellis. Kellis argues that searching for meaning is the meaning, and that humans are unusual because they can perceive every layer of reality, from atoms to ecosystems. He sees recent human progress as driven by shared cultural "software" running on brain "hardware" that has barely changed.

## Key points

- **Music before language.** Kellis walks through Greek, French and English songs (Sting, Pink Floyd, Leonard Cohen, Alison Krauss's "The Lucky One") as lessons in how to live. He argues that ritual, dance and song came before language and laid the groundwork for it, and that art is a requirement for cognition, not a distraction from it.
- **Why 42.** Kellis lists the coincidences he collected for his 42nd birthday, from the rainbow angle to 42 in binary and the 42-line Bible, while admitting the number was picked at random.
- **Hardware versus software.** Kellis says the human brain has hardly changed in about 70,000 years. Progress came from language, printing and the sharing of knowledge, which he illustrates with the coronavirus genome being published in January and a vaccine about 90% effective arriving within roughly ten months. He is optimistic about the asteroid Apophis in 2068.
- **Seeing the layers.** A lion or a bat works within one layer of reality, Kellis argues, while humans understand the whole stack, much as an AI could become aware of the hardware it runs on.
- **Scaling and AI.** Kellis notes that humans share an architecture with great apes, only with much more of it, and compares this to GPT-3 versus GPT-2. Fridman observes that many AI researchers resist the idea that scaling alone could reach general intelligence. Kellis is sceptical of Neuralink-style brain-to-brain transfer, arguing that conversation re-encodes ideas better.
- **Legacy.** Kellis says he lives in the present but sees a legacy in his lectures, free online since 2001, his academic "family" of students and his papers. The episode ends with two of his poems.
