## Thesis

Framed as a debate, the conversation finds economist Bjørn Lomborg and journalist Andrew Revkin largely agreeing: both say warming is real and human-caused, but that end-of-the-world framing skews policy. Lomborg argues from cost-benefit numbers that innovation and poverty reduction beat expensive emission cuts; Revkin argues that most disaster losses come from where and how people build, so reducing vulnerability offers the quickest action.

## Key points

- **Vulnerability over CO2.** Revkin cites Pakistan's growth from 40 million to 225 million people living in the Indus floodplain, and Lomborg cites Florida's housing growth since 1940, as reasons damage has risen. Both point to zoning, insurance and warning systems as levers that work faster than emission cuts.
- **Hurricanes.** Revkin describes paleotempestology, which shows big storms recurring over centuries, and research linking the Atlantic "hurricane drought" to European smog. Lomborg says storms may become slightly fewer but stronger, while damage as a share of GDP is falling.
- **Costs of climate policy.** Lomborg estimates the EU's 2030 target at about a trillion dollars a year, returning roughly 30 cents per dollar, against about $43 per dollar for tuberculosis treatment and $45 for early-childhood nutrition. Lomborg says climate innovation returns about $11 per dollar.
- **Innovation and nuclear.** Lomborg says fourth-generation nuclear or other technology that becomes cheaper than fossil fuels is the only realistic fix. Revkin adds production scale, social innovation such as LPG schemes in Nairobi and India, and regulatory reform for small modular reactors.
- **Media and narrative.** Revkin describes the New York Times "front page thought" and "narrative capture", and traces the "12 years" meme to the spin around the 2018 IPCC report. Fridman pushes back with Bob Ward's criticism that Lomborg cherry-picks models; Lomborg says he uses averages from the Stanford Energy Modeling Forum.
- **Politics and cooperation.** Revkin cites Yale data showing no red-blue divide on clean-energy innovation, and Peter Coleman's case for cooperation over agreement.
