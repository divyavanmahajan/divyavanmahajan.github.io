## Thesis

Hikaru Nakamura, the world's top-ranked blitz player and a leading chess streamer, tells Lex Fridman that results among elite players are driven as much by psychology as by preparation. He traces his own swings in form to confidence and money pressure, argues that computers have changed how the game is played, and calls Magnus Carlsen the greatest player because he has no clear weakness.

## Key points

- **The 2010 private match.** Nakamura calls his 40-game private blitz match with Carlsen in Moscow, which he lost 24.5 to 15.5, a competitive mistake: it showed Carlsen his weak openings, and Carlsen's saves of drawn end games made him seem "superhuman".
- **"I literally don't care."** Nakamura says that before the pandemic all his income came from playing, and only the top 20 to 30 players make a living. Earning from streaming took that pressure off and let him play more freely.
- **Computers and style.** Engines show that small advantages rarely win, Nakamura says, so players now press with white and play solid with black. He expects a solved game would be a draw and finds beauty in engine moves he can't explain.
- **Niemann controversy.** Nakamura says hard evidence of over-the-board cheating would need someone caught in the act. He is undecided, expects results over 6 to 12 months to settle it, and puts the main blame on chess.com rather than Carlsen. He says he is an independent contractor there.
- **Greatest of all time.** Nakamura picks Carlsen for having the highest rating ever, credits Kasparov's 20-plus years at number one, and excludes Fischer for lack of longevity. He names Levon Aronian, not himself, as the best player never to reach a title match.
- **Blitz, streaming and advice.** Nakamura puts his blitz strength down to intuition from over 300,000 online games and rarely blundering in time trouble. He credits fans for his streaming motivation and tells young people to pursue their passion.
