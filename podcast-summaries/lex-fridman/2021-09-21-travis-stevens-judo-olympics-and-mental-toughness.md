## Thesis

Travis Stevens, the 2016 Olympic judo silver medallist and now Lex Fridman's judo coach, describes a career built less on perfect technique than on strategy and the refusal to dwell on what he cannot change. He traces how a disputed 2012 semi-final loss nearly made him quit, and how injuries, brutal weight cuts and a shift in mindset led to the Rio medal. Fridman, who took up judo after watching Stevens in 2008, opens with a criticism of the IOC for keeping Olympic footage private.

## Key points

- **Strategist first.** Stevens calls himself aggressive but above all a strategist who sees only his opponents' faults. He sorts players into about 20 types, such as those who panic late in a match.
- **Weight cutting.** Stevens walked around at 88-89 kg and fought at 81 kg. He describes three-day cuts, including one in Sochi after the 2015 Pan Am Games, starting at almost 94 kg with lost luggage and a sauna he had to reserve. He never missed weight, but warns young athletes not to take pride in big cuts.
- **Injuries.** He lists nine broken ribs at one nationals, five herniated discs in his neck, a fractured back and a 2015 concussion, plus a leg infection that put him in hospital for seven days less than a year before Rio.
- **London 2012.** Fridman and Stevens review the semi-final against Ole Bischof: eight minutes, no penalties, and a unanimous referee decision against Stevens. Stevens says he was angry for years and was suspended once for his conduct towards referees.
- **Road to Rio.** After trying to quit twice, Stevens asked coach Jimmy Pedro to send him to Tokai in Japan for three weeks of 14 rounds a day, which he did with an injured hand. In Rio he beat the Georgian world number one with a choke he says he drilled for two years.
- **Visualisation and identity.** With a sports psychologist he shifted from picturing throws to rehearsing the physical sensations of a match. He says he avoided post-Olympic depression because he never identified as an Olympian.
