## Thesis

Nic Carter, partner at Castle Island Ventures and co-founder of Coin Metrics, tells Lex Fridman that Bitcoin is not a neutral payment rail but an encoding of values: non-discretionary monetary policy, property rights, censorship and seizure resistance, and leaderless governance. He argues that the small-block side was right in the blocksize wars, because Bitcoin should be a slow, high-assurance settlement layer with faster payments built on top. Fridman, who opens with a long statement on online mockery, presses him on maximalist toxicity.

## Key points

- **Core values.** Carter says the fixed issuance schedule toward 21 million coins matters less for its numbers than for the fact that nobody can change it, ruling out bailouts or covert dilution. Running a full node (about 350 GB) lets anyone audit that no covert inflation occurred.
- **Blocksize wars.** Carter describes how the 1 MB limit Satoshi added in 2010 led to a conflict between big blockers wanting Visa-scale payments and small blockers worried that only data centres could run nodes. The big-block forks Bitcoin Cash and Bitcoin SV now trade, he says, below 1% of Bitcoin's value.
- **Layered scaling.** Carter compares Bitcoin to Fedwire, with Visa, PayPal and Lightning-style layers above; credit card payments are not final for 90 to 120 days. He calls Lightning a bar tab and Schnorr/Taproot an incremental gain for privacy and scaling.
- **Criticisms.** Using his 12-sided dice of stock critiques, Carter argues that mining buys stranded energy, such as curtailed power in four Chinese provinces, and that governance is split between node operators, developers and miners.
- **Ethereum and Dogecoin.** Carter sees Ethereum as a deliberately different trade-off that can be mutualistic with Bitcoin, but says the DAO bailout weakened its social contract. He warns that Dogecoin is unmaintained and not a store of wealth.
- **Writing and maximalism.** Carter wishes Bitcoiners were gentler but explains their defensiveness as a response to past co-option attempts. He says good writing requires humility rather than vanity.
