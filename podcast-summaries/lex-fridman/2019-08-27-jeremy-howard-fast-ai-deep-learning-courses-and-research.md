## Thesis

Jeremy Howard, founder of fast.ai and former Kaggle president, tells Lex Fridman that deep learning's real value comes when domain experts apply it to their own problems. He argues that techniques like transfer learning make state-of-the-art results possible with small datasets and a single GPU, and that the "only Google can do this" story discourages people from learning a useful tool.

## Key points

- **Why fast.ai exists.** Howard founded Enlitic, which he describes as the first deep learning company for medicine, but concluded he could not pick every domain himself. fast.ai aims to teach domain experts, many of whom cannot code, to do it themselves. The courses are free and the organisation has no revenue.
- **Research versus practice.** Howard says most deep learning research is minor incremental work, while practical topics like transfer learning and active learning are understudied. His one paper, ULMFiT with Sebastian Ruder, brought transfer learning to NLP and was published at ACL.
- **DAWNBench on a budget.** Howard and a few students topped Stanford's DAWNBench for CIFAR-10 against Google and Intel, then trained ImageNet by starting on 64x64 images before moving to 224x224. He credits Leslie Smith's "super convergence" learning-rate work and calls multi-GPU training largely a waste of time for iteration.
- **Languages and frameworks.** Howard praises J and APL, calls Python too slow to be hackable, and hopes Swift plus MLIR will fix this. fast.ai moved from Theano and TensorFlow to PyTorch; he is strongly critical of TensorFlow's codebase and calls Nvidia overpriced for lack of competition.
- **Learning and startups.** Howard says tenacity separates students who succeed, recommends fine-tuning lots of models on your own data, and uses Anki spaced repetition for Chinese. He self-funded FastMail and Optimal Decisions, both profitable within about six months, and finds VC pressure to grow scary.
- **Concerns.** Howard says he is "desperately concerned" about labour force displacement and argues data scientists must consider human oversight, feedback loops and appeals processes.
