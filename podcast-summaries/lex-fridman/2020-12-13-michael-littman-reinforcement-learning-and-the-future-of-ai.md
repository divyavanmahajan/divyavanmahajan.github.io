## Thesis

Michael Littman, a computer science professor at Brown, tells Lex Fridman that he is not persuaded by the runaway-superintelligence story, because building capable systems will teach us how to shape them. Drawing on decades in reinforcement learning, he stresses the skilled people behind breakthroughs and the limits of learning from text alone. Fridman is more open to the idea that exponential progress could surprise us.

## Key points

- **Existential risk.** Littman compares AGI to a 747: it will not assemble itself, so the process of building it will give us the knowledge to control it. He suggests Elon Musk's belief in the power of ideas makes the doom scenario feel natural to him. Fridman argues social media algorithms already steer human thinking.
- **History of reinforcement learning.** Littman recalls being given Rich Sutton's 1984 TD paper at Bellcore by Dave Ackley, and Sutton visiting to talk about Watkins' Q-learning, whose off-policy property he calls a revelation. He used the term "self-play" in his 1996 PhD dissertation, borrowing it from Gerry Tesauro's TD-Gammon work.
- **People behind the results.** Littman says his students repeatedly failed to reproduce TD-Gammon's success, and credits Tesauro and DeepMind's David Silver with unusual skill at making networks work. He was more impressed by AlphaGo than by AlphaZero, while Satinder Singh found the removal of human games the bigger leap.
- **Ceilings and the Bitter Lesson.** Littman thinks finite games have an optimal strategy, so self-play gains must flatten. On Sutton's Bitter Lesson, he notes chip development costs also double each generation and expects exponentials to become sigmoids.
- **Language models and driving.** Littman argues GPT-3-style models cannot master language without people pushing back at them in conversation. Teaching his son to drive showed him that driving is a social activity that requires a theory of other drivers' minds.
- **Books and meaning.** He recommends Douglas Rushkoff's Program or Be Programmed, Brian Christian's The Alignment Problem and Ted Chiang's Exhalation, and says his answer to the meaning of life is balance.
