## Thesis

Chris Mason, a Cornell professor and author of The Next 500 Years, tells Lex Fridman that awareness of extinction is a uniquely human trait, and that it gives us a duty to keep life going beyond Earth. He argues that, over roughly 500 years, data from astronaut studies, better spacecraft and engineered protection for the body could make Mars settlements and even a generation ship to another star possible. Fridman pushes the conversation towards AI companions, culture on Mars and the ethics of who goes.

## Key points

- **The NASA Twins Study.** Mason describes molecular profiling of Scott Kelly's 340 days in space: around 260 samples collected over three years. Coming back to gravity hit Kelly harder than going up. Most changes reversed within a year (99% of genes back to pre-flight levels), and lengthening telomeres turned up in all 59 other astronauts studied.
- **Protecting the body, not just the hull.** Mason proposes temporarily switching on the body's own repair systems before a mission, alongside shielding, and suggests about 2040 as a point when this could be tried in astronauts.
- **Mars is close, not ideal.** Mason says Mars scores only about 0.7 on the Earth similarity index. Settlers would probably live in lava tubes, and he expects true self-sufficiency to take several hundred years. He sees a multi-country "space race 2.0", noting China's stated goal of boots on Mars by 2033.
- **Commercial spaceflight lowers the barrier.** Mason says flying is now mostly a question of money: the Inspiration4 and Axiom crews volunteered for extensive science, and Axiom plans a free-flying private station by 2028. Fridman cites a $55 million price for the Axiom 1 trip to the ISS.
- **Engineering experiments for orbit.** Launch costs about $10,000 per kilogram, according to Mason, and any hardware, such as the first DNA sequencer flown in space, must pass destructive, fire and vibration testing.
- **AI and duty.** Mason says he is "matter agnostic" about cognition and hopes AI inherits the same duty to protect life. On HAL 9000, he suggests a system should consult other AIs when uncertain.
