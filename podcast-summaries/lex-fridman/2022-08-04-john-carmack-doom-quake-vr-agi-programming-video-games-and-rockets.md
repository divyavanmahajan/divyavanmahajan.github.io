## Thesis

Talking with Lex Fridman for over five hours, John Carmack argues that engineering should be driven by user value, and that his biggest results came from understanding the whole stack and giving up something that turned out not to matter. He applies this to id Software's games, VR at Oculus and Meta, and his new focus on AGI, which he believes will come from a handful of simple ideas.

## Key points

- **Programming practice.** Carmack still writes "C flavored" C++ for serious work and uses Python for ML. He calls garbage collection good for almost all programs, always runs his code under a debugger, and says running every static analysis tool over a codebase of a couple of million lines was humbling.
- **id Software techniques.** He describes adaptive tile refresh for smooth PC scrolling, the Commander Keen trilogy earning about $30,000 a month, ray casting and compiled scalers in Wolfenstein 3D, and BSP trees in Doom. He says Quake should have been split into two games: networking and a scripting language on the Doom engine first, then the full 3D engine.
- **VR lessons.** Carmack says he underestimated how much controllers matter. Beat Saber succeeded because it plays only to VR's strengths. He prefers cheaper, lighter headsets and calls Meta's reported $10 billion a year on Reality Labs inefficient, although Quest 2 is roughly the headset he hoped for.
- **Bets on the future.** He has a $10,000 bet against humans on Mars by 2030, which he sees as depending too much on Elon Musk. He has another $10,000 bet with Jeff Atwood that robotaxis will run in major cities by 2030. He also says cheap nuclear fission is held back by social, not technical, factors.
- **AGI.** Carmack gives 55-60% odds of "signs of life" by 2030. He expects early systems to need thousands of GPUs and cost about $1,000 an hour, rejects fast-takeoff fears, and thinks embodiment in robots is a handicap. He had just signed a term sheet to take investment for his AGI company.
