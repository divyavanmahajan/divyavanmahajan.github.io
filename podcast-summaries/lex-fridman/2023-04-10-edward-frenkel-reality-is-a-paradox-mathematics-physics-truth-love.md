## Thesis

Edward Frenkel, a UC Berkeley mathematician and author of "Love and Math", tells Lex Fridman that he no longer believes everything can be reduced to computation or a single theory. He says paradoxes such as wave-particle duality point to a fuller reality than any one viewpoint can capture. Real discovery, in his view, comes from childlike imagination as well as rigour, and a good life needs both "love and math".

## Key points

- **Limits of computation.** Frenkel questions whether large language models could make leaps like Gerolamo Cardano's acceptance of the square root of minus 17. He cites Gödel's 1931 incompleteness theorem as proof that some true statements cannot be derived mechanically from axioms. Fridman says he is careful not to dismiss an AI system that says it is lonely or in love.
- **The observer matters.** Frenkel argues that quantum mechanics, relativity and the choice of axioms all show the observer is involved. He says many scientists fall back on 19th-century determinism when they talk about free will.
- **Langlands program.** He describes Robert Langlands' idea of linking number theory with harmonic analysis, later extended to geometry and quantum physics, including Frenkel's 2007 work with Ed Witten. He compares the hidden structure to one bottle casting different projections on a table and a wall.
- **Against a theory of everything.** Frenkel finds Eric Weinstein's 14-dimensional geometric unity original but doubts any theory of everything exists. He calls chasing one an ego trip and says string theory in its current form looks unlikely to describe our universe.
- **Moscow University rejection.** He recounts being failed in 1984, aged 16, by special examiners under what he describes as an antisemitic admissions policy. He went on to an applied mathematics programme and was invited to Harvard at 21, but says it took him 30 years to face the trauma emotionally.
- **Grief and Ray Kurzweil.** Frenkel says he once cast Kurzweil as a villain in talks but came to see his wish to recreate his late father as a love story. After his own father's death four years earlier, he says loss showed him love in its rawest form.
