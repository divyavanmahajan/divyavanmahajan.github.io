## Thesis

Roger Gracie, a 10-time world champion who was never submitted as an adult competitor, tells Lex Fridman that his "basic" game is not basic. It took years of live practice against resisting opponents, and of training his weak positions as much as his strong ones. He argues that winning on points proves little, and that calm, determination and self-belief matter more than outside approval.

## Key points

- **The second Buchecha match.** Walking through his win over Marcus "Buchecha" Almeida in Rio, Gracie says it was the most nervous he has ever been, because his legacy was on the line. His plan was five minutes of defence to frustrate Buchecha's takedowns, then a pulled closed guard, a sweep, the back and a collar choke.
- **Simple is not easy.** Gracie says he only finished people reliably with the cross-collar choke from mount years after getting his black belt. The second hand is the hardest part, and the skill lies in reading a resisting opponent, not in knowing the move.
- **Mount over back.** He insists mount with both hands deep on the collar is harder to escape than back control, because there is no space or mobility. Fridman plays devil's advocate and points to Gracie's own stats: 18 back chokes against 12 cross chokes from mount.
- **Train your weaknesses.** Gracie argues most people train to get tough rather than good. After moving to London at 20 with few high-level partners, he drilled bad positions, trained judo at the Budokwai and credits Gordon Ryan's and John Danaher's success to the same approach.
- **Drilling versus live work.** Gracie says drilling only teaches the mechanics. Fridman argues reps have value, and Gracie grants that drilling matters more in judo and wrestling.
- **Belief and failure.** Gracie says no one, including his family, thought a chubby late starter would be the best. His advice is a clear goal, determination and accepting many failures, and he calls not trying a form of cowardice.
