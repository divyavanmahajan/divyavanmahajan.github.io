## Thesis

Nathalie Cabrol, who directs the Carl Sagan Center at the SETI Institute, tells Lex Fridman that understanding the nature of life, which she frames as the gathering, storing and exchange of information, would give scientists a universal biosignature. She expects simple life to be common in the universe and studies high-altitude volcanic lakes in the Andes as stand-ins for early Mars. Fridman and Cabrol disagree, politely, on AI, technology and public fascination with UFOs.

## Key points

- **Mars then and now.** Cabrol traces the field from Mariner 4's disappointing images and Mariner 9's views of valleys and volcanoes to the confusing Viking results. She thinks life was as likely to start on Mars as on Earth, and that it may survive in the subsurface not far below the surface.
- **Nature over origin.** Citing Schrödinger and Jeremy England, Cabrol says there are 123 definitions of life. She compares how life is built, from atoms to DNA, with how language is built, from letters to sentences.
- **AI and technology.** Fridman points to AlphaZero and AlphaFold as signs that AI may out-understand humans. Cabrol calls AI a very smart tool built by humans and worries more about unfiltered information on social media.
- **SETI and UFOs.** Cabrol says SETI searches for messages, not UFOs, and that UFO folklore has meant no government agency funds SETI; NASA funds technosignature work. Fridman argues scientists should use that public curiosity.
- **Andes expeditions.** At about 20,000 feet Cabrol free-dived, then used Navy SEAL rebreathers. She describes nearly blacking out during one dive, and a magnitude 7.8 earthquake followed by a sulphur cloud from the nearby Lascar volcano. Cyanobacteria there keep their UV protection switched on permanently.
- **Personal resilience.** Cabrol speaks about a suicide attempt as a teenager ("give tomorrow a chance") and about her husband, who died that August. She says humans are causing the loss of about 150 species a day.
