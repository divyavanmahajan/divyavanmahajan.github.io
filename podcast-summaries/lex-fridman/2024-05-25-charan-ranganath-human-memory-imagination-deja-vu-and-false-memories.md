## Thesis

Charan Ranganath, a UC Davis neuroscientist and author of Why We Remember, argues that memory exists to serve the present and the future, not to archive the past. The brain keeps a biased, economical sample of experience, captured at moments of surprise, and rebuilds memories each time they are recalled. That same reconstructive machinery powers imagination and also makes memory easy to distort.

## Key points

- **Forgetting is by design.** Ranganath points to Ebbinghaus, who lost about 60% of memorized material within a day. He says the goal should be remembering better, not more, and that a brain running on an estimated 12 to 20 watts is built to reuse information.
- **Memories form at event boundaries.** His lab finds that hippocampal activity spikes at moments of surprise or transition, such as walking into another room. That activity predicts how well people later remember a story, and it declines with age.
- **Errors drive learning.** Spacing out study helps long-term retention because recall in a new context strips away context-specific detail. Testing yourself (the testing effect, which his group modeled in PLOS Computational Biology) works because exposing the errors is, in his words, conceptually similar to backprop.
- **Deja vu is a partial match.** He cites Anne Cleary's virtual-reality studies, where a museum and an arcade shared the same layout under different skins. People felt intense familiarity without consciously spotting the match.
- **False memories and confessions.** Building on Elizabeth Loftus's misinformation research, Ranganath describes how repeated recall plus outside suggestion can produce vivid memories of events that never happened. His recipe for false confessions: stress, an authority figure and fed-in details.
- **What AI lacks.** Fridman asks about OpenAI adding memory. Ranganath frames the problem as Steve Grossberg's stability-plasticity dilemma, which humans solve by pairing episodic and semantic memory. He says he will not accept that AI replicates human intelligence until it makes the same characteristic mistakes people make.
