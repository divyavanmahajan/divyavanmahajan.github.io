## Thesis

Karl Deisseroth, a Stanford professor of bioengineering and psychiatry who still treats patients, tells Lex Fridman that psychiatric disorders are strongly genetic and biological, yet are still diagnosed only by talking to people. He argues that optogenetics, his method for controlling chosen brain cells with light, lets researchers find which circuits cause individual symptoms, even without a single unifying theory of the mind.

## Key points

- **Disorder as a spectrum.** Deisseroth says lifetime prevalence of a psychiatric disorder approaches 25%, that something counts as a disorder only when it disrupts social or occupational functioning, and that autism, anorexia and bipolar disorder correlate positively with intelligence and education at population level.
- **Optogenetics, step by step.** He describes putting light-sensitive proteins from algae into selected neurons, an idea Francis Crick wished for in 1999. The work moved from cultured neurons in 2005 to behaving mice in 2007, and by 2019 stimulating 20 to 25 cells could make a mouse act as if it had seen something. He cites Botond Roska's first human optogenetic therapy, which partially restored sight in a blind patient.
- **Depression's symptoms versus the syndrome.** In animals, Deisseroth says, motivation, reward and social interaction can each be turned up or down via specific projections; what remains unknown is why these symptoms cluster, the way heart failure explains both breathlessness and swollen feet. He points to the habenula as a target for reducing psychic pain.
- **Treatment and talk.** He combines medication, brain stimulation and talk therapy, recommending exercise, regular sleep and roughly 10 to 12 sessions of cognitive therapy for mild cases.
- **Autism and schizophrenia.** Deisseroth frames autism as difficulty with unpredictable, high bit-rate information rather than only a social problem. He says schizophrenia could be upwards of 80% genetic, affecting about 1% of people worldwide, and possibly a failure of the brain to recognise its own inner monologue.
- **Consciousness thought experiment.** He asks whether replaying every neuron's activity, even with the neurons spread across North America, would recreate an experience, and says the paradox shows something fundamental is still missing.
