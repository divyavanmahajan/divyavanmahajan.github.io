## Thesis

Neil Gershenfeld, director of MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms, tells Lex Fridman that computer science went wrong by treating software as separate from physics. He argues that communication and computation have had their digital revolutions, and fabrication is next: machines that assemble error-correcting building blocks, as the ribosome does, until assemblers can make assemblers.

## Key points

- **Turing and von Neumann's "mistake".** Gershenfeld says separating the head from the tape in Turing's machine, built into hardware through von Neumann's EDVAC memo, means computers spend much of their effort moving data between memory and processors. Both men, he notes, ended their careers studying how computation becomes physical form.
- **Digital materials.** By analogy with Lego and the 20 amino acids, his lab joins small carbon-fibre parts reversibly. He says this set a record for ultralight, high-modulus material and led to morphing aircraft with NASA and walking robots that build structures, including robots made from the parts they assemble.
- **The eight-order gap.** He puts a 100-petaflop supercomputer level with the brain at about 10^17 operations a second, but says a chip fab places about 10^10 transistors a second while biology places about 10^18 parts.
- **Fab Labs.** The MIT course How to Make Almost Anything grew into about 2,500 community labs in 125 countries, which he says double every 18 months. He describes a move from machines making parts to machines making machines, with super Fab Labs in places like Bhutan.
- **Risk.** Gershenfeld dismisses "gray goo" fears because biology already competes for the same resources, but says the chance of someone learning to make a virus worries him. His answer is to encourage openness rather than rely on command and control.
- **Ready, fire, aim.** He says most of his results came from failures: a failed shoplifting-tag project led to early NMR quantum computing, and a failed fluidic ribosome produced bubble logic.

## Quotes
