## Thesis

Judea Pearl, Turing Award winner and inventor of Bayesian networks, tells Lex Fridman that today's deep networks are essentially conditional probability estimators, and that looking only at data can never reveal "the strings behind the facts". In his view the human should supply a qualitative causal model of who listens to whom, and the machine then uses the do-calculus to derive quantitative answers. Fridman keeps pushing on how such models could be learned rather than hand-built.

## Key points

- **Representation before discovery.** Pearl insists the first question is how to represent causal knowledge and what can be inferred from it; learning or discovering models comes second. Every arrow in a causal graph only says an influence is possible, and the more arrows, the less can be identified from observational data alone.
- **The do-operator.** Pearl explains intervention as "surgery" on the model: cut the incoming arrows into x and ask for the probability of y. This lets you reason about experiments you cannot run, such as changing a person's blood pressure.
- **Counterfactuals as explanation.** Asking whether it was the aspirin that cured a headache, or the firing-squad case of what happens if rifleman A refuses to shoot, needs counterfactual reasoning, which Pearl links to responsibility, regret and free will. He calls the "fundamental law of counterfactuals" the legacy he hopes for.
- **Metaphor and familiarity.** Pearl describes metaphor as mapping an unfamiliar problem to a familiar one with stored answers, citing Eratosthenes measuring the Earth versus Babylonian astronomers who only fit curves.
- **AGI, ethics and consciousness.** Pearl argues an ethical machine needs a causal model of the people it affects, defines consciousness as "having a blueprint of your software", and says he is concerned that AI is a new, uncontrolled species.
- **Daniel Pearl.** Pearl talks about his son's murder in Pakistan, his Wall Street Journal article on the normalisation of evil, and his memory of Daniel's openness to strangers.
