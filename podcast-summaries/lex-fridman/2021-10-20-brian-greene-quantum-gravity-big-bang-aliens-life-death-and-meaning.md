## Thesis

Brian Greene, a theoretical physicist at Columbia and author of Until the End of Time, tells Lex Fridman that he accepts Bertrand Russell's bleak physics: the second law of thermodynamics means everything eventually decays. He reads the conclusion differently, though. Because the laws of physics carry no built-in purpose, he argues, people have to make their own meaning, and being short-lived ordered structures should prompt gratitude rather than despair.

## Key points

- **Consciousness.** Greene calls the hard problem among the deepest questions we face, but predicts that in a hundred or a thousand years people will see it as simply something that happens when particles come together. Fridman, drawing on robots he has built, agrees that the mystery starts to fade once machines report inner states.
- **String theory.** After 30 years in the field, Greene rejects the claim that string theory has fallen out of favour. He cites strong theoretical progress, for example on black holes, but no contact with experiment yet, so no Nobel Prize.
- **Extra dimensions and time.** Greene suggests the Large Hadron Collider could detect extra dimensions as missing energy after collisions. He says relativity clearly allows travel into the future, but most physicists doubt that wormholes permit travel into the past.
- **Free will.** In Greene's view every thought is particle motion governed by physical law, so traditional free will does not exist. What humans do have is freedom from the narrow range of behaviour available to rocks and bottles of water.
- **Big Bang and aliens.** Greene still favours inflationary cosmology, but less confidently than he did a few years ago, given critiques from Paul Steinhardt and others. On the Fermi paradox, he thinks the simplest explanation is that there are no advanced civilisations. Fridman believes there are many, but that humans are too limited to detect them.
- **Mortality.** Both cite Ernest Becker's The Denial of Death and terror management research. Fridman suggests intelligent machines may need a sense of mortality; Greene adds that a system that understands entropy would recognise its own finitude.
