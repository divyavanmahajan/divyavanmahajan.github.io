## Thesis

Anthropologist and historian Jack Weatherford argues that Genghis Khan, an abandoned outcast who built the largest contiguous empire in history, should be judged by the same standard as other conquerors, including modern states. In his view the conquest lasted about 20 years, while the institutions that followed, such as religious freedom, protected trade and merit-based promotion, are what let a nation of one million rule hundreds of millions.

## Key points

- **A childhood of betrayal.** Weatherford traces Temüjin's path through his mother's kidnapping, his father's poisoning, abandonment by his clan, enslavement in a wooden yoke and the kidnapping of his wife Börte, which he calls the defining moment of his life. He concludes that Temüjin learned early that family could not be trusted.
- **A small, adaptive army.** The army probably never exceeded 100,000, at most 110,000, all cavalry and organised in units of ten, with five horses per man. Weatherford credits Genghis Khan with learning from failure: a botched attempt to flood a Tangut city became a tactic used as far as Baghdad in 1258.
- **Terror with rules.** Cities that surrendered were spared and kept their local rulers; artisans were always spared; cities that surrendered and then rebelled were wiped out. Asked about an estimate of 40 million deaths, Weatherford says Americans should not condemn the Mongols while excusing their own wars.
- **Individual religious freedom.** Weatherford says Genghis Khan's first law for non-Mongols gave each person the right to choose a religion, an idea he says inspired Thomas Jefferson. Tax exemptions for religious figures, physicians and teachers, and protection of envoys (Kiev was destroyed in 1240 for killing one) came from the same practical approach.
- **Women and the Secret History.** Daughters ruled allied kingdoms and Toregene ruled the empire in her own name. Weatherford believes a passage in the Secret History about rewarding daughters was deliberately cut.
- **Kublai Khan and decline.** Kublai conquered the Song using trebuchets mounted on boats but failed twice to invade Japan. Weatherford says the plague, spread along the Mongol postal and trade network, helped bring down the system, and that the Mongols left China in 1368.
