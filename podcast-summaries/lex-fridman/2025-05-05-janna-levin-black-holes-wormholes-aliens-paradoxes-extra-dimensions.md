## Thesis

Theoretical physicist Janna Levin tells Lex Fridman that black holes are best understood as event horizons, empty regions of spacetime, rather than crushed stars. Because they are as featureless as fundamental particles, she argues, they are the main testing ground for quantum gravity. Her preferred resolution of Hawking's information paradox, ER = EPR, points to gravity emerging from quantum entanglement rather than being fundamental.

## Key points

- **History of an idea.** Karl Schwarzschild solved Einstein's equations from the World War I trenches as a pure thought experiment. Oppenheimer's 1939 paper with a student, published the day the Nazis advanced on Poland, argued that heavy stars collapse into black holes. John Wheeler popularised the term "black hole" in 1967 after years of disputing Oppenheimer.
- **Falling in.** A black hole of 10 solar masses is about 60 kilometres across. Inside, space and time swap roles, so the singularity sits in the infalling astronaut's future. For a stellar-mass black hole it arrives within microseconds.
- **Information paradox.** Hawking radiation looks thermal and carries no information, which conflicts with unitarity in quantum mechanics. Levin rejects information loss, fuzzballs, soft hair and the AMPS firewall. She favours ER = EPR (Maldacena and Susskind), which builds on holography.
- **LIGO.** The detector took about 50 years. It uses 4-kilometre arms to measure changes smaller than one ten-thousandth of a proton. The first signal arrived on 14 September 2015 from a collision more than 1.5 billion years ago. Levin recalls Ray Weiss fearing the project had led the country down the wrong path.
- **Wormholes, dark sectors, aliens.** Following Kip Thorne's work, wormholes need negative energy, which the Casimir effect shows is possible in principle. Levin hopes dark matter, dark energy and extra dimensions turn out to be linked. With likely more planets than stars in the Milky Way, she urges imagining alien life unlike us, such as jellyfish.
- **Turing, Gödel and Pioneer Works.** Her book links Gödel's incompleteness to Turing's uncomputable numbers and their tragic deaths. As chief science officer of Pioneer Works in Brooklyn, she treats science as culture, not outreach.
