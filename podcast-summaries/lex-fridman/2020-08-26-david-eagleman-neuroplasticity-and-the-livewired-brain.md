## Thesis

David Eagleman, a Stanford neuroscientist discussing his book Livewired, tells Lex Fridman that the hardware/software metaphor fails for the brain: it is "livewire", a system that physically reconfigures itself at many levels as it learns. Because the brain moulds itself around whatever data is relevant, Eagleman argues, new senses can be added without surgery, and AI will need something like caring about relevance before it matches human intelligence.

## Key points

- **Plasticity by region and data.** Eagleman notes children under about seven can have a hemisphere removed and still function. He argues each brain area hardens at a pace set by how stable its input is: the visual cortex sets early, while body-related areas stay malleable. Older memories are more stable because they sink into deeper layers, an idea he borrows from Stewart Brand's "pace layers".
- **Doubts about invasive BCI as a business.** Eagleman calls Neuralink valuable for patients with conditions such as Parkinson's or epilepsy, but doubts healthy people will accept open-head surgery to type faster. He points to cochlear and retinal implants as proof the brain learns to read signals that don't match its natural code.
- **Neosensory.** His company sells a $399 wristband that turns sound into vibration so deaf users learn to hear through their skin. He says it costs less than a tenth of a hearing aid, and he has tested infrared and ultraviolet input as "sensory expansion".
- **In-groups.** His lab found brains respond more to pain in a hand labelled with the viewer's own religion, and the bias appears even with coin-toss teams.
- **Law and psychiatry.** Through his Center for Science and Law, he argues for mental health and drug courts instead of one-size-fits-all incarceration.
- **GPT-3 and AI.** Eagleman calls GPT-3 a good impersonator whose best outputs are picked out by humans. He argues AI lacks relevance and a drive to survive, using the example of a wolf that adapts after losing a leg while the Mars rover Curiosity got stuck. Fridman is more open to scale surprising people.
