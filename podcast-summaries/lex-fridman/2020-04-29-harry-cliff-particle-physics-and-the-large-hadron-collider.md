## Thesis

Harry Cliff, a particle physicist at Cambridge working on the LHCb experiment, tells Lex Fridman that the Large Hadron Collider is best seen as a microscope for the vacuum: particles are ripples in invisible quantum fields, and colliding protons hard enough makes new fields ripple. The Higgs discovery completed the Standard Model, but Cliff argues the Higgs itself raises deep fine-tuning puzzles, and a decade of data has not yet revealed the new particles many expected.

## Key points

- **Size and magnets.** Cliff explains the 27 km ring, about 100 metres underground near Geneva, is sized by how strong a bending magnet can be built. Protons travel in bunches of around 100 billion, with 40 million bunch crossings per second.
- **A short history of the Standard Model.** He traces the path from the electron (1897, Cavendish) through the particle "zoo" of the 1960s, Gell-Mann and Zweig's quarks, and electroweak theory, whose W and Z bosons were found at CERN in 1983-84.
- **Why the Higgs is fishy.** Cliff says the Higgs field's value looks fine-tuned: naive calculations push it either to zero or to the Planck scale. Supersymmetry was the favoured fix, but no superpartners have appeared.
- **Footprints, not elephants.** LHCb studies beauty quarks, made in the billions, looking for subtle effects of unseen fields on their decays. Cliff reports intriguing anomalies there, not yet statistically conclusive, and describes matter-antimatter oscillations as a test of that symmetry.
- **Collaboration and the future.** Cliff notes the spokesperson of a 3,000-person experiment has no real power, so work proceeds by consensus. He describes the planned luminosity upgrade and the proposed 100 km Future Circular Collider, estimated at about 30 billion euros, with no data before roughly 2040.
- **Machine learning and communication.** Only about one ten-thousandth of collision data is kept; Cliff hopes machine learning on raw detector hits can improve triggers. He says explaining physics, including in his Royal Institution lectures, forces him to understand it better.
