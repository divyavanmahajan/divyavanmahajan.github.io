## Thesis

Craig Jones argues that submission grappling will only grow if athletes are paid properly and events are given away for free, the way comedians grew audiences with free specials. His CJI tournament, scheduled against ADCC, is both a protest and a test of that model. Between the jokes, he also describes what he saw on two trips to Ukraine's front lines.

## Key points

- **Why CJI exists.** Jones says a second-place ADCC finish earned him only $6,000, and that ADCC moved to the T-Mobile arena for about $2 million while CJI pays roughly $200,000 for Thomas & Mack. An anonymous friend funded a $3 million budget: $10,001 to every entrant, $1 million to each division winner.
- **Free and transparent.** CJI streams free on Meta, X and YouTube, runs as a nonprofit, and sends ticket profits to charities such as Tap Cancer Out, matched by Rich Byrne. Jones wants public view counts so athletes can use them in sponsorship talks.
- **Ripple effects.** He says ADCC equalised women's prize money after champion Ffion Davies joined CJI, and allegedly began paying show money to keep athletes.
- **The Alley.** CJI uses angled walls inspired by Karate Combat to end edge resets, plus five-minute rounds, MMA-style 10-point scoring and hard stalling penalties, with no prize for second.
- **Ukraine.** Jones got within 0.7 km of the front, toured shelled Kherson in armoured vehicles, felt a Patriot interception near his Kyiv hotel and visited mined Chernobyl. Soldiers told him a cheap FPV drone can destroy a multi-million-dollar tank; Fridman worries about autonomous drone swarms.
- **Training and trolling.** Jones advises beginners to pick safe partners and stay calm, and says known athletes should train privately because strangers at seminars come hunting for a win. Asked what gives him hope, he says that you can still make fun of anything as long as it's funny.
