## Thesis

S. James Gates Jr., a Brown University physicist working on supersymmetry and superstring theory, tells Lex Fridman that string theory is not yet a theory in the strict sense but a large, consistent collection of mathematical facts. He argues that, like Einstein's general relativity, it must eventually be confirmed by observation, though he expects that to take decades and a combination of evidence rather than one experiment.

## Key points

- **Supersymmetry as a filled pie.** Gates explains particles as two buckets, matter (fermions such as electrons and quarks) and force carriers (bosons such as photons and gluons). Supersymmetry fills the two empty quadrants with partners like photinos, selectrons and squarks; he wrote the first MIT physics PhD thesis on the idea in 1977.
- **Adinkras and error-correcting codes.** Gates says the graphs he devised with Michael Faux in 2005 to encode supersymmetric equations turned out to contain Hamming-style error-correcting codes. It took three years to convince other physicists the result was sound.
- **String theory's status.** He describes spin-offs such as weak-strong duality and holography, and notes that four-dimensional strings were found in the late 1980s, including by Gates and Warren Siegel.
- **Proving Einstein right.** His book with novelist Cathie Pelletier describes how war and weather blocked a 1914 eclipse measurement that would have contradicted Einstein's incorrect 1911 prediction. Einstein corrected it in 1915, and 1919 observations supported him.
- **Mars and spaceflight.** Gates is convinced no human will reach Mars by 2030, citing radiation and funding: Apollo used about 5% of US economic output, Mars is about a hundred times farther, and spending is a tenth as large. He guesses around 2090-2120.
- **Creativity and service.** He credits dreaming with solving problems, recalling a dream that unlocked integral calculus at MIT, and describes Barack Obama, whom he advised for seven years on PCAST, as an exceptionally fast learner.
