## Thesis

Paul Rosolie, founder of Junglekeepers, tells Lex Fridman that he protects the western Amazon in Peru's Madre de Dios through concrete, local work. That means turning the people who cut the forest into the people who guard it, and showing the jungle to the public as it really is. He sets this measurable fight for wildlife and habitat apart from the wider climate debate, where he says he is not qualified to judge.

## Key points

- **Converting extractors.** Rosolie says Junglekeepers offers loggers who earn about $15 a day destroying old trees $35 a day, plus a uniform, health insurance and security, to protect the forest instead. One gold miner whose barge the Peruvian navy blew up later built an ecotourism lodge.
- **Gold mining and mercury.** He describes lawless artisanal gold mining that has destroyed hundreds of thousands of acres. Because anacondas sit at the top of the food chain, his team took tissue samples to see whether they could track how mercury moves through the ecosystem.
- **The "Eaten Alive" lesson.** Rosolie says Discovery recut his anaconda project, changing his words and its message, and the backlash cost him scientific credibility. A viral 2019 Instagram video of the fires, later shared by Joe Rogan, brought in Lightspeed founder Dax Dasilva, whose funding now helps protect 50,000 acres.
- **Uncontacted tribes.** He links the tribes' hostility to the rubber boom and recounts fatal arrow attacks on loggers. He argues they should be left alone and respected, and he describes running from one group during a solo trip.
- **Hunting versus poaching.** Working with VETPAW in Africa, Rosolie separates poachers, who kill endangered animals for parts, from regulated hunting of non-endangered game, which he says funds reserves.
- **Humans and nature.** He rejects anti-human environmentalism and calls humans unique. He also rejects headlines calling the Amazon a "manmade garden", which he fears politicians could use to justify more clearing.
