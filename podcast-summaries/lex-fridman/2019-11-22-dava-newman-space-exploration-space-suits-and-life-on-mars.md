## Thesis

Dava Newman, MIT's Apollo Program professor and former NASA Deputy Administrator, tells Lex Fridman that getting humans to Mars in the 2030s depends on first spending a decade on the Moon testing habitats, life support, suits and in-situ resource use. She sees exploration as humans and robots working together, needing far more autonomy than exists today, and insists the most urgent mission is still caring for Earth.

## Key points

- **Life on Mars.** Newman says she is fairly confident there was life on Mars about 3.5 billion years ago, and expects evidence of past, likely fossilised, life within the next decade, pointing to organics and seasonal water found after 50 years of robotic exploration.
- **Moon before Mars.** She describes Artemis and the lunar south pole, including water ice in Shackleton crater, as a testbed for fuel production, 3D printing and radiation protection, and notes that NASA's SLS, SpaceX and Blue Origin will soon give three heavy-lift options.
- **Public-private partnership.** Newman says SpaceX was built on NASA and US Air Force funding, praises startups' appetite for risk, and mentions a NASA award for "failing smart". She highlights CubeSats, such as those Planet uses to image the whole Earth daily, and reusable first stages saving around 70 percent of cost.
- **The BioSuit.** Current gas-pressurised suits weigh about 140 kilos and limit movement. Her mechanical counter-pressure design applies a third of an atmosphere directly to the skin through patterned materials, with a gas-pressurised helmet that becomes an augmented-reality information portal.
- **Autonomy and AI.** With roughly 20-minute communication delays, Newman argues Mars crews and systems must be fully autonomous, like Magellan's crew. Today she applies AI mainly to Earth observation and climate data.
- **Earth first.** She rejects Mars as a backup planet and frames everyone as astronauts of "spaceship Earth", while expecting thousands of people in low Earth orbit and on the Moon.
