## Thesis

Lisa Randall, a theoretical physicist at Harvard, tells Lex Fridman that dark matter is something we can deduce firmly from gravity even though we cannot see it, and that we should stay open about what it is. She defends testing speculative but measurable ideas, such as a thin disk of interacting dark matter, and argues that good science means believing in your theories while constantly questioning them.

## Key points

- **Why we know it is there.** Randall says eight or nine independent methods agree on how much dark matter exists, carrying about five times the energy of ordinary matter. Because it cannot radiate, it stays roughly spherical in the Milky Way and, in her account, drives galaxy formation.
- **The dark disk and the dinosaurs.** She stresses this is speculative: if a fraction of dark matter radiates "dark light", it could collapse into a thin disk. As the solar system bobs through it, Oort Cloud objects may be dislodged, raising the odds of impacts like the one that ended the dinosaurs.
- **No reason to call it weird.** To critics who say the theory makes dark matter weirder, she replies that nobody knows what it is yet. WIMPs remain unfound and the LHC is now unlikely to find them, she says, though xenon detectors keep looking.
- **LHC as victory and warning.** The Higgs discovery confirmed a 50-year-old prediction, but Randall calls it a cautionary tale: many expected supersymmetry, and the cancelled Superconducting Supercollider would have been a more robust test.
- **Present-day risks.** She suggests we may be in an extinction now by species count, and says people are less worried about nuclear weapons than they should be. She has become more concerned about AI losing control.
- **Physics, Rovelli and AI.** Against Carlo Rovelli, she holds that electrons exist before measurement and wave functions are real. She sees large language models as generalisations of existing knowledge and does not think AI has yet discovered new laws of physics.

## Quotes
