## Thesis

Donald Knuth tells Lex Fridman that good programming and good technical writing both come from seeing a problem at many levels at once and explaining it twice, formally and informally. He describes The Art of Computer Programming as a living project that keeps being rewritten as new ideas arrive, and he argues that what is mathematically true (an algorithm exists) can stay far beyond human reach.

## Key points

- **What makes a geek.** Knuth, who first met computing on an IBM 650 with 2,000 words of drum memory, estimates about 2% of people think this way. Their traits are fluently jumping between levels of abstraction and being comfortable with non-uniform, many-case rules; he calls Alan Turing the first full geek.
- **Literate programming.** He says his life is a "convex combination of English and mathematics" and writes even one-shot programs in literate style because he gets them right faster.
- **A book that keeps growing.** The 1962 plan was one book of 12 chapters about compilers; he is now in volume 4B. Binary decision diagrams (Randy Bryant, 1986) and SAT solvers after 2000 forced new sections; the SAT material runs past 300 pages, and he wrote 15 SAT solvers while preparing it.
- **P versus NP.** Knuth suspects P may equal NP but thinks a proof would not yield a usable algorithm. He cites the Robertson-Seymour graph-minor theorem, which guarantees polynomial-time tests whose finite list of forbidden minors nobody knows, and the game of Hex, where a winning strategy provably exists but is unknown.
- **AI and understanding.** He credits AI researchers with supplying many of computer science's best problems, but worries about the gap between understanding and the illusion of understanding. He suggests ant colonies might be easier to study as a model of cognition.
- **Life and TeX.** Knuth's rule is that "point eight is enough" happiness; he describes facing prostate cancer in 2006 with acceptance and premiering his long-planned piece of music on his 80th birthday. TeX began when a new printing of volume 2 looked wrong to him, especially the numeral 2.
