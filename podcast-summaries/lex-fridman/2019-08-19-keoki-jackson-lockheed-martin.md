## Thesis

Keoki Jackson, CTO of Lockheed Martin, tells Lex Fridman that autonomy, increasingly AI-enabled, will be retrofitted into existing systems and designed into all future ones, from spacecraft to fighter jets. He frames the hard problem as verifying and explaining non-deterministic, learning systems in safety-critical missions, and argues that a safety culture and human control matter as much as the technology. Fridman presses him on the Boeing 737 MAX, AI arms races, secrecy and the military-industrial complex.

## Key points

- **Space ambitions.** Jackson describes the Orion spacecraft and a "Mars base camp" concept, and says the challenge is "not so much to go but to stay". He cites OSIRIS-REx autonomously mapping asteroid Bennu before a seconds-long "pogo stick" sample grab.
- **Humans plus AI.** He says humans still lead in adapting on the fly; Lockheed's Maya assistant (an "Alexa in space") would use the spacecraft's digital twin and flight data to flag anomalies for astronauts.
- **Levels of autonomy.** Jackson defines a spectrum from none, through human-in-the-loop and supervisory autonomy, to full autonomy, and says verifying learning systems is an active research area Lockheed funds, including at MIT.
- **Safety culture.** Asked about the 737 MAX, he argues that safety comes from a "culture of mission success": continuous skepticism, root-cause analysis and corrective action, as in commercial aviation's falling accident record.
- **Autonomy in flight today.** He cites the F-35 pilot as a "mission manager", the Have Raider autonomous F-16 wingman demo with AFRL, and Auto GCAS, which he says has saved seven aircraft and eight pilots. Sikorsky's MATRIX aims at "optimal piloting".
- **Policy and deterrence.** Jackson points to a short DoD directive on human control of autonomous weapons, describes compressing the OODA loop, and argues strategic deterrence now extends to cyber threats against infrastructure.
