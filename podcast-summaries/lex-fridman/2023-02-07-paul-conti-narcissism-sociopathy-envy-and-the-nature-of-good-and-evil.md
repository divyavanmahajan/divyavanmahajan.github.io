## Thesis

Paul Conti, a psychiatrist and author of a book on trauma, tells Lex Fridman that everyone has a capacity for evil, but orchestrated evil is driven by envy: the wish to bring others down to soothe one's own sense of inadequacy. He links that inadequacy largely to trauma, especially in childhood, and argues that truth, humility and gratitude are the healthy counterweights. Fridman pushes back at several points, notably on whether jealousy and envy are really different in kind.

## Key points

- **Envy versus jealousy.** Conti calls jealousy a benign wish for what someone else has, while envy wants to destroy it; crossing from one to the other is a choice. Fridman argues they are the same flame at different strengths, made worse by trauma, propaganda and crowds. Conti agrees that culture and demagogues can harness mass envy.
- **Narcissism as inadequacy.** After 20 years in practice, Conti says narcissism is not a common diagnosis but causes more than half of the destructive things he sees. He separates benign narcissists, who can tolerate others doing well, from malignant narcissists, whom he equates with sociopathy.
- **Empathy is not care.** Narcissistic people can read others well, Conti says, but that "empathic attunement" serves the self rather than kindness.
- **Trauma and childhood.** Drawing on his brother's suicide when he was 25, Conti describes how trauma teaches false lessons of shame. He says the first step to recovery is putting the trauma into words, and calls child abuse perhaps the greatest cause of suffering. He cites an annual US cost of over $300 billion from schizophrenia as one sign that society underinvests in mental health.
- **Finding a therapist.** Conti urges people to act as empowered consumers and interview therapists, and criticises insurance rationing, session limits and fear of litigation in healthcare.
- **Pride and humility.** Fridman says he is happier facing criticism with humility and gratitude rather than pride. Conti argues that some healthy pride balances vulnerability, and cites Winnicott's idea of being "good enough".
