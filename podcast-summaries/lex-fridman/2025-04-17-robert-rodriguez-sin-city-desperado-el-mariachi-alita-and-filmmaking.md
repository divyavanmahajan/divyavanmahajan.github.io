## Thesis

Robert Rodriguez argues that limits on money, time and equipment improve the work, and that a director who can shoot, edit, mix sound and do effects can move faster and cheaper than a large crew. He tells Lex Fridman that failures carry the seeds of later hits, and that people who want to be creative usually lack an identity as a creative person, not the desire.

## Key points

- **El Mariachi as film school.** Rodriguez shot the $7,000 film in one take per shot, without knowing whether the borrowed camera was exposing correctly, and planned it as a practice film for the Spanish video market. His agent suggested taking $20,000 for it; Columbia paid about ten times that, and it won Sundance.
- **Sifting failure.** He says the commercial flop Four Rooms gave him the ideas for Spy Kids and for Sin City's single-director anthology structure. His advice is to commit to a body of work rather than judge each project.
- **Editing as the hidden skill.** Rodriguez claims that on crews of 150 to 200 people nobody knows how to edit, which drives reshoots and costs. Because he cuts in his head, he shot Bruce Willis's Sin City scenes in nine days, Benicio del Toro's in three and Robert De Niro's Machete role in four.
- **Sin City and deadlines.** He made a green-screen test to persuade Frank Miller, who kept his own rights, offering to carry all the risk. Spy Kids 3D began shooting in January 2003 and was in theatres by July because McDonald's would have sued for $20 million over a delay.
- **Sound and Alita.** He calls sound "half the picture": an audience believes it saw an action it only heard. On Alita, his studio built a real set in Austin around Rosa Salazar's performance capture, which he says helped Weta on later Avatar films.
- **Brass Knuckle Films.** His new venture lets audience investors pitch ideas for a slate of four action films budgeted at $10 to 30 million, citing John Wick's $20 million first instalment.
