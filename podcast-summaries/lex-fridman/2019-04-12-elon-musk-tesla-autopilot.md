## Thesis

Elon Musk tells Lex Fridman that autonomy is one of two revolutions in the car industry, and that cars without it will become about as useful as a horse. He argues that Tesla's data lead and its new full self-driving computer mean the hardware in cars being built now is capable of full self-driving, with the rest a software problem. Fridman, whose MIT group had just published a study on driver vigilance with Autopilot, opens by stating that he disagrees with Musk on the value of camera-based driver monitoring.

## Key points

- **Data advantage.** Musk says nearly half a million Teslas carry eight external cameras, radar, usually 12 ultrasonic sensors, GPS and an IMU, and estimates that gives Tesla 99% of all such data.
- **FSD computer.** Musk describes Tesla's own computer as two fully redundant systems on a chip, comparable to a twin-engine aircraft, processing an order of magnitude more than the NVIDIA system it replaces. He claims a Tesla bought today is an appreciating asset.
- **All input is error.** Musk says driver takeovers and interventions are treated as signals, and that Navigate on Autopilot without stalk confirmation removes most takeovers for lane changes and freeway exits.
- **Proving safety.** Musk expects hands-on-wheel detection for at least six months, and suggests regulators may need Autopilot to be 200 or 300% safer than a person, measured in incidents per mile. He says regulators pay disproportionate attention to what generates press.
- **Vigilance and monitoring.** Fridman reports his team found drivers stayed functionally vigilant across about 18,900 disengagements. Musk calls the question moot, predicting human intervention will soon decrease safety, and compares drivers to elevator operators.
- **Adversarial examples and AGI.** Musk says adversarial attacks can be blocked by training on what is definitely not a car. He thinks a few key ideas are still missing for general intelligence, and that Tesla appears "vastly ahead" in self-driving.
