## Thesis

Andrew Strominger, a theoretical physicist at Harvard, tells Lex Fridman that no physical equation is exact and that the places where theories break down, above all the singularity inside a black hole, are what drive discovery. He sees the conflict between quantum mechanics and general relativity as the central puzzle of present-day physics, and string theory as a stepping stone towards resolving it rather than something simply right or wrong.

## Key points

- **Einstein's own doubts.** Strominger notes that Schwarzschild found the black hole solution in early 1916, yet 25 years later Einstein wrote a paper saying black holes did not exist. He also doubted gravitational waves, confused by coordinate transformations.
- **Counting a black hole's information.** Bekenstein and Hawking showed a black hole must store information proportional to its area, not its volume: the holographic principle. Strominger and Cumrun Vafa used string theory to reproduce that count, which he calls a moment of truth for the theory.
- **Soft hair.** With Stephen Hawking and Malcolm Perry he argued that zero-energy "soft" photons and gravitons leave subtle imprints on a black hole's horizon, undermining Hawking's 1970s argument that black holes destroy information. Whether this is the decisive flaw remains open, he says.
- **Photon rings.** Through the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration, which imaged M87, he describes a black hole as a hall of mirrors: light orbiting it produces an infinite sequence of images whose relationships depend only on the black hole's geometry and spin, not on the swirling matter around it.
- **Limits of string theory.** He bets "the farm" that string theory will be seen as a stepping stone in 100 years, but says it does not look like a final theory and has said almost nothing useful about the Big Bang. He thinks time, like space, must ultimately be emergent.
- **Scientists' responsibility.** On nuclear weapons, Strominger argues the bomb would have come without Oppenheimer, but tells Fridman that AI researchers have a real opportunity to influence how their work affects society.
