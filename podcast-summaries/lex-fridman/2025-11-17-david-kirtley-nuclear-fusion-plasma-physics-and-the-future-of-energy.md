## Thesis

David Kirtley, CEO of Helion Energy, tells Lex Fridman that fusion is safe, cannot be used to make weapons, and is now mainly an engineering and manufacturing problem rather than a physics one. He argues that Helion's pulsed field-reversed configuration (FRC) approach, which recovers electricity directly from the magnetic field instead of boiling water, combined with small, rapidly built machines, is the fastest route to commercial fusion power.

## Key points

- **Safety and proliferation.** Kirtley says a fusion system holds about one second of fuel, so stopping the fuel stops the reaction. Even a meteor-strike scenario analysed for the NRC would not require evacuation. Fusion is regulated under Part 30, like particle accelerators, and proliferation experts urged Helion to move "as fast as possible".
- **How the FRC works.** Reversing the magnetic field within a millionth of a second makes the plasma carry its own current and trap itself. The plasma is naturally unstable, like a top that falls over. It stays stable through a parameter Kirtley calls S* over E, and FRCs have lasted thousands of times longer than basic theory predicts.
- **Direct recovery and helium-3.** Fusion output scales with magnetic field to roughly the 3.77 power, which favours pulsed magnets that have exceeded 100 tesla. As the burning plasma pushes back on the field, it recharges the capacitors. Kirtley cites over 95% recovery of input energy and possible 80-85% conversion of fusion energy, against 30-35% for steam turbines. That makes deuterium-helium-3, which releases charged protons, the preferred fuel.
- **Build fast, build small.** Helion has built seven machines, from IPA to Trenta, which reached 100 million degrees in 2020. The team grew from 50 to over 500 people and is now 50% technicians. Kirtley describes buying used turbomolecular pumps on eBay to avoid nine-month lead times, and running in-house power-supply production lines.
- **Microsoft and data centres.** A 2023 deal commits Helion to delivering first electrons to Microsoft by 2028. Kirtley wants to feed DC power straight to GPUs, and imagines factories turning out 50 MW generators daily against 4,000 GW of installed fossil capacity.
