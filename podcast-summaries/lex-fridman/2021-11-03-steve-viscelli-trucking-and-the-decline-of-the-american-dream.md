## Thesis

Steve Viscelli, a University of Pennsylvania sociologist who drove a truck for six months while researching his book The Big Rig, tells Lex Fridman that trucking went from a well-paid Teamsters job to one where many drivers barely earn minimum wage. He argues that self-driving trucks are a transformative technology, and that the real question is not what the future will be but what society wants it to be. Fridman is more hopeful that bold innovators can find solutions.

## Key points

- **Pay by the mile.** Viscelli started at 25 cents a mile; drivers now start around 30 to 35 cents. He says hours spent waiting at docks go unpaid, so many drivers work 80 to 90 hours a week, and he argues a proper minimum would be about $55,000 to $60,000 a year.
- **No real shortage.** For a California study, he cites about 145,000 jobs needing a Class A licence against roughly 435,000 licensed drivers. In his view the problem is the quality of the job, not a lack of drivers.
- **The golden age.** By the late 1970s, Viscelli says, Teamsters under the national master freight agreement earned over $100,000 in today's dollars and got home every night. Unions tied wages to productivity.
- **Smaller automation impact, bigger transformation.** He estimates about 300,000 jobs directly at risk, far fewer than early forecasts. But longer driverless hauls could change where warehouses go, take freight from rail and put more strain on electrification plans.
- **Six scenarios.** Viscelli describes platooning, teleoperation, facility-to-facility, exit-to-exit, autopilot with a resting driver, and his own idea of a human-led truck with a driverless follower. He says the DOT/DOL workshop dropped the sixth, the one that would upskill workers, because no developer was building it.
- **Who benefits.** Viscelli points to Harry Bridges' "Men and Machines" report, which made the case for longshoremen accepting automation in return for a share of the gains. He argues the outcome depends on worker power, not on the technology alone.
