## Thesis

Philip Goff, a philosopher at Durham University and author of Galileo's Error, tells Lex Fridman that consciousness is a unique datum: it is qualitative and not publicly observable, so standard science cannot fully capture it. He defends panpsychism as a middle way between materialism and dualism: physics describes what matter does, while consciousness is what matter is. Fridman pushes on whether consciousness could instead be an extra property that science could measure, and on what this means for robots.

## Key points

- **Against materialism and dualism.** Goff argues that the smell of coffee or the red of a sunset cannot be captured in quantitative language. He now thinks neuroscience is too immature to rule out dualism, citing that a brain-scan pixel covers 5.5 million neurons, but rejects it as unparsimonious.
- **Physics as causal skeleton.** Following Bertrand Russell, Goff says physics captures behaviour, not intrinsic nature. He answers Sabine Hossenfelder's critique by saying mass, spin and charge are forms of consciousness, not extra properties, and compares consciousness to hardware running the software of physics in his debate with Sean Carroll.
- **Measuring consciousness.** Goff is drawn to integrated information theory but doubts that experience itself comes in degrees, as opposed to its complexity.
- **Zombies and moral status.** Goff holds that consciousness grounds moral value, so turning off a non-conscious "philosophical zombie" is not murder, and he follows a no-factory-farming principle. Fridman worries about manipulative robots and about past groups being denied consciousness to justify violence.
- **Free will.** Goff sees no strong argument against libertarian free will. Citing Jonathan Lowe, he says responsiveness to reasons separates free choice from randomness, and argues the Libet experiments study only arbitrary button presses.
- **Uploading and meaning.** Because he thinks consciousness depends on substrate, Goff warns that mind uploading might amount to suicide. He chooses to live in hope that existence has a purpose without claiming certainty.
