## Thesis

Levy Rozman (GothamChess), an International Master and chess YouTuber, tells Lex Fridman that the Hans Niemann affair exposes how weak over-the-board anti-cheating measures are in a world where engines far outplay humans. He says the evidence against Niemann looks stronger day by day but stresses there is no physical proof, and he tries to cover it without setting his audience on anyone. Fridman frames cheating as an existential threat to trust in the game.

## Key points

- **The timeline.** Rozman recounts Niemann's win over Carlsen in Miami, his win with black at the 2022 Sinquefield Cup, Carlsen's withdrawal and Mourinho tweet, the one-move resignation in the Champions Chess Tour, and Carlsen's statement. Fridman reads the statement, which says Carlsen believes Niemann "has cheated more and more recently than he has publicly admitted".
- **The evidence.** Rozman lists Niemann's admitted online cheating at 12 and 16, engine-correlation statistics from his 2020-2021 games, odd post-game interviews and past work with Grandmaster Maxim Dlugy, who was banned from chess.com. He notes each item can be argued away and that "you can't prove you're not cheating".
- **Detection is hard.** Rozman says security is a metal wand, players keep their shoes on, and a smart cheater picks the engine's fourth-best line or loses games on purpose. Fridman jokes about building a Bluetooth vibration demo after the viral anal-beads meme.
- **Audience power.** Rozman describes being swarmed by Indonesian users after he reported a cheating account on stream, which led him to geo-block his channel. He says that is why he avoids pushing his 1.6 million subscribers toward anyone.
- **Bots and engines.** Rozman says beginner bots make random mistakes no human would make, while Stockfish at 0.1 millisecond per move still beats humans. Engine moves, he says, look outlandish and counter-intuitive until you see the lines behind them.
- **Learning and burnout.** Rozman explains why he retired from competitive play because of anxiety. His advice to improvers: expect to lose much more than you win, and get puzzles right instead of rushing through them.
