## Thesis

Daniel Schmachtenberger, a founding member of the Consilience Project, tells Lex Fridman that humanity now has god-like technological power but still runs on rivalrous, Stone Age game theory. He argues that the post-war Bretton Woods order cannot handle many actors with many kinds of catastrophe weapons, and that new social technology for emergent, rather than imposed, order is needed. Fridman is more optimistic, arguing that love, markets and platforms like Wikipedia are already pushing in that direction.

## Key points

- **Self-terminating systems.** Schmachtenberger defines them as systems that degrade the substrate they depend on, citing Easter Island and fallen empires, and points to Joseph Tainter's The Collapse of Complex Societies and Jared Diamond's Collapse.
- **After Bretton Woods.** He credits the UN, World Bank, IMF, trade interdependence and mutually assured destruction with about 75 years without superpower war, but says that growth model hit planetary boundaries and created fragile supply chains, as COVID showed. Multiple actors with cheaper weapons, such as drones, break two-player deterrence.
- **Externalities.** Harm comes both from rivalry and from side effects. His example: Facebook did not set out to damage democracy, but optimising its news feed for time on site did so as a second-order effect.
- **Mimesis and conflict.** The two discuss René Girard's mimetic theory of desire. Schmachtenberger warns against treating any single model as reality, citing an Aboriginal Australian friend who says his tribe resolved conflict by dancing, not scapegoating.
- **Better metrics.** Fridman proposes that platforms let users self-report what makes them better people. Schmachtenberger counters that every single metric becomes a "paperclip maximizer"; he favours a society low in addiction and high on the "compassion compersion" axis.
- **Emergent order.** He argues China's imposed order coordinates long-term projects such as high-speed rail better than US four-year cycles, but has its own failure modes. Democracy instead needs education in the "science of government", a healthy fourth estate and technology that rebuilds town-hall-style proposition refinement.
