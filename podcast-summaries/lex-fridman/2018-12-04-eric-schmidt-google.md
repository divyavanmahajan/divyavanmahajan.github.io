## Thesis

Eric Schmidt, CEO of Google for ten years and then chairman, tells Lex Fridman that the lesson he learned late was scale: technology matters when it reaches hundreds of millions of people, so builders should aim for general platforms that solve common problems. He argues that companies need a real five-year model of the underlying platforms, a structure that funds bets, and that AI's next decade should be spent spreading its benefits in medicine and education rather than worrying about killer robots.

## Key points

- **Scale over cleverness.** Schmidt says that at Xerox PARC he saw the Alto was consequential but did not grasp what 100 million users instead of 100 would mean; he now looks for things that scale into platforms such as mobile and Android, and warns that narrow products get pushed into a corner by general ones.
- **Five-year plans.** He argues almost everyone has a one-year plan but few have a proper five-year plan built on a model of platform change, citing the slowdown of Moore's law, specialised hardware and high-bandwidth wireless bridging the last mile alongside fiber.
- **Funding bets.** Schmidt describes Alphabet as a structure of bets financed by Google's predictable revenue, combining bottom-up 20% time with top-down founder review, and Sergey Brin's 70/20/10 split between core, adjacent and unrelated work.
- **AI risk and timeframes.** He disagrees with Elon Musk's emphasis on existential risk, saying killer robots are not being built and that the next five to ten years are about broad deployment, especially AI tutors and health data; he supports Google's published AI policies.
- **Leadership and start-ups.** Schmidt says there is no single leadership formula, but the leaders Lex names share high intelligence and early starts. Asked by Lex about founding an AI assistant start-up, he points to Larry Page and Sergey Brin's simple beginnings, $100,000 from Andy Bechtolsheim, and the PageRank insight as a replicable model.
- **Money and meaning.** He cites happiness research showing that above a modest threshold money does not matter, and that purpose and service to others do.
