## Thesis

Matthew Johnson, a psychedelics and addiction researcher at Johns Hopkins, tells Lex Fridman that classic psychedelics such as psilocybin and LSD can produce some of the most intense experiences of a person's life with very little physical risk. In his view they work by weakening the "priors" and habits people rely on, which can help with addiction and possibly creative problem solving. He pairs that optimism with caution about overclaiming, especially on consciousness.

## Key points

- **Safety profile.** Johnson says classic psychedelics act on the serotonin 2A receptor, have no known lethal overdose for most people and are not addictive. He places MDMA in between: somewhat addictive and riskier for the body.
- **Addiction through behavioral economics.** He explains addiction with demand curves and delay discounting. People and animals discount future rewards hyperbolically, which causes preference reversals: the smoker who wants to quit in the doctor's office but accepts a cigarette when it is offered.
- **Tobacco and drug policy.** Johnson calls tobacco the deadliest drug: about half a million US deaths a year, four times alcohol. He favours regulating rather than banning drugs, backs safe injection sites and naloxone, and warns that banning nicotine without good e-cigarette alternatives could create black markets.
- **Psilocybin for smoking.** In his pilot of 15 smokers, 80% were biologically confirmed smoke-free at six months and 60% after about two and a half years. In an ongoing randomised trial of 80 people, 59% of the psilocybin group and 27% of the nicotine patch group were abstinent at one year among the first 44 to finish.
- **Drugs and sexual risk.** Using a delay-discounting task about condom use, his lab found that cocaine raises sexual desire, while alcohol seems to work through disinhibition.
- **Consciousness and the future.** Johnson says the theory that psychedelics dissolve the ego by disrupting the default mode network was overstated, and that psychedelics have not yet explained the hard problem. Speaking as an "armchair philosopher", he leans towards panpsychism. Fridman links first-principles thinking at Elon Musk's companies and Neuralink to the same openness.
