## Thesis

Kevin Systrom tells Lex Fridman that Instagram succeeded because its founders read usage data quickly, cut everything except photo sharing, and solved user pain points with simple engineering. Looking ahead, he argues that recommender systems drawing on all content, not just what friends share, and better reward design will shape the next big social network.

## Key points

- **From Burbn to photos.** Systrom says Instagram began as Burbn, a 2010 check-in app competing with Foursquare. Nobody used it, so the team listed three features users loved; photos topped the list. He compares this to a learning rate: shift towards what the data shows, neither too slowly nor too wildly.
- **Hiding latency.** Filters such as X-Pro II, inspired by a Holga camera from his photography studies in Florence, made poor phone photos look intentional. Choosing 512 by 512 pixels and uploading in the background while users typed captions, an idea from his time on the Gmail team, made the app feel fast.
- **A simple stack.** Objective-C, Python with Django, Postgres and Redis lasted to roughly 50 million users, Systrom says. The team started on one rented server in LA before moving to AWS; he credits early tests for letting four people move fast.
- **The sale.** Investors balked at a $500 million valuation; Sequoia and Greylock eventually invested. Facebook doubled that to $1 billion in April 2012, when Instagram had 13 people. Systrom describes a "we have arrived" letdown and staying until 2018.
- **Less social networks.** Systrom argues that pulling candidates only from what friends share favours divisive content, praises TikTok for letting anyone go viral, and says designing a scalar reward for long-term happiness is very hard. Fridman wants metrics beyond engagement.
- **Facebook and the whistleblower.** On Frances Haugen's documents about Instagram and teenage girls, Systrom says blame is spread across many factors and leaders should acknowledge problems openly. Fridman questions selective research and media incentives. Systrom also argues Facebook should stay true to its "job to be done", citing Clay Christensen.
