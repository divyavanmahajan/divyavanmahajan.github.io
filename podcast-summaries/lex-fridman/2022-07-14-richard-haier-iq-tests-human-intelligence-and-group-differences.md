## Thesis

Richard Haier, a psychologist and editor of the journal Intelligence, tells Lex Fridman that the g factor is one of the most replicated findings in psychology, that IQ scores estimate it well, and that genes strongly influence it. He argues that decades of environmental interventions have failed to raise it, so molecular neuroscience is the most promising path. Fridman repeatedly raises the discomfort of these findings and the risk of misuse; both agree intelligence says nothing about a person's worth.

## Key points

- **What g is.** Haier traces g to Charles Spearman's observation that all mental tests correlate positively. He says g accounts for about half the variance in a test battery, and that g factors from different random sets of 12 tests correlate above 0.9.
- **IQ predicts life outcomes.** Haier cites Ian Deary's Scottish study of children tested at age 11: about twice as many people in the top IQ quartile were alive 70 years later as in the bottom. In the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, he says, even within the top one percent the highest quartile had more patents and publications.
- **Interventions have not worked.** According to Haier, n-back training, the "Mozart effect" and compensatory education have shown no lasting effect on g. He says the Coleman report and later work found school and teacher variables explain about 10 percent of achievement.
- **The Bell Curve and group differences.** Haier says Herrnstein and Murray stated they were agnostic about whether the average Black-white gap was genetic, and argues many critics did not read it. He recounts the hostility Arthur Jensen's 1969 paper drew and calls Stephen Jay Gould's critique political. Fridman stresses scientists' duty to anticipate misinterpretation.
- **Flynn effect and brain efficiency.** Haier says scores have risen about three points per decade, likely from nutrition and health care. His early PET studies found an inverse link between glucose use and intelligence, leading to the brain efficiency hypothesis.
- **Why it matters.** Haier notes that about 51 million Americans have IQs under 85 and suggests an "IQ pill" might emerge from Alzheimer's drug research.
