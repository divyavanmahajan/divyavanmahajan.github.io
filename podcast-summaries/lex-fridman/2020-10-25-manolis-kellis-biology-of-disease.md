## Thesis

Manolis Kellis, head of the MIT Computational Biology Group, tells Lex Fridman that disease research is moving from one-gene-at-a-time biology to a systems, data-driven discipline. Because the same genome "circuitry" underlies very different disorders, he argues it should be mapped once and reused, and that automation, pooled experiments and machine learning can shrink the path from genetic discovery to therapy.

## Key points

- **Genetics breaks down departmental silos.** Kellis says his group's epigenome map now covers 833 tissues and shows enrichments in 540 disorders, often in unexpected places, such as immune cells for Alzheimer's. He compares a shared circuitry effort to dynamic programming (do the work once) and to "eat your own dog food", since his lab also uses its own tools on disease projects with collaborators and pharma.
- **Most disease variants sit outside genes.** Kellis says about 93% of disease-associated variants do not change a protein, so the nearby gene is often the wrong target. His obesity work found that the well-known FTO signal actually controls two genes about 1.2 million nucleotides away; he was introduced at a conference as the author of "the most depressing paper of 2015" for upsetting pharma's assumptions.
- **A repeatable pipeline.** He describes six steps: find the tissue, the causal variant, the disrupted motif, its regulator, the target genes and their biological process.
- **Scale through technology.** Kellis lists robotic screening, massively parallel reporter assays testing 10,000 variants at a time (up to seven million with a newer method), CRISPR perturbations and single-cell sequencing; his team generated 10 million brain cells from 1,500 individuals in one year.
- **Biology needs computer scientists.** He says experiments must be designed around the analysis pipeline, and calls single-gene biology a component, not a strategy.
- **Toward systems medicine.** Kellis says discovery-to-therapy time has fallen from about 50 years to about 10, and expects network-level, personalised interventions that avoid the "on target side effect" of hitting a multifunctional gene.
