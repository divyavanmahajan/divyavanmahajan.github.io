## Thesis

Lex Fridman calls this an emergency episode after AI systems released proofs of problems the guests thought were decades away. Alex Kontorovich (Rutgers), Ankur Moitra (MIT) and Steven Brunton (University of Washington) agree the results are real and field-altering. They argue that mathematics is about understanding, not just a pile of theorems. When problem-solving becomes cheap, asking good questions, building theory and caring for the community become more valuable.

## Key points

- **What dropped.** Kontorovich describes OpenAI's release of about 700 results, including a quasi-Riemann hypothesis: no zeta zeros with real part between seven-eighths and one. He also cites Anthropic's 13-million-line Lean proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, built by two or three people with no domain expertise. He says it shows autoformalization is basically solved.
- **Convex hull or more.** Kontorovich says the quasi-RH proof chains known techniques rather than introducing a new idea. Moitra replies that the results still change which problems researchers will pursue, citing an Anthropic 3SUM algorithm that undercut part of fine-grained complexity theory.
- **The doorman fallacy.** Moitra warns against reducing mathematicians to theorem-provers, just as a doorman does more than open a door. Kontorovich proposes a "solved axiom" (anything a human plus AI can solve, AI alone can solve) and argues that even then pure mathematics survives, though he does not believe the axiom.
- **Navier-Stokes.** Brunton explains that the AI built a forced 3D vortex that stretches until vorticity becomes infinite. He calls it important mathematics with no effect on day-to-day engineering. Turbulence prediction and inverse design remain hard.
- **Unique Games and young researchers.** Moitra says the proof of the Unique Games Conjecture builds on the Two-to-One Games Theorem by Dor Minzer and Irit Dinur. He worries that students now think on three-to-six-month horizons and that researchers may start hiding ideas.
- **Risks and advice.** Moitra uses "subliminal learning", where a model fine-tuned on random numbers picks up another model's love of owls, to argue that theory is needed for alignment. Kontorovich sees bimodal undergraduate grades, split by how students use AI. Moitra passes on Sanjeev Arora's advice to act as if you already have tenure.
