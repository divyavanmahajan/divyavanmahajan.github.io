## Thesis

Ryan Graves, a former Navy F/A-18F pilot, tells Lex Fridman that he approached unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) as an aviation safety officer, not as a believer in aliens: his squadron kept tracking objects in its training airspace and nearly collided with one. He argues the topic needs serious, cross-domain scientific analysis, and that AI in combat is most valuable when it makes tactics look random to an enemy.

## Key points

- **Radar upgrade, new contacts.** Graves says VFA-11 "Red Rippers" began seeing objects after moving from the APG-73 to the APG-79 radar. The objects stayed stationary against the wind or flew at 0.6 to 0.8 Mach for hours in airspace off Virginia Beach. He estimates 80 to 90 percent of the squadron saw them on radar, and infrared sensors later detected them.
- **Near miss.** Two squadron jets flew past an object that pilots described as a dark cube inside a translucent sphere. After that, Graves says, the squadron filed hazard reports. A former student told him pilots still see them.
- **Gimbal video.** Graves was airborne the day the Gimbal footage was recorded. He describes a formation of four to six objects on the situational awareness display, and an admiral who watched the video for a few seconds and walked out, suggesting to Graves he had seen such footage before.
- **Oversight.** Graves calls the House hearing chaired by André Carson "a bit disingenuous", calls the new AARO office a step in the right direction, and is working with the AIAA to assemble expertise. He disagrees with the debunking approach of a skeptic named West for dismissing aircrew testimony.
- **AI and autonomy.** Graves argues machine learning could make each engagement look random to the enemy. He wants humans commanding manned-unmanned teams at the level of mission objectives, and supports more autonomy under moral leadership because it allows more precision.
- **Aviation and life.** He names the F-35 the best fighter because information wins fights, now works on quantum materials simulation at GenMat, and advises young people not to fear looking foolish.
