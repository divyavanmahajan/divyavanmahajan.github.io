## Thesis

Vijay Kumar, Dean of Penn Engineering and former director of the GRASP lab, tells Lex Fridman that the interesting problem in robotics is making small, agile aerial robots that navigate and cooperate with no external infrastructure. He argues that swarms must be designed so individuals act on local information while the group stays resilient, and that the hard limits ahead are battery energy density, data-hungry learning and unstructured environments.

## Key points

- **Swarms as low-dimensional systems.** Kumar points to his lab's 2011 work on UAVs forming deformable 3D shapes in midair. He argues that engineers must design individuals with local views of the world so the swarm can be reasoned about as one unit, drawing on how ant colonies adapt.
- **True autonomy.** Kumar defines it as flying with no GPS, no communications to a base station, no pilot and no prior map. He prefers "aerial robot" to "drone", which he calls pejorative.
- **Why quadrotors took off.** He credits cheap inertial measurement units, driven down in price by car airbag accelerometers, plus the computing jump around 2007, for making agile flight possible between 2007 and 2009.
- **Learning versus models.** Kumar says machine learning has succeeded in perception but not in fielded action. He expects accuracy gains from 90% to 99.9% to need exponentially more data and energy, citing data centres at two percent of US electricity in 2014, and favours learning combined with model-based methods.
- **Delivery and flying cars.** He sees potential for last-mile delivery, for example his work with WeRobotics in the Peruvian Amazon, but says battery power density is the limit and doubts that electric flying cars are economically viable soon.
- **Humans and risk.** Kumar frames human-robot interaction as commanding, collaborating or bystanding, finds search and rescue more interesting than driving, and worries that swarms are easy to weaponize because only one robot needs to reach its target.
