## Thesis

Kevin Scott, CTO of Microsoft and formerly SVP of engineering and operations at LinkedIn, tells Lex Fridman that AI should be treated as a platform, not a tool owned by a handful of companies in a few cities. He frames Microsoft's history as a platform company, argues that hard questions about content, face recognition and deepfakes need democratic regulation, and closes with technological optimism about global challenges.

## Key points

- **AI as a platform.** Scott cites Bill Gates's test that a successful platform creates far more economic value for those who build on it than for its owner. He compares AI to the steam engine, which became a commodity component over time.
- **Data as labour.** He describes work by economist Glen Weyl (author of "Radical Markets"), Jaron Lanier and his team on "data dignity": mechanisms to value people's explicit and indirect data contributions, much as data-labelling firms such as Scale AI pay labellers.
- **AI inside products.** Scott says AI should not be the first-order feature, comparing excitement about it to excitement over a hash table. He points to dozens of Office features, search, Xbox anti-bullying moderation, Microsoft Graph and the upcoming Fluid Framework for co-authoring in Teams.
- **Mixed reality's surprise market.** HoloLens is selling to technicians and factory workers who never used a computer for their work before.
- **Policy and trust.** Scott says Microsoft has drawn its own lines on face recognition (referring to a Brad Smith blog post) but wants democratically set rules. On deepfakes he suggests cryptographically signed content with a verified chain of custody.
- **Leading large teams.** Scott stresses forming early opinions on infrastructure, tools and culture to avoid technical and "culture debt", and a clear mission, citing Yuval Noah Harari's "Sapiens" on storytelling beyond Dunbar's number. He ends hopeful that technology can help with global warming, feeding a growing population and ageing societies, as the Green Revolution once did.
