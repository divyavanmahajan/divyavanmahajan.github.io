## Thesis

Lisa Feldman Barrett, a neuroscientist at Northeastern University and author of Seven and a Half Lessons About the Brain, tells Lex Fridman that the brain is a prediction machine whose main job is running a "budget" for the body. She rejects single, essential causes in favour of many weak, interacting ones, and applies that view to love, evolution, evil and the idea of a single "true self". Fridman repeatedly plays the romantic and devil's advocate.

## Key points

- **Falling in love by text.** Barrett recounts meeting her husband Dan through anonymous Usenet personal ads around 1992: about 100 emails over two days, then hours-long phone calls before ever meeting. She doubts love at first sight, saying the brain fills information gaps with predictions, but values a partner who understands you; her example of romance is a six-way power plug.
- **Writing with brevity.** She says a book always takes about three times longer than planned, and that the hardest skill for an academic with over 200 papers is knowing what to leave out. The new book is a deliberately short set of essays.
- **No evolutionary ladder.** Barrett calls the layered "lizard brain, limbic system, neocortex" story a myth. She argues brains evolved under the pressure of hunting in the Cambrian period, using the eyeless lancelet as a model, and says the human cortex is the size expected for our brain.
- **Humans are not special, but combined.** Every human ability exists in some other animal, she says; what is distinctive is having them all interwoven. When Fridman asks if rerunning Earth would produce us again, she estimates well under 50%.
- **Evil and environment.** Barrett does not see Hitler as an anomaly: most people can do bad things given enough environmental push, and kindness is equally widespread. She cites colleague Michele Gelfand's research suggesting tight cultures fare better in pandemics.
- **Many selves.** She calls "just be yourself" a Western idea that can become an excuse, and advises choosing environments that bring out your best selves.
