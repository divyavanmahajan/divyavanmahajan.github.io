## Thesis

Robert Crews, a Stanford historian of Afghanistan, Russia and Islam, tells Lex Fridman that invading Afghanistan in 2001 was a mistake made in panic by a Bush administration that had ignored al-Qaeda warnings and treated a global network as if it had a home address. He describes a cosmopolitan, mobile Afghan society that changed a great deal over 20 years, and argues that successive US presidents, ending with Biden, made choices they were never held accountable for. Fridman largely agrees and puts the blame on a lack of leadership.

## Key points

- **Why Afghanistan.** Crews says Afghanistan was a soft target for revenge and for restoring legitimacy. He notes that most of the 9/11 hijackers were Saudi, and that Richard Clarke's warnings lost out to Condoleezza Rice's focus on great powers.
- **Bin Laden as politician.** Crews calls bin Laden an engineer, not an Islamic scholar: a modern politician who used grievances over Kashmir, Palestine, sanctions on Iraq, the environment and women's treatment to recruit. He calls him a mass murderer whose movement never became a mass one.
- **The Taliban today.** Crews describes them as a well-organised, mostly Pashtun clerical-military organisation that is good at diplomacy and media but unable to govern. He says frozen banks and the loss of NGOs could lead to hunger that kills tens of thousands or more.
- **Regional winners.** According to Crews, Pakistan backs the Taliban as cover against India, while Iran, Russia, China and Qatar each gave the Taliban legitimacy. He says ISIS now lets the Taliban present itself as a counterterrorism partner.
- **Democracy and youth.** Crews argues that the US backed rigged elections, yet young Afghans built a multi-ethnic public sphere. He singles out the American University of Afghanistan and says Biden did not have to keep to Trump's Doha agreement.
- **Accountability.** Fridman argues the internet rewards leaders who admit mistakes. Crews warns that the defeat could feed far-right politics among disillusioned veterans and in Europe's debate over Afghan refugees.
