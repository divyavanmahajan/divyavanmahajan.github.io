## Thesis

Edward Gibson, who heads the MIT Language Lab, argues that human languages are invented tools for communication whose structure is largely explained by one pressure: keep words that depend on each other close together. He sets his data-driven, experimental approach against Noam Chomsky's thought experiments and innate grammar, and cites brain-imaging and patient evidence that language is separate from thinking. That, he says, is why large language models are excellent at form but weak on meaning.

## Key points

- **Short dependencies everywhere.** Gibson notes that about 95% of the roughly 1,000 well-documented languages are "harmonic" (verb-object with prepositions, or object-verb with postpositions). His former student Richard Futrell scrambled sentences 100 times each across about 60 parsed languages; real word orders always had shorter dependencies than the random controls.
- **Against movement and innateness.** Gibson prefers dependency grammar and lexical copying (after Ivan Sag) to Chomsky's movement rules, arguing that movement created the learnability problem that led Chomsky to posit universal grammar.
- **Language is not thought.** Work by Ev Fedorenko shows a stable, left-lateralised language network that stays quiet during maths, music, spatial memory or programming tasks. Rosemary Varley's global aphasia patients lose language but can still play chess and do arithmetic.
- **LLMs do form.** Gibson calls LLMs arguably the best current theory of language, citing Chris Manning's finding that BERT builds dependency-like structures. But a tweaked Monty Hall problem still fools them, while they struggle with centre-embedded sentences just as humans do.
- **Why legalese is hard.** With lawyer Eric Martinez, Gibson found about 70% of contract sentences contain centre embedding versus roughly 20% in other texts. Passive voice had no effect on comprehension; centre embedding hurt laypeople and 100 lawyers alike, and the lawyers preferred plain versions.
- **Words follow needs.** The Piraha have no exact number words, only terms for few, some and many, and only approximate quantities beyond about four or five in hidden-set tasks. The Tsimane have three to seven colour words; Gibson argues cultures name only what they need to communicate.
