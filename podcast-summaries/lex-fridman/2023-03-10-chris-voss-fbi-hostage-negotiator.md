## Thesis

Chris Voss, former FBI hostage negotiator and author of Never Split the Difference, tells Lex Fridman that every negotiation, from kidnappings to car deals, is driven by feelings rather than reason. His core tool is "tactical empathy": showing the other side you understand where they are coming from without agreeing with them. Fridman pushes back that a drop of compassion helps; Voss says compassion is helpful but not essential.

## Key points

- **Reason rests on what you care about.** Responding to Sam Harris's case for reason over empathy, Voss argues people decide based on what they care about. He draws on Bob Mnookin's book Beyond Winning, which defines empathy as understanding and articulating the other side's view without liking or agreeing.
- **Labeling negativity.** Voss claims the brain is about 75% negative and that naming a negative emotion defuses it. He describes moving from "You sound angry" to "I know you're angry" when he met families of hostages held by Al-Qaeda around 2004.
- **"That's right" and the future.** He aims for the other side to say "that's right", which he links to oxytocin and serotonin. Borrowing from lawyer Tom Girardi, he suggests picking a future point both sides like and working backwards.
- **Walking away.** Voss says hostage negotiators succeed about 93% of the time, and that you should walk away when the other side is not really negotiating. He says US policy since NSPD 12 in 2002 has been no concessions, not no talking.
- **Tools and voices.** He describes three styles (assertive, analyst, accommodator), mirroring, what and how questions, and the word "fair" as a manipulation weapon. In a training exercise, one participant's heart rate jumped to 170 under an assertive voice.
- **Chatbots and conflict.** Voss recalls a negotiation chatbot company reporting better outcomes in about 80% of interactions, which he puts down to bots never being in a bad mood. Fridman argues a good negotiator needs the capacity to be mean and choose not to be.
