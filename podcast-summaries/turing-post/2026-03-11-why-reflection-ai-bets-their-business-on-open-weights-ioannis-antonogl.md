## Thesis

Ioannis Antonoglou, a founding engineer at DeepMind and now co-founder and CTO of Reflection AI, argues that frontier models should be released as open weights. In this first part of his interview with Ksenia Se, he says openness speeds up and validates science, makes models safer through outside scrutiny, and counters a dangerous concentration of power in closed labs. He also sees it as a viable business, as Chinese labs have shown.

## Key points

- **From publishing to secrecy.** Antonoglou joined DeepMind in 2012, when it had 20 to 25 people. He worked on DQN, AlphaGo, AlphaZero and MuZero, and led RLHF for Gemini. He says DeepMind published all of that work, and labs stopped publishing only after ChatGPT set off the competition. He describes Reflection as the only frontier lab committed to open science.
- **Two bets for investors.** The pitch, made before the DeepSeek moment, was that reinforcement learning would unlock the next capabilities and that open models serve "sovereigns": anyone who wants absolute control over their AI. He points to Llama 3 as early proof of an open-model commercial engine.
- **Vindicated.** Since then, he says, commercially successful open frontier models from Chinese labs, together with RL-trained reasoners and coding and tool-use agents, have confirmed both bets.
- **Openness and safety.** He compares open models to open-source software: more eyes find more blind spots. He calls OpenClaw "a good thing" because it showed people outside the big labs how much damage capable agents can do with computer access, and it started a debate about safety.
- **The usage gap.** Ksenia Se raises Nathan Lambert's admission that he mostly uses closed models. Antonoglou replies that people use the best models, so open models must be genuinely close to the frontier.
- **Who benefits.** Universities and research institutions would get frontier models to study safety and capabilities. Enterprises and governments could own their full stack on their own infrastructure.
