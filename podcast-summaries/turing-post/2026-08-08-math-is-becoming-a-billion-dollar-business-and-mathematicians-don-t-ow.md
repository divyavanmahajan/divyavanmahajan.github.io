## Thesis

In this solo episode, Ksenia Se examines OpenAI's claim that its next model, Astra, produced ten mathematical advances with Lean proofs. She argues that AI is breaking the mathematical community's monopoly on producing proofs, but not on judging, explaining and absorbing them. The open question is whether a few companies that own the models become a new guild.

## Key points

- **What Astra did.** OpenAI announced on August 1st ten results, from sphere packing and group theory to lattice cryptography. One settles a group-theory question open since 1999, and another advances a conjecture Eugène Ehrhart posed in 1964. OpenAI released a 249-page manuscript and Lean files. Se notes that OpenAI's own wording (each result resolves a problem or makes substantial progress on it) is more careful than the headline.
- **The $2,000 caveat.** The successful searches cost about $2,000 in tokens. Se stresses this excludes training, salaries, problem selection, formalization and failed attempts, so it prices the winning tickets rather than the whole lottery. The reasoning walkthroughs are reconstructions written after the fact, not raw logs.
- **Lean and the human contract.** Lean checks every step down to the axioms, but humans must still confirm that the formal statement matches the intended question. Lean's creator, Leonardo de Moura, calls that readable statement "the human contract".
- **Anthropic's counterclaim.** Within 24 hours, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said Claude Fable had matched five results. As of August 3rd these were unverified, with no PDFs or Lean files. Alpöge and Fable also found a counterexample to the general Jacobian conjecture in dimension three and higher, which Terence Tao explained.
- **A proof industry.** Axiom Math raised $200 million at a $1.6 billion valuation, and its prover solved all 12 Putnam 2025 problems. Math Inc.'s Gauss formalized a strong prime number theorem in about 25,000 lines of Lean in three weeks. Harmonic has raised $295 million.
- **Proof indigestion and authorship.** Tao warns that verification, explanation and acceptance by the community will lag behind generation. The Leiden Declaration, endorsed by the International Mathematical Union, keeps credit with humans, while OpenAI argues that human authorship would misrepresent the work.
