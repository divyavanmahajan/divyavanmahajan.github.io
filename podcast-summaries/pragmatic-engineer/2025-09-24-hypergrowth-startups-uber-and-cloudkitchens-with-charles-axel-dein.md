## Thesis

Charles-Axel Dein, roughly engineer number 20 at Uber and now at CloudKitchens, describes hypergrowth as productive chaos: constant hiring, frequent incidents and little time to fix root causes. He argues that standout engineers keep shipping, lift the people around them and do not stop at team boundaries. He also says the era of zero-interest-rate hyperscale startups is probably over, and that this is a good thing.

## Key points

- **Inside hypergrowth.** Orosz notes that Uber grew from about 20 engineers to more than 2,000 in five years. Dein recalls weekly onboarding, poor on-call and Friday-evening firefighting that showed where Redis, Postgres and queues broke.
- **Incidents and releases.** Dein says CloudKitchens tracks time to detection, mitigation and resolution, with mitigation the most important. He calls decoupling release from deployment with feature flags the simplest stability fix and says Uber should have used it more.
- **Hiring.** He treats hiring as a funnel, builds close partnerships with recruiters and pairs interviewers so they shadow, then reverse-shadow, and give each other feedback.
- **Ship and lift.** Dein says career ladders above senior over-focus on meta work. At CloudKitchens he expects staff engineers to ship, find 10x improvements and help colleagues. He describes the ideal profile as a "rake", following Martin Fowler's expert-generalist article.
- **Project management.** He runs his own training after an outside course went badly at Uber, and favours weekly updates that always include ETAs. On tech debt, he says it only counts as debt if you know you are taking it on.
- **AI.** Dein says CloudKitchens' own study found AI moderately useful, with migrations driven by a shared prompt as the best use case so far. He expects more supply-chain-style security problems, saw 25 of 30 recent resumes generated by AI, and never pastes AI-written prose.
