## Thesis

Emily Glassberg Sands, Stripe's head of information, argues that a company that is not primarily an AI company should first put LLMs safely in the hands of all employees and let bottom-up experimentation show where the value is. Small funded teams then turn the best ideas into products. In her view, Stripe's payments data could make it an "economic operating system" for its users, well beyond payments.

## Key points

- **LLM Explorer.** Three engineers built an internal ChatGPT-like tool in three weeks; within days a third of Stripe's roughly 7,000 employees were using it. It started with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, now serves more than half a dozen models, and strips PII. About 300 shared prompt "presets" appeared almost overnight.
- **Accelerators.** One- or two-pizza teams are funded for about six months by CTO David Singleton's office, mostly staffed by long-tenured employees on rotation. Four exist today. An internal LLM API now supports 60 applications, with default models but team choice on cost and latency.
- **Customer products.** Radar Assistant writes custom fraud rules from natural language, and Sigma Assistant turns plain-English questions into SQL over Stripe data. Sands says both let non-technical small businesses use tools they could not use before.
- **Payments optimisation.** ML on authorization retries recovers about 10% of false declines, Smart Dunning cuts recurring-charge declines by about 30%, and Radar checks a thousand signals in under 100 milliseconds. She speculates that a financial foundation model could add 100-200 basis points.
- **AI startups differ.** Sands lists four differences from earlier waves: high compute costs from the start, global demand from day one, subscription models, and lean teams that have to operate like mature businesses early. Over half of Forbes' top 50 AI companies use Stripe.
- **Education.** Drawing on her time at Coursera and her training as a labour economist, she argues that skill measurement is the fairest way to signal learning in the job market.
