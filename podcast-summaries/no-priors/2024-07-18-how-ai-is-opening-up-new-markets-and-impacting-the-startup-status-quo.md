## Thesis

In a hosts-only episode, Sarah Guo and Elad Gil push back on a Goldman Sachs report that questions the payoff of AI spending. They argue that enterprise adoption has barely started and that, as with the internet, the gains will go to companies that change their cost structure. Gil adds that new capabilities, API access and corporate "AI mandates" are opening markets in ways that make incubations and AI-driven buyouts worth doing.

## Key points

- **Rebutting the skeptics.** Guo says Daron Acemoglu's claim that AI will touch under 5% of tasks ignores how scale and data have improved models. Gil says many critics still think in terms of old-style ML, and he recalls IBM's CEO calling internet companies "fireflies before the storm".
- **Three market drivers.** Gil lists general-purpose capabilities, access through a simple API, and executive mandates to adopt AI. He sets about $0.5 trillion of US software spend against $5 trillion in services headcount costs.
- **AI-driven buyouts.** Gil has backed one or two firms that buy companies and rework them with AI, citing Klarna cutting its support headcount by 700. He contrasts this with older "tech-enabled" roll-ups that were mainly valuation arbitrage. Guo says founders in services tell her the automation works but distribution is the hard part.
- **Incubation, normally a bad idea.** Gil says incubations usually fail, but AI has left some markets crowded and others wide open. He incubated Braintrust with Ankur Goyal and is pulling together a healthcare team.
- **Public markets.** Guo would short mid-to-late-stage companies that lack the will to change. Gil sorts holdings into a new "Magnificent Seven", big tech (perhaps an Apple upgrade cycle driven by AI), AI-durable businesses, and an AI index beyond Nvidia.
- **Teams.** Guo sees founders aiming for high revenue per employee and choosing speed over hiring slower experienced leaders. Gil thinks real headcount cuts are a couple of years away, with SDRs and support first.
