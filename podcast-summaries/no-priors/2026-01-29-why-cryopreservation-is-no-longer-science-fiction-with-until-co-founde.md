## Thesis

Laura Deming, founder of the Longevity Fund and now co-founder and CEO of Until, tells Sarah Guo that reversible cryopreservation is already proven at small scale. The open question is whether it can be scaled up. Until's long-term goal is whole-body cryopreservation for medical hibernation, but its first product is pausing biological time for single human organs so that timing stops limiting transplants.

## Key points

- **Why this problem.** Deming says she is drawn to areas with a "social blind spot", which is how she sees ageing. Funding seemed to be the field's main bottleneck, so as a teenager she started a fund. She calls cryopreservation the problem she could imagine spending the next decade on. Co-founder Hunter Davis doubted it at first, did back-of-the-envelope maths on ice formation, and came back convinced it was feasible.
- **The use case.** Deming cites metastatic melanoma, where new combination therapies moved prognosis from 6-9 months to a decade or more. Hunter's father-in-law missed a clinical trial for a therapy by a couple of months. Hibernation could bridge gaps like that.
- **The science.** Ice forms through random nucleation, and expanding ice damages tissue. Below roughly minus 30 degrees ice formation stops, so the aim is to pass through that range quickly. Human embryos have been cryopreserved for over 30 years and used to create pregnancies. Greg Fahy's work showed that a rewarmed vitrified kidney transplanted into a rat restored normal function after about a month.
- **Engineering versus biology.** Faster cooling and rewarming mean less of the toxic cryoprotective agent is needed. Deming argues that temperature gives physics-style leverage that is rare in biology.
- **First product and the open questions.** Patients wait within about a 2-hour radius of a transplant centre, and surgeons fly on private jets to collect organs. Until works on human organs and whole-rat hibernation in parallel. Deming names the brain as the big unknown for whole-body preservation.
