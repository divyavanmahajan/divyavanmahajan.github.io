## Thesis

Glenn Fogel, CEO of Booking Holdings, tells Elad Gil that AI is a tool for doing Booking's job better for travellers and partners, not a threat that makes travel intermediaries worthless. He insists there is no lasting moat, but argues that the complexity of the business, from hotel relationships to merchant-of-record regulation, is greater than AI-native challengers expect.

## Key points

- **Career and the dot-com lesson.** Fogel joined Priceline in early 2000, just as the Nasdaq peaked; within nine months its value fell to a couple of hundred million dollars and the stock hit a dollar. After a reverse split it came close to $6,000 a share last summer, more than a thousandfold gain. He sees parallels with today's AI boom but will not guess how many companies survive.
- **Agents for complex trips.** Fogel describes planning a family trip to Europe with Priceline's Penny assistant, including when to use frequent-flyer miles. Gil cites Booking's team saying Penny adoption has doubled monthly for several months. Fogel's goal is an agent that predicts disruptions and fixes the knock-on effects.
- **Token economics.** Fogel says Penny is still small against $186 billion of travel and over a billion room nights last year, partly because Booking is not yet pushing it while it works out the cost per booking and lifetime value.
- **Customer service.** Cost per contact is down and satisfaction up with AI support, Fogel says, but some customers want a human and Booking must allow for that.
- **Capital.** About $700 million is being invested this year across many areas, not only AI. Fogel favours returning cash when investment or acquisitions cannot clear the bar, noting roughly 40% of shares bought back over a dozen years.
- **Jobs.** Machine translation eliminated the human translation and multilingual roles Booking.com once had. Fogel worries that job losses may outpace job creation, argues for upskilling staff to be AI literate, and doubts government retraining programmes.
