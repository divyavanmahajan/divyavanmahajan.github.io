## Thesis

Matt MacInnis argues that Rippling's advantage as a compound startup is its unified employee data, and that combining it with LLMs lets the company judge performance from actual work product instead of a manager's vibe. He presents Talent Signal as an input for managers, deliberately limited in scope to build trust, not a decision-maker.

## Key points

- **Compound startup.** MacInnis says Rippling has about 3,500 employees, tens of thousands of customers and roughly 25 SKUs, with over 150 former founders on staff; he argues mature SaaS economics converge on cross-selling to the existing base.
- **How Talent Signal works.** It reads source code, sales and support interactions, plus job level, but no demographic data, and labels individual contributors as high potential, typical or needing attention, with concrete examples. Results are calibrated within each company.
- **Deliberately narrow.** In an early access program it produces one signal per employee based on the first 90 days, so companies can back-test it against what they learned later; MacInnis cites the Overton window as the reason.
- **Against bias, with caveats.** He describes an engineer in India singled out as high potential by the model, and a misattribution where a CTO's own pair-programming made a struggling engineer look strong. Rippling's internal policy forbids decisions based on the model alone.
- **Why not a chatbot.** MacInnis says Rippling skipped copilots and chatbots because they would not sell extra software, and used its data platform (GitHub and Salesforce data linked to HR records) for something only it could build.
- **Early adopters.** Sales, engineering and support teams with competitive, coaching-heavy cultures signed up first; MacInnis says he welcomes critics who raise questions about bias.
