## Thesis

Abridge founder and practising cardiologist Shiv Rao tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that doctors and nurses will not be fully automated in the next decade, so the first signal to capture is the clinician-patient conversation. Abridge starts by taking clerical work off clinicians. Rao sees that as a wedge into orders, billing, clinical-trial matching and eventually decision support.

## Key points

- **Notes are bills.** Rao explains that US doctors are paid for the care they document, not the care they deliver. An Abridge note has to satisfy clinical, patient and revenue-cycle needs, so it is much more than a generic summary.
- **Hardest market first.** Rao says Abridge deliberately targeted large health systems such as Kaiser and Sutter, where the quality bar is highest. University of Kansas Health System, Emory and Yale were early wins. He says Abridge usually goes head-to-head with Microsoft for three to four weeks and has never lost one.
- **Timing.** Founded in 2018, Abridge struggled to sell demos in 2021-22. After ChatGPT, CIOs and CMIOs called back. Rao credits post-pandemic burnout and generative AI arriving at the same time.
- **Own speech stack.** Rao argues that error rates of three to five percent matter in medicine. Abridge runs in-house speech models that handle drug pronunciations and multilingual visits (Vietnamese, Haitian Creole, Brazilian Portuguese, Punjabi) and produce English notes within seconds.
- **Feedback loop.** With millions of conversations every few days, clinician edits feed preference tuning, DPO, reward modelling and reinforcement learning. Rao cites a 60% cut in cognitive burden within six weeks and up to 50% less burnout on a Stanford survey.
- **Where the money goes.** Abridge has raised more than $500 million, including a $250 million Series D. Rao says about 80% should go to R&D. He also describes "mutants": staff who are both clinicians and engineers.
