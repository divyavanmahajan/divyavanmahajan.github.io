## Thesis

In a hosts-only listener Q&A, Sarah Guo and Elad Gil cover open-source models, regulation and where to invest. Both say it is too early to regulate AI broadly, and both argue that despite the hype there is a lot to build at every layer of the stack, with incumbents likely to capture much of the value.

## Key points

- **Open source catches up.** Guo says nothing open is yet at GPT-3.5 or Claude quality, but more teams now know how to train large models and compute keeps getting cheaper; she bets on an open 3.5-level model within a year. Gil notes that would still leave open source a generation or two behind.
- **Agents and memory.** Guo describes autonomous agents as orchestration of LLMs in a loop rather than an architectural change. Gil is more interested in persistent memory across sessions and users.
- **Regulation.** Gil argues regulation tends to lock in incumbents and would limit it for now to export controls on advanced chips and defence uses, with robotics as the long-term risk because it gives AI physical form. He expects the 2024 election to be a pivot point. Guo agrees it is too early but cites fast compounding progress and the risk of a panicked political reaction as the strongest arguments for acting.
- **Hype cycles.** Gil compares the moment to the mobile photo-app wave, where only Instagram lasted. Guo hears that investors are wary at the application layer but competing hard for researcher-led foundation model companies.
- **Opportunity areas.** Both like voice synthesis and dubbing and compliance; Guo adds audit, tax and accounting, annotation and retrieval over enterprise data. Gil cites Harvey AI and tooling such as LangChain, Chroma and LlamaIndex, and estimates an 80/20 split of value in favour of incumbents.
- **Healthcare.** Gil sees faster wins in healthcare operations and payments than in drug discovery, which he says is held back by regulation and incumbency. Guo pushes back that AI could cut drug costs by making developers right more often.
