## Thesis

Kevin Scott tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that Microsoft treated AI as a platform once transfer learning began to work, concentrating compute and partnering with OpenAI rather than spreading resources thinly. He argues the valuable products are those that move from impossible to hard, and that the public debate underplays AI's potential for good, including for jobs.

## Key points

- **Concentrated bets.** Scott says Microsoft's AI spending was "peanut buttered" across many projects, so he took control of the company's entire GPU budget and allocated it where there was evidence scale would pay off.
- **Why OpenAI.** Scott describes the partnership as a "no regrets" investment in Satya Nadella's sense: even in the worst case Microsoft would learn to build large-scale AI infrastructure, and training compute had been growing roughly 10 times a year since 2012.
- **Supercomputers and models.** Microsoft built its first AI supercomputer in 2019, on which GPT-3 was trained, and is deploying Nvidia Hopper at scale; Scott expects a mix of open and closed models, citing Databricks' Dolly 2.0.
- **The copilot stack.** Scott outlines layers from UI and plugins to orchestration (LangChain, Microsoft's open-source Semantic Kernel), meta prompts, retrieval augmented generation and safety filtering in both directions.
- **Jobs.** Scott says we have a cognitive exponential, not a robotics one, so demand for surgeons, nurses, electricians, machinists and solar technicians will rise, and creative work stays human-centred because people want humans in their stories.
- **Optimism and regulation.** Scott points to Sal Khan's tutoring work and Bloom's two-sigma result, predicts the next trillion-dollar company may be founded this year, and calls demand for regulation a sign the work matters.
