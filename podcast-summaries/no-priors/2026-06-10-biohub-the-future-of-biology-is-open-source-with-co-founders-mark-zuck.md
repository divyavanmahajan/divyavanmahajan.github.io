## Thesis

Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan say Biohub will not cure diseases itself. Instead it builds tools that help the whole scientific field move faster. Biology lacks internet-scale data, so they argue an AI lab has to sit together with a biology effort that invents new ways to generate data. Alex Rives, the new head of science, presents ESMFold2 as early evidence that general models of biology can be used to design proteins.

## Key points

- **From mission to main focus.** Chan describes the original goal of curing, preventing and managing all disease by the end of the century, which Nobel-winning scientists laughed at. Zuckerberg now calls that timeline too conservative. Biohub is their main philanthropy, with $500 million committed to a virtual biology initiative and sites in San Francisco, New York and Chicago.
- **Building models hierarchically.** Zuckerberg argues you cannot model cells without first understanding proteins, or immune systems without understanding cells. So Biohub builds simulations level by level and collects data that links them, such as translucent zebrafish imaging.
- **ESMFold2.** Rives says the open, language-model-based system folded over 1.1 billion proteins. Antibody design came out of it as an emergent property: from one 96-well plate of designs the team found nanomolar binders, which they confirmed with cryo-EM.
- **Why a nonprofit.** Zuckerberg points to a 10-15 year horizon, heavy capital needs and data that cannot simply be bought. Chan adds that a neutral nonprofit draws in more of the field, including people working on the long tail of rare diseases.
- **Medicine for the individual.** Chan wants to trace each patient's chain from gene variant to protein to disease. Single-cell atlases could predict off-target effects such as kidney toxicity before a drug reaches human trials. Elad Gil, a former biologist, notes that clinical trials can take 15 years and cost $1.5 billion.
- **Talent and openness.** Zuckerberg says the main labs lack a frontier biology side and that a dozen or a couple dozen strong researchers can make real progress. He ties open source to empowering individuals rather than one central superintelligence, while noting biosafety trade-offs.
