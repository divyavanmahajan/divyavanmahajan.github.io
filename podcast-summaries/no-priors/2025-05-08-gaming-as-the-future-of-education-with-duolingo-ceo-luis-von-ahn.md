## Thesis

Luis von Ahn tells Sarah Guo that the main obstacle to learning is motivation, so Duolingo borrows the engagement tricks of mobile games to get people to spend the hundreds of hours a subject needs. He sees large language models as a strong fit for a language company: they generate course content at a scale humans could not, and they make conversation practice possible. He expects schools to adopt AI tutoring, but slowly.

## Key points

- **Origins in boredom.** Von Ahn and his PhD student Severin, now CTO, built the first Spanish and German courses but could not make themselves use them, so they turned the product into a game. He says neither of them likes learning languages, which pushed them to build for the average person.
- **What worked.** Cutting lessons from 30 minutes to 2 minutes was the first big win. Streaks proved more powerful than he expected (10 million active users have streaks over 365 days), as did a "passive aggressive" notification saying reminders would stop.
- **Hours on the app.** Von Ahn says an English speaker needs about 500 hours to reach a good level in Spanish and about 2,000 for Chinese, so the goal is simply time spent. Exercises are pitched at roughly an 83% chance of success, which he says maximises enjoyment.
- **AI-built content.** Duolingo rebuilt its content pipeline around LLMs, with little human involvement, allowing it to offer its 40 languages from every base language rather than mainly to English speakers. AI conversation practice lets learners talk without feeling judged.
- **New subjects.** Math, music and soon chess qualify because they have audiences of hundreds of millions, take hundreds of hours to learn, and have an internal champion. Von Ahn says 16,000 A/B tests shaped the app.
- **Brand and schools.** The owl's "unhinged" persona grew out of internet memes and a junior marketer's TikToks. He predicts classrooms will use AI for individual attention within 20 years, with private schools and some countries moving first.
