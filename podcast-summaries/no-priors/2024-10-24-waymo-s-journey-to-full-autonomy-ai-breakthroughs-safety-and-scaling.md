## Thesis

Waymo co-CEO Dmitri Dolgov argues that getting started in self-driving is easy and getting to full autonomy is very hard, because the difficulty lies in the "many nines" of rare events. In his view Waymo has now "cracked the nut": it has a driver that works and the machinery to evaluate it. What remains is optimisation and gradual scaling, paced by public trust rather than by technology.

## Key points

- **From DARPA to 100,000 rides a week.** Dolgov began with Stanford's DARPA Urban Challenge team. He helped start Google's project in 2009, which pivoted from driver assistance to full autonomy around 2013. Waymo now runs over 100,000 paid rides and more than a million miles a week in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles and Austin.
- **Generational jumps.** Dolgov describes the 2015 Firefly as the "zero to one" moment and the fourth-generation Pacifica fleet as the one that opened to the public in Chandler in 2020. He credits the fifth-generation Jaguar platform to Transformers, bigger models and a data and evaluation flywheel, with vision-language models now adding world knowledge.
- **Safety data.** Across 22 million rider-only miles, Dolgov says Waymo is about twice as good as human benchmarks on low-severity collisions and about six times better on airbag-deployment crashes. A study with Swiss Re found a 4x reduction in damage claims and a 100% reduction in bodily injury claims over just under 4 million miles.
- **Sensors tested empirically.** Asked about camera-only approaches, Dolgov says Waymo can now remove lidar or radar in evaluation. Doing so is not good enough for full autonomy, though it might be for a driver-assist system.
- **Partnering, not building cars.** Ride-hailing is the focus. Dolgov says Waymo partners with OEMs and tier ones rather than building vehicles, and designed its sixth-generation car around the rider, with flat floors and sliding doors.
- **Why off-the-shelf models fall short.** Dolgov says a fine-tuned vision-language model can drive impressively for hundreds of miles. Imitation learning alone, however, plateaus at the wrong place for full autonomy, which also needs synthetic data and closed-loop simulation.
