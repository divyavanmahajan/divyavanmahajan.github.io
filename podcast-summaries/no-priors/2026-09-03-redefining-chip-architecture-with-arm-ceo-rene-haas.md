## Thesis

Rene Haas, CEO of Arm and SoftBank Group International, tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that the attention paid to accelerators has hidden a basic fact: every AI system still needs CPUs to orchestrate where tokens go. He describes Arm's move from licensing IP to selling physical chips. He expects compute supply to stay constrained for at least three to five years, and he argues that the US should keep chip manufacturing and data centres at home.

## Key points

- **From IP to chips.** Arm moved from licensing CPU IP to "compute subsystems" (a Lego-style blueprint), and then to its first product, the Arm AGI CPU, announced last March. Meta asked for a general-purpose agentic CPU that nobody else offered. Haas says customers such as Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft and Google backed the move, even though it forced Arm to build supply-chain and physical-design teams that its 98.5% gross margin IP model never needed.
- **AI in chip design.** Chip design takes 24 to 36 months, and most of that time goes into verification, validation and debug, where Haas says AI is strong. He says 80-90% of Arm engineers use AI tools daily. RTL generation and physical design lag because the relevant data is proprietary. He thinks idea-to-GDS2 automation for simpler designs is possible within five or more years.
- **Bottlenecks.** Haas expects data centre construction, plus local resistance to it, to be the next constraint after packaging and memory. He sees no bubble in supply versus demand, because demand is insatiable while the transformer remains compute and memory intensive.
- **SoftBank's role.** He oversees Ampere, Graphcore and Stack AV, and helps Masayoshi Son carry out a strategy built on robotics, OpenAI, infrastructure and Arm. He says the planned SoftBank neocloud could become a home for young chip companies.
- **Robotics and policy.** Haas expects both humanoid and task-specific robots, mostly running on Arm, with distribution centres and delivery automated first. He calls for more US fabs for national security reasons. He calls the claim that data centres create no jobs "BS", pointing to energy, liquid cooling and electricians.
