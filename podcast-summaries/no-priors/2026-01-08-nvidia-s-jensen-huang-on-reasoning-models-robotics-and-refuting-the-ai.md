## Thesis

Jensen Huang, NVIDIA's founder and CEO, argues that AI should be understood as a five-layer stack (energy, chips, infrastructure, models, applications) that spans many kinds of information, not as one chatbot or a coming "God AI". Seen that way, he says, the doomer, job-loss and bubble narratives fall apart: AI changes the tasks in a job rather than its purpose, and demand for computing is outrunning supply across every industry.

## Key points

- **2025 surprises.** Huang was pleased by progress on grounding, reasoning and search-connected models, and somewhat surprised at how fast reasoning-token demand grew and that those tokens are now profitable, citing OpenEvidence's reported 90% gross margins along with Cursor and Claude.
- **Task versus purpose.** He cites Geoffrey Hinton's prediction about radiology: AI now powers radiology applications, yet the number of radiologists has grown, because their purpose is diagnosing disease, not reading scans. He applies the same logic to software engineers, lawyers and waiters, and points to new jobs for electricians and other trades building chip plants, computer plants and AI factories.
- **Open source and China.** Huang says startups, higher education and century-old industrial firms would be "suffocated" without open models. He calls DeepSeek probably the single greatest contribution to American AI last year, and says no country contributes more to open source than China.
- **Falling costs.** NVIDIA's hardware improves 5-10x per year, and Huang would not be surprised if token-generation cost fell about a billion times over ten years once algorithms and models are included. He defends a programmable architecture because transformer variants, such as hybrid SSMs, keep changing.
- **Next "ChatGPT moments".** He expects them in digital biology (proteins, chemistry) and in reasoning-based cars and multi-embodiment robots. He says NVIDIA's self-driving stack just received the top safety rating, with Tesla second.
- **No bubble.** Without chatbots, Huang says NVIDIA would still be a multi-hundred-billion-dollar company, because computing is shifting to acceleration. He notes that its AV business is nearing $10 billion, and argues that the world's roughly $2 trillion of annual R&D is moving onto supercomputers.
