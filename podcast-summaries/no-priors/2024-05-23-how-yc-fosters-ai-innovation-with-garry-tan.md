## Thesis

Y Combinator president and CEO Garry Tan argues that the AI wave has reopened markets so that young, highly technical founders can win again, and that YC's job is to find them and push them to ship every week. Speaking with Sarah Guo and Elad Gil, he defends so-called GPT wrappers as real businesses, warns about raising too much money, and closes with a call for civic engagement in San Francisco.

## Key points

- **Scale of YC today.** Tan's 2008 batch had about 25 companies; YC now has 14 group partners who each fund 15 to 25 companies. He says around 70% of a batch is AI-related, and roughly two-thirds of those are applications built on frontier models.
- **Wrappers are real software.** Tan rejects the "wrapper" label, comparing it to calling all SaaS a MySQL wrapper. He cites Jake Heller of Casetext, who mapped what a lawyer does step by step into prompts and workflows once GPT-4 rarely hallucinated, and says a recent batch grew from about $6 million to north of $30 million ARR in three months.
- **First-hand knowledge.** Founders should talk to customers directly, such as corporate litigators rather than "all lawyers"; talking to other founders, investors or reading the press is second-, third- or fourth-hand.
- **Shipping culture.** Tan credits Paul Buchheit, creator of Gmail, with group office hours that use peer pressure on weekly goals, and points to Kyle's early Cruise demo, a $10,000 attachment for an Audi A4 that drove Highway 101 in 2013.
- **Younger founders, bigger cheques.** Tan was 27 at YC, about today's average, and expects the current batch to be closer to 25 or 26 as 19- to 21-year-olds drop out for AI. He links waves of innovation to YC raising its investment, most recently to half a million dollars in 2022.
- **Too much money hurts.** Tan says some of the best technical founders raised too much and then couldn't make deep cuts; he also argues big tech is now a "weak elephant" that rarely stomps startups, citing Scale.
