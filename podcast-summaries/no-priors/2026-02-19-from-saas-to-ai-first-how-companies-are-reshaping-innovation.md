## Thesis

In a hosts-only episode, Elad Gil and Sarah Guo argue that the market panic over the end of software projects the habits of five-person startups onto the Fortune 100. They expect durable incumbents to survive the short term. They also say AI compresses a decade of disruption into a year or two, so founders should be honest about their control points and think about exits sooner than in the SaaS era.

## Key points

- **Vibe coding will not replace Samsara.** Gil says nobody will vibe code a fleet-management app, build its in-cab cameras and sell it through "vibe sales". He sees a real shift only in categories like customer support, where Decagon and Sierra move from per-seat to usage pricing. Guo notes AI-native portfolio companies with fewer than 50 engineers that have hired close to 100 salespeople.
- **The open problem is code quality.** Guo says nobody knows how to manage human attention to engineering when agents produce code that no one reads. Gil adds that engineers who value craftsmanship may be less happy at larger companies, while those who code to ship product will feel freer.
- **Revenue and token signals.** Gil's team found that ADP and Adobe took about 20 years to go from $1 billion to $10 billion in revenue, Google, Meta and AWS took a few years, and the AI labs took about one. The cost of GPT-4-level tokens fell 150x in 21 months, and o1-level tokens fell 88x in 11 months.
- **Tech's share of the economy.** The top eight tech companies hold about $23 trillion of market cap and over 50% of the S&P 500. Tech's share of US GDP has grown from about 4% in 2005 to about 12%. Gil models 15-30% by 2035.
- **Internet era, not SaaS era.** Both hosts compare the moment to the dot-com wave and to Lotus. Gil advises booking a board meeting on exits once or twice a year, so the topic stays unemotional, and cites Mark Cuban's sale to Yahoo.
- **Bundles as defence.** Gil argues that multi-product bundles are the best defence. Guo lists platforms, ecosystems, networks and hardware as control points.
