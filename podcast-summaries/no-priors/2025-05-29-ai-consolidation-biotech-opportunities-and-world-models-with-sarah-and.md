## Thesis

Elad Gil argues that after two to three years of uncertainty, a subset of AI markets (LLM foundation models, coding, customer support, medical scribing) is consolidating around a few likely winners for the next two years, and that leading startups should consider merging to fight incumbents instead of each other. He also says biotech's funding model leaves large markets unaddressed. Sarah Guo describes world models and reinforcement learning as an open, hard research path toward broader intelligence.

## Key points

- **Markets settling.** Gil names Cursor, Codeium, Cognition and Microsoft Copilot in coding, and Sierra and Decagon in customer success, while sales, financial analysis and accounting remain open. Guo adds Abridge and OpenEvidence as examples, says she is unsure how sales will be won, and adds pharma as a document-driven market.
- **Coding is still moving.** Guo points to Microsoft open-sourcing Copilot to counter Cursor, and to OpenAI betting on both async cloud agents (Codex) and the IDE through its Windsurf acquisition.
- **Merge the number one and two.** Gil cites X.com and PayPal as precedent; ego, integration worries and valuation block deals. At Twitter he priced an offer for a rival social network by the ratio of combined users; revenue works the same way.
- **Biotech gaps.** Gil sees neglected demand in ageing, hair, vision, hearing, tooth regrowth and fertility technology, noting Botox became a $40 billion company on about $1.5 billion a year of cosmetic revenue. He blames old incumbents (no $50 billion-plus biotech built from scratch since Regeneron in the 1980s, Moderna aside), VC models built to sell into pharma pipelines, regulation, and scientists seeing commercial work as low status.
- **World models and RL.** Guo says behaviour cloning on human traces is brittle once agents go off-path. RL needs designed rewards and environments rich enough to teach yet cheap to run, so she doubts near-term commercial uses such as generated games or robotics data.
- **Unconstrained search.** Gil cites Go moves humans later copied and asks how code would change if models optimised for outcomes, not imitation.
