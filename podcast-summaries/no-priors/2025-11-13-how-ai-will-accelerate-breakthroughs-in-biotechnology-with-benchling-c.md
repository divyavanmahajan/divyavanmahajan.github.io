## Thesis

Sajith Wickramasekara, co-founder and CEO of Benchling, tells Sarah Guo that drug development costs over $2 billion and about ten years per medicine because the industry's digital workflows are bespoke and artisanal. He argues the near-term gain from AI comes from augmenting scientists across many small steps, not from a fully autonomous "AI scientist", and that pressure for speed and cost, driven partly by China, makes this change urgent.

## Key points

- **Benchling's scale.** Benchling started about 13 years ago and now serves about 1,300 biotech and pharma companies and scientists at over 7,000 academic institutions, including Eli Lilly, Regeneron and Isomorphic Labs.
- **A biotech bust and China.** Wickramasekara describes the last couple of years as biotech's dot-com bust after the COVID-era peak. Large pharma now buys molecules from fast, cheap Chinese biotechs; he cites J&J's Carvykti, licensed from Legend Biotech.
- **Benchling AI.** It combines simulation (open-source and in-house models such as AlphaFold and Chai inside the wet-lab workflow) with agents. One customer used the deep research agent to find that mouse studies planned to take eight months had already been run years earlier at an acquired company.
- **Augmentation first.** He compares fully autonomous discovery to Waymo and stepwise augmentation to Tesla, and hopes to cut 7-10 years to 2-3. He says most scientists in Boston and London barely use AI in R&D yet, held back by accuracy, IP and security concerns.
- **Business models and data.** He expects pure bio-model companies to drift into therapeutics because model building is commoditising, and notes data rarely changes hands because no one trusts another lab's data. He cites Eli Lilly's TuneLab as a give-to-get federated model.
- **Company building.** Co-founder Ashu gave up his direct reports to go full-time on AI. Wickramasekara still spends 30-50% of his time with customers and says five to ten close customers represent the whole industry.
