## Thesis

Andrew Feldman, co-founder and CEO of Cerebras, tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that a radical improvement requires a radically different architecture. Cerebras's dinner-plate-sized wafer-scale chip was fast for years before anyone cared. Once models became useful enough for daily work in 2025, he argues, speed became essential, which led to an OpenAI deal, an AWS agreement and an IPO valuing the company at about $63 billion.

## Key points

- **The contrarian bets.** Feldman says new workloads create openings for new architectures, as graphics did for Nvidia and mobile did for ARM. Cerebras builds a 46,000 square millimetre chip and claims inference 15-20x faster than GPUs.
- **Years of not working.** From mid-2017 to mid-2019 the team could not yield the wafer while spending about $8 million a month. Feldman notes that Gene Amdahl had failed at wafer scale. A co-founder's estimate that a compiler takes 10 years turned out right.
- **Crossing the chasm.** The first generation sold perhaps a dozen units and the second about 300. Early buyers were national labs, oil and gas and pharma. G42's billion-dollar order let Cerebras rebuild its supply chain and test clusters at scale. Cerebras plans to increase manufacturing 10x this year.
- **The OpenAI deal.** After talking to Sam Altman in mid-2025 and running trials, Cerebras signed a term sheet the night before Thanksgiving and a master agreement on 24 December. Feldman says the deal is worth over $20 billion and was done in about four and a half weeks.
- **Why go public.** Feldman calls the IPO a move from corporate adolescence to adulthood. It brings legitimacy with large customers and lets the public market buy an AI pure play. It took Cerebras 10 years, so the company allowed employees to sell shares along the way.
- **Culture and tokens.** Spending on coding tokens rose from under $1,000 per engineer eight months ago to $25,000-30,000. Feldman says that turns a few engineers into "100x" people. With about 850 staff, he worries most about losing the company's fearless culture and settling in hiring.
