## Thesis

Joshua Xu, co-founder and CEO of HeyGen, argues that filming, not editing, is the costly bottleneck in video production, and that AI can replace the camera. Instead of generating a whole clip end to end like Sora, HeyGen splits a video into components (avatar A-roll, B-roll, voice, music) and assembles them with an orchestration engine, which Xu says gives businesses the quality, control and consistency they need.

## Key points

- **From Snap to HeyGen.** Xu spent about six and a half years at Snapchat, the last two on AI camera features such as the 2018 baby and Disney-style filters, before founding HeyGen roughly three and a half years ago.
- **The camera is the bottleneck.** Xu says a CEO video needs two weeks of scheduling, a crew and a studio, and often 20 minutes of recording for two minutes of footage. He groups use cases into create, localise (more than 175 languages and dialects) and personalise, citing a McDonald's campaign for messages to relatives in other languages.
- **Quality line and stack.** Xu describes an invisible quality threshold of about "90" below which output is unusable, and says avatars are now above it. HeyGen uses OpenAI for text and orchestration, OpenAI and ElevenLabs for voice, and builds avatar creation, rendering and B-roll in house. Next are full-body and streaming avatars; he treats Sora as a possible component, not a rival.
- **Safeguards.** HeyGen bans political and election content and uses live video consent, a dynamic verbal passcode and human review.
- **Video as a new format.** Xu expects avatar pipelines to run as real-time streams within about two years and whole videos to be generated in real time within five, such as ads tailored to each viewer. The hosts connect this to personalised education.
- **Evaluation and scale.** Lower loss does not mean better visuals, so HeyGen relies on in-product A/B tests, a lesson Xu took from Snap's camera work. The company has just over 40 people serving over 40,000 paying customers, mostly "Main Street" businesses.
