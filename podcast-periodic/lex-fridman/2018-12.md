## Overview

December was the busiest month of the show's first year, with five guests. Stuart Russell and Eric Schmidt took opposite views on how much attention long-term AI risk deserves, while Pieter Abbeel, Juergen Schmidhuber and Tuomas Sandholm covered how machines learn to act: through reinforcement learning, curiosity and game theory.

## Main themes

### Control, risk and deployment

- Russell argues the standard model, where a machine optimises an objective handed to it, is the root of the control problem. A system sure of its goal “has no incentive to listen to us anymore”, so machines should stay uncertain about what humans want ([Russell](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/stuart-russell-long-term-future-of-ai/)).
- Russell lists loss of control, misuse and overuse as failure modes. He calls for bias standards and a rule that machines identify themselves, comparing unregulated software to pharmaceuticals before the FDA ([Russell](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/stuart-russell-long-term-future-of-ai/)).
- Schmidt disagrees with Elon Musk's emphasis on existential risk: “the killer robots are not arriving this year, and they're not even being built.” He wants the next decade spent spreading AI into education and health ([Schmidt](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/eric-schmidt-google/)).
- Sandholm calls value misalignment a fairly theoretical worry and names climate change and nuclear war as his main concerns ([Sandholm](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/tuomas-sandholm-poker-and-game-theory/)).

### Google, scale and funding bets

- Schmidt says technology matters when it reaches hundreds of millions of people, so builders should aim for general platforms. He urges real five-year plans built on a model of platform change ([Schmidt](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/eric-schmidt-google/)).
- He describes Alphabet as a set of bets paid for by Google's predictable revenue, using 20% time and Sergey Brin's 70/20/10 split ([Schmidt](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/eric-schmidt-google/)).

### Hype, AI winters and self-driving cars

- Russell recalls the expert-system winter of the late 1980s and names self-driving cars as the likely flagship of the next disappointment. He notes a car changed lanes on a freeway in 1987, and says driving needs about eight nines of reliability ([Russell](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/stuart-russell-long-term-future-of-ai/)).
- He finds AlphaGo a little disappointing because its design of position evaluation plus selective look-ahead resembles much older game programs ([Russell](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/stuart-russell-long-term-future-of-ai/)).

### Learning to act: RL, meta-learning and curiosity

- Abbeel says sparse rewards are reinforcement learning's bottleneck. He looks to self-play, human comparisons, demonstrations and training across many simulators for more signal per experience ([Abbeel](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/pieter-abbeel-deep-reinforcement-learning/)).
- Schmidhuber says true meta-learning lets a system inspect and modify its own learning algorithm, and calls much current work simple transfer learning. Abbeel saw some sign of life for meta-learned hierarchy but no real-world scale yet ([Schmidhuber](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/juergen-schmidhuber-godel-machines-meta-learning-and-lstms/), [Abbeel](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/pieter-abbeel-deep-reinforcement-learning/)).
- Schmidhuber frames science as compression progress and rewards curious agents for it. He says passive pattern recognition for ads is only one or two percent of the world economy, and expects active machines to reshape production ([Schmidhuber](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/juergen-schmidhuber-godel-machines-meta-learning-and-lstms/)).

### Game theory beyond the table

- Sandholm explains how Libratus beat top heads-up no-limit pros over 120,000 hands using Nash-equilibrium reasoning and abstraction of a game tree of about 10^161, without modelling opponents ([Sandholm](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/tuomas-sandholm-poker-and-game-theory/)).
- He points to kidney exchange and sourcing auctions as real-world uses, and wants to show business and military strategy can be done with computational game theory ([Sandholm](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/12/tuomas-sandholm-poker-and-game-theory/)).
