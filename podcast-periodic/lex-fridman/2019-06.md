## Overview

June had two episodes with little overlap. Rajat Monga told the story of TensorFlow, from Google Brain's early scaling work to an open-source ecosystem preparing its 2.0 release. Rosalind Picard covered affective computing, the field she launched, and argued that consent and user control matter more than how well machines read emotion.

## Main themes

### TensorFlow and open source

- Monga traces Google Brain to Jeff Dean and Andrew Ng's idea of scaling research methods with Google's compute and data, with some training runs on 10,000 machines ([Monga](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rajat-monga-tensorflow/)).
- Fridman calls open-sourcing TensorFlow “a definitive moment in the tech industry”. Monga credits Dean with pushing for openness after watching Hadoop and HBase become standards built on Google papers ([Monga](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rajat-monga-tensorflow/)).
- Monga says the core is still monolithic and splitting it with clean interfaces is the next challenge. Governance has moved to RFCs and special interest groups, with more transparency on direction ([Monga](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rajat-monga-tensorflow/)).

### TensorFlow 2.0, Keras and enterprise needs

- With several competing high-level APIs confusing users, the team settled on François Chollet's Keras as the standard for 2.0, with eager execution by default ([Monga](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rajat-monga-tensorflow/)).
- Monga acknowledges PyTorch's research-first approach was a useful competitor that sped up eager execution ([Monga](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rajat-monga-tensorflow/)).
- He argues enterprises mostly want stability, structured-data predictions and full pipelines rather than the newest models, and advises starting with something basic that works ([Monga](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rajat-monga-tensorflow/)).

### Affective computing and what sensors can read

- Picard defines affective computing as any computing “that relates to arises from or deliberately influences human emotion”, and says the problem is as hard as she predicted ([Picard](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rosalind-picard-affective-computing-emotion-privacy-and-health/)).
- She says a laptop camera can pick up heart rate and breathing from a neutral face, but thoughts and nuanced feelings stay private ([Picard](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rosalind-picard-affective-computing-emotion-privacy-and-health/)).
- Picard sees wearables as the best modality for health uses. Empatica's Embrace wristband is FDA cleared, and she calls getting clearance far harder than publishing ([Picard](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rosalind-picard-affective-computing-emotion-privacy-and-health/)).

### Privacy, consent and regulation

- Picard worries about technology that reads affect non-contact and without consent, and says use of face reading in places like China made her pull back on some research ([Picard](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rosalind-picard-affective-computing-emotion-privacy-and-health/)).
- She says “control is one of the biggest factors” in reducing a person's stress about being sensed. Her lab built a way to jam heart rate signals in video as well as extract them ([Picard](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rosalind-picard-affective-computing-emotion-privacy-and-health/)).
- She would accept data ownership, limits on emotion recognition in hiring and a firewall between agents that read emotion and agents that sell things ([Picard](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/06/rosalind-picard-affective-computing-emotion-privacy-and-health/)).
