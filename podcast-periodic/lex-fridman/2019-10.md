## Overview

October had three episodes. Two looked back at landmark contests between IBM machines and humans, Watson on Jeopardy and Deep Blue against Garry Kasparov, and drew lessons about what machine intelligence is. The third was a speculative tour of aliens, space travel and the future of humans with Michio Kaku.

## Main themes

### What counts as machine intelligence

- David Ferrucci separates pattern-based prediction from reasoning that can be explained. He calls a system that predicts well but cannot explain itself an alien intelligence, and wants machines that act as thought partners ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).
- Kasparov argues machines beat him not by understanding chess better but by making fewer mistakes. He calls AlphaZero a first step towards machine-generated knowledge, but says machines cannot tell which questions are relevant ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).
- Kasparov expects machines to prevail in closed systems, so the real question is how humans add value alongside them. He says autonomous vehicles should be judged on making fewer mistakes than humans ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).
- Ferrucci's main worry is giving machines too much leverage, such as amplifying persuasion. He guesses general AI is closer to 20 years away than 10 ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).

### Inside the IBM contests

- Watson ran on roughly 2,000 to 3,000 cores with its corpus in memory, generating thousands of candidate answers and applying hundreds of scorers. A component stayed only if it improved end-to-end accuracy ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).
- Ferrucci recounts how IBM executives turned the Jeopardy challenge down twice before he won approval in 2006 and delivered in four years ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).
- Kasparov says the 1997 Deep Blue loss was the first match he ever lost. He admits he played poorly but believes the match gave IBM unfair advantages ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).

### The future of humans in space

- Michio Kaku expects humans to pick up alien signals this century, citing thousands of known exoplanets. He extends the Kardashev scale to types 4 and 5 ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).
- Kaku predicts a brain net carrying emotions, digitised minds sent by laser, humans on Mars in the 2030s and fusion as the backbone of a type 1 civilisation. He is explicit that some of this is personal speculation ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).
- He presents string theory as the only surviving candidate for unifying quantum mechanics and relativity, while allowing that it could be wrong ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).

### Politics and history

- Kasparov argues centrally planned systems cannot drive innovation and that communism was never condemned as fascism was. He says he lives in New York for safety; Fridman raised election interference without taking a position ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).
- Kaku, unusually among scientists, he says, believes technology has a moral direction and that the internet spreads democracy ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).
