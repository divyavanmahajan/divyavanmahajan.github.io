## Overview

October brought two conversations about how worried we should be about AI and what it still cannot do. Steven Pinker took apart the common doom scenarios, while Yoshua Bengio steered attention to near-term harms and to the research gaps that keep neural nets from real understanding.

## Main themes

### Existential risk and near-term harms

- Pinker argues the two usual AI doom stories, machine takeover and collateral damage from a badly specified goal, are both incoherent. He says the takeover fear “confuses intelligence with a will to power”, a bundle that natural selection gave humans ([Pinker](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/steven-pinker-ai-in-the-age-of-reason/)).
- He calls the paperclip scenario self-defeating and rejects analogies with nuclear weapons, whose purpose is destruction. He relies on an engineering culture that tests systems before giving them control, backed by legal responsibility for careless engineers ([Pinker](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/steven-pinker-ai-in-the-age-of-reason/)).
- Bengio considers existential risk very unlikely but worth academic study. He wants public debate to focus on face-recognition surveillance, autonomous weapons, jobs, concentration of power and discrimination ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).

### Bias, jobs and regulation

- Bengio says debiasing methods such as adversarial training are mature enough for governments to require them of companies like insurers, because firms will not accept lower accuracy voluntarily ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).
- Pinker sees the jobs AI may remove, such as picking crops and mining coal, as a boon and expects income can be redistributed. He warns that fatalism about imagined threats crowds out attention to likely ones such as pandemics, nuclear war and climate change ([Pinker](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/steven-pinker-ai-in-the-age-of-reason/)).
- Both Pinker and Fridman agree autonomous vehicles could cut the roughly 40,000 US road deaths a year ([Pinker](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/steven-pinker-ai-in-the-age-of-reason/)).

### What deep learning still lacks

- Bengio says today's nets have an understanding that is “very basic it's it's very low-level”, and that scale alone will not fix it ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).
- He argues the lever is training objectives and frameworks, not data or architecture: moving from passive observation to agents that learn by intervening ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).
- Models need millions of examples for simple grid worlds that humans learn from dozens, which he says leaves room for academics without Google-scale compute ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).
- Pinker sees current systems as extracting statistical regularities without a semantic level, though nothing in principle stops silicon from matching a brain ([Pinker](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/steven-pinker-ai-in-the-age-of-reason/)).

### Causal structure, memory and credit assignment

- Bengio wants representations in which causal factors are separated, and argues the mechanisms relating those variables must be disentangled too, like the well-factorised rules of classical expert systems ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).
- He contrasts recurrent nets, which handle dozens or hundreds of time steps, with humans who revise judgments about something done last year. He links this to selective memory and consciousness ([Bengio](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/10/yoshua-bengio-deep-learning/)).
