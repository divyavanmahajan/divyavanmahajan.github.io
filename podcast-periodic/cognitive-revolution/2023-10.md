## Overview

October on The Cognitive Revolution mixed builders and research reviews. Swyx, Yohei Nakajima, Aravind Srinivas and Anton Troynikov talked about agents, search and retrieval in practice, while Nathan Labenz ran three research roundups on compute, long context and AI control. Two episodes looked at AI in the hands of novelists and historians.

## Main themes

### Agents and the AI engineer

- Swyx defines the AI engineer as a software engineer who applies models through APIs, and says most come from software rather than ML ([Swyx](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/ai-engineers-pendants-and-competition-between-openai-and-developers-wi/)).
- He prefers narrow agents such as Calendly-style scheduling and Code Interpreter, and warns agents may stay “5 to 10 years away from full self-driving” for a while ([Swyx](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/ai-engineers-pendants-and-competition-between-openai-and-developers-wi/)).
- Yohei Nakajima built BabyAGI as a weekend loop of about 105 lines. He advises pointing agents first at tasks you wish you did but skip ([Yohei Nakajima](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/ai-agents-vc-insights-on-ai-and-building-in-public-with-yohei-nakajima/)).
- As a VC, Nakajima sees wrappers, LLM-structured data with higher margins, and scaled services as the main business models ([Yohei Nakajima](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/ai-agents-vc-insights-on-ai-and-building-in-public-with-yohei-nakajima/)).

### Platforms, search and retrieval

- Swyx doubts OpenAI's promise not to compete with developers is ironclad and says startups must out-ship it ([Swyx](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/ai-engineers-pendants-and-competition-between-openai-and-developers-wi/)).
- Aravind Srinivas says Perplexity has grown 6 to 7x since March and is replacing borrowed parts with its own index and models. He argues Google's real customers are advertisers ([Perplexity](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/gunning-for-google-with-perplexity-ceo-aravind-srinivas/)).
- Anton Troynikov of Chroma says distracting context “does tend to measurably destroy the performance” of RAG apps, so teams should measure retrieval first ([RAG & Beyond](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/rag-beyond-semantic-storage-and-retrieval/)).

### Compute and the future of the transformer

- Labenz estimates Inflection's 22,000-H100 cluster could train a GPT-4-scale model in about 5 days, letting labs combine many advances. Trey Kollmer notes surplus compute simply becomes more experiments ([Transformer Pt 1](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/the-future-of-the-transformer-pt-1-w-trey-kollmer-nvidia-s-h100-chips/)).
- Microsoft's STOP paper showed GPT-4 improving its own code, and sometimes removing a sandbox flag ([Transformer Pt 1](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/the-future-of-the-transformer-pt-1-w-trey-kollmer-nvidia-s-h100-chips/)).
- Pause tokens, attention sinks and ring attention point to much longer context. Labenz thinks 10-million-token training could let models learn things experts don't know ([Transformer Pt 2](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/the-future-of-the-transformer-pt-2-with-trey-kollmer/)).

### Jailbreaks and control

- Labenz reviews representation engineering and Anthropic's monosemanticity work as real progress in reading model internals ([Scouting Report](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/the-ai-scouting-report-jailbreaks-and-defense/)).
- He sets that against jailbreaks via low-resource languages and Shadow Alignment, where about one GPU hour fine-tunes Llama 2's safety out. He concludes control is still far from reliable ([Scouting Report](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/the-ai-scouting-report-jailbreaks-and-defense/)).

### Writers and historians

- James Yu argues Sudowrite should keep the writer in charge. His launch video drew a wave of hate during the Writers Guild strike, and he describes witch hunts against authors who use AI ([Sudowrite](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/ai-is-supercharging-writers-with-sudowrite-s-founder-james-yu/)).
- Mark Humphries says fine-tuned models have just started to speed up archival research. He says what “AI opens up as a possibility is going through everything eventually” ([AI for History](/podcasts/cognitive-revolution/2023/10/hardcore-ai-for-history-with-mark-humphries-history-professor-at-wilfr/)).
