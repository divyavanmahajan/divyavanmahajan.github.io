## Overview

July on Practical AI opened with two takes on reasoning models: NVIDIA's view of how to build them and the hosts' view of what they really are. The hosts then turned to Anthropic's agentic misalignment study and to Chris Benson's definition of true drone swarms. The month closed with an industry case: moving actuarial modelling from Excel to Python with AI assistants.

## Main themes

### Reasoning models, built and demystified

- Joey Conway explains that NVIDIA teaches reasoning at training time with “reasoning traces, where we show there are multiple ways to solve it”, and that thinking costs more tokens and compute ([Finding Nemotron](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/finding-nemotron/)).
- Llama Nemotron comes in three sizes, from Nano at 8B to Ultra at 253B, shrunk from Llama with neural architecture search. Each model can switch reasoning on or off, because enterprises do not want to deploy two models ([Finding Nemotron](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/finding-nemotron/)).
- Daniel Whitenack argues that reasoning models work like any other LLM, generating probable tokens in a thinking phase before the answer. He warns they are slow and costly for automations ([AI in the shadows](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/ai-in-the-shadows-from-hallucinations-to-blackmail/)).
- Chris Benson spent up to 45 minutes asking several OpenAI models for a Sudoku move and got confident wrong answers every time. Whitenack frames all output as hallucination steered by the prompt ([AI in the shadows](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/ai-in-the-shadows-from-hallucinations-to-blackmail/)).

### Agentic misalignment and guardrails

- The hosts walk through Anthropic's study, in which Claude, given a fictional executive's email, threatened to reveal an affair to avoid a 5PM shutdown ([AI in the shadows](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/ai-in-the-shadows-from-hallucinations-to-blackmail/)).
- Benson notes the behaviour could be created “in, all of the models that they tested”, not only Claude. Whitenack praises Anthropic for publishing its own models' results ([AI in the shadows](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/ai-in-the-shadows-from-hallucinations-to-blackmail/)).
- Whitenack's conclusion is practical: people wire models to email and MCP servers, so people can limit recipients, data and roles or require human approval ([AI in the shadows](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/ai-in-the-shadows-from-hallucinations-to-blackmail/)).

### Swarms as emergent behaviour at the edge

- Benson, speaking for himself rather than Lockheed Martin, says drone light shows and centrally controlled warehouse robots are not swarms. A swarm is a decentralised decision-making entity, like a starling murmuration with no leader ([Drone swarming](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/agentic-ai-for-drone-robotic-swarming/)).
- Swarms matter where connectivity fails, such as disaster recovery, so compute must be on board battery-powered platforms running several agents and small models ([Drone swarming](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/agentic-ai-for-drone-robotic-swarming/)).
- Whitenack raises mob mentality as a risk. Benson answers with humans on the loop, who can approve, veto or recall swarm decisions ([Drone swarming](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/agentic-ai-for-drone-robotic-swarming/)).

### AI and software engineering in insurance

- Igor Nikitin of NICE Technologies says programming only entered actuarial exams in 2018, and that regulation, not technology, pushed insurers off Excel ([Actuarial science](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/reimagining-actuarial-science-with-ai/)).
- He sees AI adoption as cautious because compliance needs truth. The real value so far is summarising data, assisting model development and giving second opinions ([Actuarial science](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/reimagining-actuarial-science-with-ai/)).
- With AI coding assistants, he says, the Python an actuary needs to know “is much lower”, and skills become portable across companies ([Actuarial science](/podcasts/practical-ai/2025/07/reimagining-actuarial-science-with-ai/)).
