## Overview

June on Practical AI was about understanding what models do and what they can be used for. Two guests covered interpretability, one through interactive visualisation and one through Seldon's open source explanation library. The hosts explained GANs, reinforcement learning and transfer learning, then turned to the risks of deepfakes.

## Main themes

### Interpretability for trust and deployment

- Andreas Madsen recalls a Danish bank whose loan model beat its advisors, who still distrusted it. He says “the really big challenge was actually that they couldn't communicate it” ([Visualizing RNNs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/visualizing-and-understanding-rnns/)).
- Janis Klaise argues production monitoring should include explanations, outlier detection and drift detection, not just requests per second. Seldon built Alibi because existing explanation code was mostly hard-to-use research code ([Seldon](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/model-inspection-and-interpretation-at-seldon/)).
- Alibi mirrors scikit-learn, with explainer.explain on a single instance. Klaise says the ideal is to become “the Scikit-learn of model explanations” ([Seldon](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/model-inspection-and-interpretation-at-seldon/)).
- He explains the anchors method on the US census income dataset, where a few features kept the same prediction 95% of the time ([Seldon](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/model-inspection-and-interpretation-at-seldon/)).

### Seeing inside recurrent networks

- Madsen lists three ingredients for good explanation: an understandable dataset, a good visualisation and a fast interactive feedback loop ([Visualizing RNNs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/visualizing-and-understanding-rnns/)).
- On an autocomplete task, he found an LSTM better at short-term memory and a GRU better at long-term context despite almost equal scores. He warns this is dataset-specific ([Visualizing RNNs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/visualizing-and-understanding-rnns/)).
- He describes Distill as using the web's interactivity instead of PDFs; his article took about eight months from writing to publication ([Visualizing RNNs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/visualizing-and-understanding-rnns/)).

### Training approaches, not model types

- Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson explain that reinforcement learning, GANs and transfer learning wrap ordinary networks such as CNNs inside a larger loop or workflow ([GANs, RL and transfer learning](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/gans-rl-and-transfer-learning-oh-my/)).
- Benson describes a GAN as a counterfeiter (generator) against a policeman (discriminator), tracing it to Ian Goodfellow's 2014 paper ([GANs, RL and transfer learning](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/gans-rl-and-transfer-learning-oh-my/)).
- Both hosts stress that transfer learning saves data, GPU time and cost, citing BERT and GPT-2 among the examples ([GANs, RL and transfer learning](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/gans-rl-and-transfer-learning-oh-my/)).

### Deepfakes and the response

- Whitenack says what is new is accessibility: faking media needs little expertise beyond some hours of cloud compute and sometimes a pre-trained model ([Deepfakes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/exposing-the-deception-of-deepfakes/)).
- Drawing on that day's House Intelligence Committee hearing, Benson notes that corrections reach fewer people than the original fake. He describes deepfakes as a tool of information warfare ([Deepfakes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/exposing-the-deception-of-deepfakes/)).
- Whitenack points to detection research on eye blinking and per-frame inconsistencies, and to OpenAI's staged GPT-2 release, which Benson now judges responsible ([Deepfakes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/exposing-the-deception-of-deepfakes/)).
- The hearing's six proposals included digital verification signatures and platform labelling. Whitenack doubts social media companies will fully cooperate ([Deepfakes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/06/exposing-the-deception-of-deepfakes/)).
