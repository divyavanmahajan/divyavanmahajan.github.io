## Overview

March on Practical AI had three guest episodes with little overlap. A former White House adviser made the case for compute as the strategic lever in AI policy, a former AI sceptic explained how he learned to code with agents, and an edge AI engineer described what changes when models run outside the cloud.

## Main themes

### Compute, chips and US-China competition

- Ben Buchanan says his team's 2021 insight was that computing power, not data, drove AI progress. That makes AI physical (chips and power), which governments can control ([AI policy](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-policy-and-the-battle-for-computing-power/)).
- He traces chip controls from Trump's first term to Biden's tighter rules, and calls today's willingness to sell advanced chips to China the bigger break. He argues DeepSeek's own paper admits limited compute holds it back ([AI policy](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-policy-and-the-battle-for-computing-power/)).
- Buchanan says “The only way America loses is if it folds and and does things like sell chips to China and the like.” He wants the lead spent on safety rather than an arms race ([AI policy](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-policy-and-the-battle-for-computing-power/)).

### Democracies, norms and safety

- Buchanan judges democracies on invention, adoption and using AI in line with their values. He feels best about invention ([AI policy](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-policy-and-the-battle-for-computing-power/)).
- He points to the political declaration on military autonomy, signed by about 58 countries, and uses early railroads to argue that safety rules eventually make technology faster ([AI policy](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-policy-and-the-battle-for-computing-power/)).

### Agentic coding as a skill

- Steve Klabnik went from calling himself an AI hater to building a programming language, Rue, largely with Claude. He says “agentic programming is a skill that takes time to get good at and you need to practice it.” ([Agentic coding](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/humility-in-the-age-of-agentic-coding/)).
- His second attempt at Rue worked because he wrote a spec first and built a test framework so the agent could check its own work ([Agentic coding](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/humility-in-the-age-of-agentic-coding/)).
- He merged 100 Rue PRs on Christmas Day with light review, and calls keeping quality high while agents merge code the biggest open question for the profession ([Agentic coding](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/humility-in-the-age-of-agentic-coding/)).
- Klabnik argues many industry practices are beliefs rather than evidence. He cares less about DRY now, and suggests good code for an LLM may differ from good code for a human ([Agentic coding](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/humility-in-the-age-of-agentic-coding/)).

### Edge AI as its own environment

- Brandon Shibley of Edge Impulse lists the edge constraints as size, power, connectivity, cost, reliability, latency and privacy. He describes privacy as both a limit and a selling point, since sensitive data can stay on the device ([Edge AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-at-the-edge-is-a-different-operating-environment/)).
- To save power, a cheap detector such as YOLO can discard “99% of the frames” before crops go to a vision language model ([Edge AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-at-the-edge-is-a-different-operating-environment/)).
- Edge hardware is fragmented, so Edge Impulse abstracts it during training and optimises for the target at deployment. Shibley sees ROI pressure as a healthy filter on edge use cases ([Edge AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/03/ai-at-the-edge-is-a-different-operating-environment/)).
