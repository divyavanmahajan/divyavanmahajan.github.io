## Overview

April on Practical AI ranged widely, with no topic shared across episodes. A Kaggle grandmaster, a Hugging Face engineer, a causal inference researcher and an art historian each looked at what machine learning can and cannot do. The common thread was method: faster experiments, prompting instead of training, and asking the right kind of question.

## Main themes

### Faster pipelines win competitions

- Christof Henkel of NVIDIA, a triple Kaggle grandmaster, says the edge at the top comes from running more experiments: “Most of the time, I'm not running out of ideas, but I'm running out of time in the competition.” ([Kaggle grandmaster](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/accelerated-data-science-with-a-kaggle-grandmaster/)).
- He spends the first two weeks of each competition optimising his workflow on a modular, Docker-based setup. He cites NVIDIA RAPIDS (cuDF and cuML) and says GPU data loading can be about 100x faster ([Kaggle grandmaster](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/accelerated-data-science-with-a-kaggle-grandmaster/)).
- Henkel compares a three-month competition to a prototype project at work. The two big differences are data acquisition and agreeing a business metric ([Kaggle grandmaster](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/accelerated-data-science-with-a-kaggle-grandmaster/)).

### In-context learning and open models

- Rajiv Shah of Hugging Face explains that a large model can classify reviews from three examples in the prompt, with no retraining. Chained prompts, for example with LangChain, can replace several task-specific models ([Capabilities of LLMs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/capabilities-of-llms/)).
- Shah points to HuggingGPT and Auto-GPT as early agents, which a year earlier he would have put three or four years away ([Capabilities of LLMs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/capabilities-of-llms/)).
- He argues enterprises “want full control kind of over their data that's going into the model and how it's being used”, which pushes them towards open, fine-tunable models ([Capabilities of LLMs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/capabilities-of-llms/)).

### Causal questions need causal methods

- Paul Hünermund of Copenhagen Business School says most executive questions ask what happens if they act, while standard machine learning only finds correlations ([Causal inference](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/causal-inference/)).
- He covers experiments such as A/B tests and observational methods such as difference-in-differences and directed acyclic graphs. He stresses that background knowledge is needed to rule out other explanations ([Causal inference](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/causal-inference/)).
- For starting points he recommends Microsoft's DoWhy library and The Book of Why, and says getting closer to causal is often good enough ([Causal inference](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/causal-inference/)).

### Computer vision as art history

- Amanda Wasielewski of Uppsala University argues that computer scientists classifying art by visual features are repeating early formalist art history ([Art historians](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/computer-scientists-as-rogue-art-historians/)).
- She says style labels such as action painting and abstract expressionism are rival critics' terms, not distinct classes. Models also treat images as flat pixels without understanding space ([Art historians](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/computer-scientists-as-rogue-art-historians/)).
- On forgery she notes that deep learning both creates and detects fakes, and that similar fears about trust in images were raised around Photoshop in the 1990s ([Art historians](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/04/computer-scientists-as-rogue-art-historians/)).
