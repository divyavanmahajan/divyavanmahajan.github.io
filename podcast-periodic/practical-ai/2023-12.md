## Overview

December on Practical AI had three separate threads and no shared topic. Lighthouse Reports journalists explained how they took apart a welfare fraud model used by the city of Rotterdam. Casper da Costa-Luis surveyed the open source AI ecosystem, and LanceDB's Chang She made the case for embedded vector search on disk.

## Main themes

### Auditing a suspicion machine

- Gabriel Geiger and Justin-Casimir Braun describe welfare risk models that score recipients from zero to one and flag the highest for investigation. Braun says they found only one country without such predictive analytics ([Suspicion machines](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/suspicion-machines/)).
- Rotterdam withheld its final model file, citing gaming risk, and released it after a year-long fight. Plotly histograms the city had supplied turned out to contain the whole training set ([Suspicion machines](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/suspicion-machines/)).
- The guests point to around 30 language-skill variables and subjective caseworker judgements, such as how women wore makeup. Braun adds that the fraud label “doesn't distinguish between intentional fraud and unintentional mistakes.” ([Suspicion machines](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/suspicion-machines/)).
- Only about 1,000 of over 12,000 past investigations were random, so Braun argues the model learned investigators' selection patterns. Rotterdam later stopped using it ([Suspicion machines](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/suspicion-machines/)).

### What open source AI actually means

- Da Costa-Luis separates three kinds of openness: model weights, training data and output licensing. He says many models are not open about training data, and output rights depend on how that data was licensed ([State of open source AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/the-state-of-open-source-ai/)).
- He notes many AI licences have never been tested in court, and admits that in practice only big, famous companies get sued ([State of open source AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/the-state-of-open-source-ai/)).
- He argues “if you're not doing some form of fine tuning, you're not producing anything of commercial value.” He prefers plain embeddings for explainability and rejects AGI talk ([State of open source AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/the-state-of-open-source-ai/)).
- The State of Open Source AI book from Prem was written by about a dozen people on GitHub, with a second volume of 10 new chapters planned ([State of open source AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/the-state-of-open-source-ai/)).

### Vector search as data infrastructure

- Chang She says LanceDB started two years earlier as computer vision data infrastructure, not as a vector database. The open source community found its index useful for generative AI ([LanceDB](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/open-source-on-disk-vector-search-with-lancedb/)).
- He argues Parquet lacks fast random access, so the team built the Lance columnar format and put indexes on disk rather than in memory. LanceDB runs in-process, like SQLite or DuckDB ([LanceDB](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/open-source-on-disk-vector-search-with-lancedb/)).
- Separating compute from storage lets stateless query nodes read tables on S3. Some users hold up to several billion vectors in one table ([LanceDB](/podcasts/practical-ai/2023/12/open-source-on-disk-vector-search-with-lancedb/)).
