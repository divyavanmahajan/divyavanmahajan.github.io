## Overview

September on Practical AI had four guest episodes with no shared thread. Waymo explained what it takes to scale driverless cars, Mozilla described open speech data for under-served languages, and two lawyers and data scientists made the case for AI incident response. NVIDIA closed the month with a look at how AI education grew and where it should go.

## Main themes

### Scaling autonomous driving

- Drago Anguelov of Waymo says launching a safe, fully driverless product is “unlike most products.” Waymo can learn a specific area well; the open work is new cities, weather and rare events ([Waymo](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/getting-waymo-into-autonomous-driving/)).
- He describes a modular stack of maps, localization, perception, behaviour prediction and planning, and calls it an integration problem ([Waymo](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/getting-waymo-into-autonomous-driving/)).
- Because other road users are not deterministic, the car must prepare for many outcomes. His MultiPath work uses about 128 static anchors to avoid mode averaging ([Waymo](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/getting-waymo-into-autonomous-driving/)).
- Anguelov cites about 1.3 million road deaths a year, mostly from human error, but expects adoption to be gradual ([Waymo](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/getting-waymo-into-autonomous-driving/)).

### Open speech data for every language

- Jenny Zhang says Common Voice's June 2020 release had 7,200 recorded hours in 54 languages, about 5,600 validated by volunteers ([Mozilla](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/speech-tech-and-common-voice-at-mozilla/)).
- Zhang says the platform must be self-serve because the team “can't cover all of the 7,000 languages in the world.” Josh Meyer notes narrow tasks can adapt an English model with almost no data ([Mozilla](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/speech-tech-and-common-voice-at-mozilla/)).
- Remy Muhire describes speech tools for Kinyarwanda, including a COVID-19 chatbot. Meyer's bias corpus finds accent and gender matter much more than age ([Mozilla](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/speech-tech-and-common-voice-at-mozilla/)).

### When AI goes wrong

- Patrick Hall of BNH.ai groups AI incidents into discrimination, privacy, security and systems that offer no appeal ([When AI goes wrong](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/when-ai-goes-wrong/)).
- Andrew Burt says there is no national AI law, but discrimination, privacy, negligence and product liability laws already apply. Hall expects regulation soon ([When AI goes wrong](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/when-ai-goes-wrong/)).
- Hall says neither cyber incident response nor model risk management covers ML failures. Burt argues that “it just does not cost a lot of money to prepare, and it costs a huge amount of money to respond” ([When AI goes wrong](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/when-ai-goes-wrong/)).
- Hall recommends debugging models like software, with sensitivity analysis, security audits and simple benchmark models ([When AI goes wrong](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/when-ai-goes-wrong/)).

### Teaching deep learning

- Will Ramey of NVIDIA traces the deep learning boom to researchers finding GPUs well suited to training neural networks, helped by open frameworks and cloud GPU instances ([Learning about Deep Learning](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/learning-about-deep-learning/)).
- He says the Deep Learning Institute has trained over 250,000 people and only builds courses once practice is settled, as happened with recommender systems ([Learning about Deep Learning](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/learning-about-deep-learning/)).
- Ramey wants school-age children to grasp how AI works, and AI to adapt teaching to individual learning styles ([Learning about Deep Learning](/podcasts/practical-ai/2020/09/learning-about-deep-learning/)).
