## Overview

July on Turing Post came down to one week and one model. Ksenia Se used Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 to examine what open weights now mean: a commercial and political strategy for Chinese labs, and a choice for the organisations big enough to run trillion-parameter models.

## Main themes

### Kimi K3, demand and the compute ceiling

- Three days after launch, Moonshot paused new K3 subscriptions because demand was pushing its GPUs to their limits. It also split membership into app and Kimi Code tiers ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).
- Se explains that agentic coding sessions run for hours with large contexts. “Each one is a small continuous compute obligation” ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).
- Moonshot is preparing a Hong Kong IPO at a potential valuation above $30 billion, with ARR reportedly at $300 million in June, up from $200 million in April. Se argues a sellout works as proof of demand before a listing ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).
- K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters and routes 16 of 896 experts per token, but all of them must be stored. Even at 4 bits the weights need about 1.4 TB, and Moonshot recommends at least 64 accelerators ([Too Big to Run](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/open-source-ai-is-getting-too-big-to-run/)).

### Openness as strategy and the politics of permission

- Se sees three logics behind China's openness. Open weights reach Western customers that a closed Chinese API cannot, free models create demand for domestic inference hardware, and the state offers intelligence as infrastructure rather than as a subscription ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).
- She puts it bluntly: “For Chinese labs, openness is not generosity. It's the only distribution channel that works, and it is working now” ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).
- Xi Jinping opened WAIC for the first time and framed open models as a public good. Alibaba teased an open Qwen 3.8, and 29 countries agreed to form WAICO ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).
- Citing Axios, Se says the Trump administration may ban Chinese models. A ban could reach APIs, app stores and procurement but not weights already downloaded. In her reading, the constraint on open AI moved from capability to compute to permission ([Kimi K3 Sold Out](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/kimi-k3-just-sold-out-then-it-got-political/)).

### Open models as operator choice

- K3 topped the Frontend Code Arena, but Se notes it scores 57 on the Artificial Analysis index against Fable 5's 60, and Moonshot admits it trails the best closed models ([Too Big to Run](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/open-source-ai-is-getting-too-big-to-run/)).
- Thinking Machines' Inkling (975B total, 41B active, Apache 2.0) scores 41, and the company says openly it is not the strongest model. Se says the business is Tinker, which fine-tunes Kimi, DeepSeek, Qwen and OpenAI models too: “The company is building around the ability to change models, including models it did not train” ([Too Big to Run](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/open-source-ai-is-getting-too-big-to-run/)).
- Inkling follows DeepSeek-V3's architecture and used some Kimi K2.5 synthetic data. Se concludes that open models now mean choice for organisations that can operate them, not small and cheap ([Too Big to Run](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/07/open-source-ai-is-getting-too-big-to-run/)).
