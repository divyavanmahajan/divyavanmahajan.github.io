## Overview

March on The Moonshot Podcast covered two X areas: health products that graduated into companies, and Materra, X's moonshot for recycling. In both, Astro Teller and his guests framed adoption as the real test, whether that means fitting into daily life or making recycling profitable.

## Main themes

### Health technology that disappears into daily life

- Teller uses three X graduates (Skip, Project Iris and Verily) to argue that health technology works best when people barely notice it, as with something they wear ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).
- Kathryn Zealand describes Skip's MO/GO, built with Arc'teryx, as an e-bike for walking. User research set the requirements: hands free, not embarrassing and useful every day. Anna Roumiantseva says Skip spun out of X in 2023 ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).
- Project Iris, a contact-lens effort, fed into a commercial product, and Will Biederman expects wearable sensing to move towards proactive at-home care ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).
- Verily CEO Stephen Gillett wants to bring precision health care to everybody. He says most personal health data sits behind institutional firewalls and argues for a medical-grade cloud for health data ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).

### How X kills and reshapes projects

- Zealand says X narrows hundreds of projects to a handful by getting good at killing them against agreed criteria ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).
- Skip's demand for washable fabric actuators failed, but Zealand says the team reread the criterion: “comfortable is definitely a kill criteria, but it doesn't matter how it's comfortable” ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).
- Teller notes that Verily has sold off some sub-businesses to focus, as the CEO role passed from Andy Conrad to Gillett ([Supercharging Human Health](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/supercharging-human-health/)).

### Recycling has not failed; materials outran it

- Rey Banatao says “less than 10% of the world's plastics” have ever been recycled, in a $600 billion industry ([Turning Trash into Treasure](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/the-moonshot-podcast-deep-dive-rey-banatao-on-turning-trash-into-treas/)).
- He argues consumer materials have changed faster than a system built for newspaper and glass. The US has more than 9,000 recycling systems, and what facilities accept varies widely ([Turning Trash into Treasure](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/the-moonshot-podcast-deep-dive-rey-banatao-on-turning-trash-into-treas/)).
- Banatao defines circularity as putting important materials back into circulation, potentially indefinitely ([Turning Trash into Treasure](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/the-moonshot-podcast-deep-dive-rey-banatao-on-turning-trash-into-treas/)).

### Molecular data, AI sorting and policy

- Materra combines infrared spectroscopy, computer vision and fast compute to classify objects on conveyor belts in milliseconds ([Turning Trash into Treasure](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/the-moonshot-podcast-deep-dive-rey-banatao-on-turning-trash-into-treas/)).
- A few percent of additives can ruin pyrolysis, which turns plastic back into oil without incineration. Banatao says Materra “delivered a tanker truck full of that feedstock” from plastic bound for landfill in Oregon ([Turning Trash into Treasure](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/the-moonshot-podcast-deep-dive-rey-banatao-on-turning-trash-into-treas/)).
- He expects molecular data to support extended producer responsibility laws, approved in five US states, and names textiles and e-waste as next targets ([Turning Trash into Treasure](/podcasts/moonshot/2026/03/the-moonshot-podcast-deep-dive-rey-banatao-on-turning-trash-into-treas/)).
