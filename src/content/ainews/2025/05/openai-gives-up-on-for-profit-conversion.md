## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, a daily podcast and video series on AI news, covers OpenAI's decision to drop its plan to convert into a for-profit company. Under the revised plan, OpenAI's for-profit subsidiary still becomes a public benefit corporation (PBC), but the nonprofit keeps oversight and control as a large shareholder. The host argues the story matters because OpenAI is one of a few companies with an outsized influence on where AI goes, and its legal structure affects rights, responsibilities and accountability. A headlines segment first covers OpenAI's reported deal to buy Windsurf, Cursor's new funding round and Goldman Sachs' advice on AI stocks.

The transcript does not name the host or give an affiliation. No source video URL was provided.

## Prerequisites

- **Corporate structures:** the difference between nonprofit and for-profit companies, and what a Delaware public benefit corporation is.
- **OpenAI's history:** founded as a nonprofit lab in 2015, it set up a "capped-profit" subsidiary in 2019, and Microsoft is a major investor.
- **Venture capital terms:** valuation, funding rounds, annual recurring revenue (ARR), dual-class shares, clawback provisions and IPOs.
- **The AI coding tool market:** products such as Cursor, Windsurf, Claude and Gemini, and why being able to switch between models matters to developers.
- **Elon Musk v. OpenAI:** a basic awareness of Musk's lawsuit against OpenAI and his company xAI.
- **State Attorney General oversight:** the Attorneys General of Delaware and California oversee charitable organisations based in or operating in their states.

## Main Points

### Headline 1: OpenAI agrees to buy Windsurf for about $3 billion
- According to Bloomberg sources, an agreement has been reached, but the deal has not formally closed. OpenAI had reportedly tried several times to buy Cursor before turning to Windsurf.
- Windsurf was valued at $1.25 billion in its August round and had been in talks with Kleiner Perkins and General Catalyst to raise money at a $3 billion valuation.
- This would be OpenAI's largest acquisition so far. It shows how important coding is as an AI use case. OpenAI has reportedly been aiming at a fully automated coding agent priced in the tens of thousands of dollars per month.
- Nimrod Kramer, CEO of Daily.dev, argues the deal is about "owning the developer": distribution, attention, a data flywheel and a way into developer teams that lean towards Claude or Cursor.
- Critics such as Nick Dobos say Windsurf could suffer if its users lose access to Claude or Gemini, because Cursor's main selling point is that users can switch models. The host notes that OpenAI has recently shown it will work with community standards such as MCP, so this outcome is not certain.

### Headline 2: Anysphere (Cursor) raises $900 million at a $9 billion valuation
- Thrive Capital led the round, with Andreessen Horowitz and Accel taking part. The previous round, in January, raised $105 million at a $2.5 billion valuation.
- The Financial Times reports ARR of $200 million in April. Others suggest it is already about $300 million. ARR was running at a $48 million pace in October.
- The host's conclusion: the revenue numbers now back up the hype around coding assistants.

### Headline 3: Goldman Sachs says to "buy the dip" on AI stocks
- Analysts say worries about DeepSeek and about overbuilding infrastructure are fading. Big tech's earnings showed strong growth in AI-related business lines.
- Microsoft is pulling back slightly on data center spending. It says this is about preparing for a possible glut in a few years, not about current conditions. The other big tech companies are increasing capital spending even though tariffs have raised construction costs.
- Goldman says AI baskets are cheaper than at the start of this year or last year, closer to pre-ChatGPT levels relative to earnings. A year earlier, the bank had published a skeptical report titled "Gen AI: too much spend, too little benefit?"

### Main story: Why OpenAI's structure is worth following
- OpenAI shapes the future of AI, and through it the future more broadly.
- Different legal structures mean different rights and responsibilities. The host says he will focus on these points rather than the personalities involved.

### Background: The December 2024 restructuring plan
- In a blog post on 27 December, "Why OpenAI's Structure Must Evolve to Advance Our Mission," OpenAI set three objectives: pick the structure best for the mission, make the nonprofit sustainable, and equip each arm to do its part.
- History given in the post: OpenAI realised that advanced AI would need more and more compute, and therefore far more capital than donations could provide. In 2019 it created a bespoke capped-profit structure, a for-profit controlled by the nonprofit. At the time it estimated it would need about $10 billion. It raised $100 million, then $1 billion from Microsoft.
- The December plan had three parts:
  1. Convert the for-profit into a Delaware PBC with ordinary shares.
  2. Give the nonprofit shares in the PBC.
  3. Separate the two, so that the PBC runs the business and the nonprofit pursues charitable work in healthcare, education and science.
- The plan meant the nonprofit would no longer run the for-profit.

### Elon Musk's opposition and the legal fight
- In February, Musk offered to buy OpenAI for $97.4 billion, saying it should return to being an "open-source, safety-focused force for good."
- Altman replied publicly with an offer to buy Twitter for $9.74 billion. Internally, he told staff the structure ensures "no one individual can take control of OpenAI."
- Musk's lawyer, Marc Toberoff, argued that if OpenAI became fully for-profit, the charity must be fairly compensated for giving up control over the technology.
- On 14 March, OpenAI published a blog post accusing Musk of acting in his own and xAI's interest. It also said he had once wanted to merge a for-profit OpenAI into Tesla. A court had denied Musk a preliminary injunction and dismissed several of his claims. OpenAI stated that "there is no for-profit conversion in the cards" and that the nonprofit was "not going anywhere."
- On 1 May, a judge narrowed Musk's lawsuit. The fraud claims and the unjust enrichment claim against OpenAI and Microsoft can proceed. The false advertising and breach of fiduciary duty claims were dismissed. The host calls this a mixed result, which was bad for OpenAI because it was not a full dismissal.

### The new announcement: The nonprofit keeps control
- Board chair Bret Taylor wrote that the decision came after hearing from civic leaders and talking with the offices of the Attorneys General of Delaware and California.
- In a letter to staff, Sam Altman wrote that "OpenAI is not a normal company and never will be." He described AGI as "the most capable tool in human history." He said OpenAI cannot currently supply as much AI as the world wants and that this could require hundreds of billions, and eventually trillions, of dollars.
- Altman's three goals: make OpenAI broadly available to all of humanity, build "the largest and most effective nonprofit in history," and deliver beneficial AGI, including work on safety and alignment, so that "democratic AI wins over authoritarian AI."
- Structure going forward:
  - The nonprofit keeps control.
  - The for-profit LLC still becomes a PBC.
  - The capped-profit model is replaced by "a normal capital structure where everyone has stock." Altman says the cap made sense when there seemed to be one dominant AGI effort, but not in a world of many AGI companies.
- A spokesperson confirmed that investors will hold regular stock with no cap on returns, to make future fundraising easier. The California Department of Justice said it is still reviewing the plan.

### How to read the outcome
- The Information called this a hollow victory for Musk. Blocking the restructuring does not stop OpenAI raising money, and an IPO stays feasible once investors hold regular shares.
- Investors already accept having no control at Meta and Google, which use dual-class shares. So nonprofit control may not put investors off.
- Musk's lawyer said the lawsuit will go ahead regardless.

### Remaining issues: Investors and Microsoft
- OpenAI's last two funding rounds depended on the conversion. SoftBank can walk away from $10 billion pledged in April. Investors led by Microsoft can claw back $6.6 billion raised last October. Axios' Dan Primack reports no apparent interest in asking for the money back, possibly because the profit cap has been removed.
- Microsoft is the key holdout. It has invested $13.75 billion at much lower valuations and has a revenue-sharing agreement. Citing WSJ reporting, Bloomberg's Matt Levine said Microsoft has a claim on 49% of profits up to a limit. He guessed the limit might be 10x, which would make the stake worth up to $137.5 billion.
- Only OpenAI insiders, Microsoft and a few early investors reportedly get a vote on approving the deal. Microsoft wants to rewrite the revenue share and negotiate its equity stake.
- The host's conclusion: OpenAI either decided it was fighting a losing battle or was told by the California Attorney General that it would not get approval. The most important part of the change, uncapped returns for new investors, still stands. Microsoft, Attorney General approval and Musk's lawsuit remain unresolved.

## Key Concepts

- **Public Benefit Corporation (PBC):** a for-profit company under Delaware law that must weigh a stated public benefit alongside shareholder interests.
- **Capped-profit structure:** OpenAI's 2019 model, in which a for-profit arm controlled by the nonprofit capped what investors and employees could earn, with any excess going to the nonprofit.
- **Nonprofit control:** an arrangement where the nonprofit oversees and controls the operating company as a large shareholder.
- **Normal capital structure:** ordinary shares of stock with no cap on investor returns.
- **Dual-class shares:** a share structure, used by Meta and Google, that gives founders the deciding votes while outside investors hold economic stakes.
- **Clawback provision:** a contract term letting investors reclaim money if a condition is not met. Here, the condition was the for-profit conversion.
- **Revenue-sharing agreement (Microsoft–OpenAI):** Microsoft's reported claim on 49% of OpenAI's profits up to an undisclosed limit.
- **Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR):** a company's predictable yearly subscription revenue, used here to measure Cursor's growth.
- **Model Context Protocol (MCP):** an open standard for connecting AI models to tools and data. OpenAI's support for it is cited as a sign it will work with community standards.
- **Unjust enrichment / fraud claims:** the claims in Musk's lawsuit that the court let proceed.
- **Democratic AI vs. authoritarian AI:** Altman's framing of the goal of making AI widely available to everyone rather than controlled by a few.

## Summary

The episode argues that OpenAI has given up its plan to strip the nonprofit of control, after pressure from Musk's lawsuit and talks with the Attorneys General of Delaware and California, while keeping the change that matters most commercially. The for-profit arm will become a public benefit corporation with ordinary, uncapped stock, which makes raising money and a possible IPO easier. The nonprofit will stay in control, much as founders keep control at dual-class companies like Meta and Google. Some commentators see this as a hollow victory for Musk. Three issues remain open: Microsoft is negotiating to protect its investment and revenue share, the Attorneys General have not yet approved the plan, and Musk says he will keep suing. The headlines put this in a wider context of large sums flowing into AI coding tools, shown by OpenAI's roughly $3 billion Windsurf deal and Cursor's $9 billion valuation, and of renewed confidence among investors in AI stocks.
