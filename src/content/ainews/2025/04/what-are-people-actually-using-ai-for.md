## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief** is presented by the host, NLW, who also runs Superintelligent. It asks what people are actually using AI for. It draws on two recent pieces of research. The first is Anthropic's Economic Index report, *AI's Impact on Software Development*. The second is a *Harvard Business Review* article by Marc Zao-Sanders, *How People Are Really Using Gen AI in 2025*. The host adds examples from consulting firms (McKinsey, EY, KPMG) and Superintelligent's own work assessing enterprises for agent adoption.

The topic matters because AI is still a new field. Most users feel they have only begun to find what it can do, so even a short snapshot of real usage helps people see where value is being found and where it is not.

**Source video:** No URL was given for this episode. An ad-free version is available at patreon.com/aidailybrief.

## Prerequisites

- **Large language model (LLM) assistants:** what tools like ChatGPT and Claude do.
- **Claude.ai vs. Claude Code:** Claude.ai is Anthropic's general chat interface. Claude Code is its coding agent.
- **Automation vs. augmentation:** the difference between AI doing a task itself and AI working alongside a person.
- **Software basics:** the difference between front-end work (user interfaces) and back-end work.
- **"Vibe coding" tools:** platforms like Lovable and SoftGen, where people build apps by describing them in plain language.
- **Enterprise adoption:** a general sense of how large companies take on new technology, including security reviews and compliance.

## Main Points

### 1. Why AI usage data matters

- Interest in how other people use AI is high. "How I use AI" episodes do well, and Lenny's podcast network has launched a show called *How I AI* on this theme.
- Most people start with the obvious use cases. The broad feeling is that the best uses are still to come.
- Usage studies are only snapshots, but they are useful in a field that changes this fast.

### 2. Anthropic Economic Index: AI's impact on software development

- Earlier Economic Index reports found that US workers in computer-related jobs used Claude far more than their share of the workforce would predict. This report looks at coding specifically.
- **Method:** Anthropic analysed 500,000 coding-related conversations across Claude.ai and Claude Code.
- **Finding 1: the coding agent is used mainly for automation.** 79% of Claude Code conversations were classed as automation (AI performs the task directly), compared with 49% on Claude.ai. Users treat Claude Code as an agent, not just an assistant.
- **Finding 2: AI coders mostly build user-facing apps.** JavaScript and HTML were the most common languages, and UI/UX tasks were among the most frequent. Anthropic concluded that jobs focused on simple apps and interfaces may be disrupted sooner than back-end work.
  - *The host's caveat:* this may partly reflect who is using the tools. New "vibe coders" and non-engineers tend to start by prototyping front ends. About 90% of what the host has built on Lovable and SoftGen is front-end work. That is not because the tools can't do full-stack work, but because front ends are what people try first.
- **Finding 3: startups are the early adopters.** Startups made up 33% of Claude Code conversations but only 13% of Claude.ai coding conversations. In the transcript the 13% figure is attached once to Claude Code enterprise use and once to Claude.ai startup use, so the comparison is somewhat ambiguous.

### 3. More detailed findings from the Anthropic report

- **Task categories:** Anthropic grouped coding work into categories, including software architecture and code design, UI/UX component development, debugging and performance optimisation, and web and mobile app development.
- Two of the top five tasks were UI development (12% of conversations) and web/mobile app development (8%). Anthropic linked these to **vibe coding**, where developers describe the outcome they want and let the AI handle the implementation.
- **Anthropic's projection:** as AI takes over component creation and styling, these developers may move towards higher-level design and user-experience work.
- **Who uses the tools:** about 30% of programming conversations were personal projects and about 25% were enterprise work. Students, academics, personal-project builders and tutorial or learning users together made up about half of all interactions on both platforms.
- **Anthropic's view on adoption:** the pattern mirrors past technology shifts. Startups adopt early for competitive advantage, while established organisations move cautiously behind security checks. Because AI is general-purpose, the gap between early and late adopters could become a large competitive difference.
  - *The host's view:* enterprise hesitation is not only about security. Current coding tools are built for individuals rather than large-team collaboration, and some engineers simply don't want to change habits. Startups are now trying to bring vibe coding to the enterprise, and the host expects this soon.

### 4. Humans stay in the loop more for coding

- In Anthropic's earlier analysis of non-coding tasks, only 3% of conversations involved automation with human feedback loops. For coding, the figure was 21%.
- "Directive automation" fell correspondingly. This is where the user hands off a task and returns when it is done.
- **Possible explanations:** coding is iterative by nature, or AI cannot yet build complex software in one attempt.
- The host chooses between vibe coding platforms largely on how well they interpret prompts and how well they fix things after the user explains more clearly what they meant.

### 5. Community reaction to the Anthropic findings

- Most people found the findings believable.
- University of Delaware professor Harry Wang said his experience matched all three patterns. He added that including data from Cursor, Windsurf and Cline would likely strengthen them.
- All About AI summarised it as AI doing much of the heavy lifting in software development, with developers still in the loop for the important work.

### 6. HBR: How People Are Really Using Gen AI in 2025

- **Method:** Marc Zao-Sanders repeated his 2024 approach with more data, limited to the previous 12 months. He searched online forums (Reddit, Quora) and articles for explicit, specific uses of the technology.
- **Limitation:** this is not a scientific study. It measures what people choose to *talk about*, which may differ from what they actually *do*. The host suggests treating it as one piece of evidence, not the final word.
- **2024 top 10:** generating ideas, therapy/companionship, specific search, editing text, exploring topics of interest, fun and nonsense, troubleshooting, enhanced learning, personalised learning, general advice.
- **2025 top 10:**
  1. Therapy/companionship
  2. Organising my life
  3. Finding purpose
  4. Enhanced learning
  5. Generating code
  6. Generating ideas (down from #1)
  7. Fun and nonsense
  8. Improving code
  9. Creativity
  10. Healthier living
- **What stands out:** highly personal uses fill the top three, ahead of anything business-related. The host asks whether that reflects real usage or just what people find interesting to discuss.
- **Agreement with Anthropic:** generating code rose from #47 to #5, and improving code rose from #19 to #8. The host reads this as growth in both augmentation (improving code) and agent-style use (generating code), with generation growing faster.

### 7. Thematic groupings and the therapy use case

- The top 100 use cases were grouped into six themes: content creation and editing; technical assistance and troubleshooting; personal and professional support; learning and education; creativity and recreation; and research, analysis and decision-making.
- **Biggest change:** personal and professional support nearly doubled, from 17% to 31% of the top 100 use cases.
- **Therapy:** many posters described using AI to process grief or trauma. Three advantages came up repeatedly: it is available 24/7, it is cheap or free, and there is no fear of being judged by another person. The trend has also been seen in China. Zao-Sanders cites research suggesting AI therapeutic responses can be indistinguishable from human-written ones.
- **Wider trend:** guidance and advice are moving from humans to AI. Professional services such as therapy, medical advice, legal counsel, tax guidance and software development are increasingly delivered partly by generative AI.

### 8. Professional services and consulting as fast adopters

- **EY:** according to Simon Brown, EY's global learning and development leader, EY is training staff in generative AI skills so they can work with sector-specific agentic systems. These systems support nearly 400,000 staff and include 150 AI agents for tax-related work.
- **Business Insider piece:** *Inside the AI Boom That's Transforming How Consultants Work at McKinsey, BCG, and Deloitte* argues that consulting firms both sell AI services and are among the fastest enterprise adopters themselves.
- **McKinsey's "Lilli":** an in-house generative AI chatbot connected to more than 100,000 documents and interviews. Over 70% of the firm's 45,000 employees use it, about 17 times a week each, for research, summarising, data analysis and brainstorming. A case study found 30% time savings.
- **KPMG Pulse survey:** daily use of AI productivity tools rose from 22% in Q4 to 58% in Q1. The survey covers more than 100 senior leaders at companies with $1B+ revenue.
- Consultants also use more specialised tools, for example for building slide decks.

### 9. Where enterprises are and aren't deploying agents (Superintelligent's view)

- **Common deployments:** SDR (sales development) agents, content creation agents and customer service agents. These are external-facing areas where companies currently feel most confident.
- **Internal uses:** knowledge management and helping employees find answers.
- **What these have in common:** the work is important but relatively low-stakes compared with mission-critical product or operational work.
- **Where adoption lags:** mission-critical areas that must be right every time, such as many finance and advisory use cases. A 97% success rate isn't good enough there; it has to be close to 100%.
- This links to Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's essay on the urgency of interpretability. He argues interpretability matters for business reliability, not only for societal alignment.

### 10. Conclusion: usage is growing in breadth and depth

- More people are using AI, and existing users are using it more.
- The main point of the HBR research is not which use case ranks first but that there are use cases for nearly everything.
- Specific uses will keep changing, but the direction is clear.

## Key Concepts

- **Anthropic Economic Index:** Anthropic's research series analysing how Claude is used across jobs, tasks and economic sectors.
- **Claude Code:** Anthropic's agentic coding tool, which carries out software tasks directly.
- **Automation vs. augmentation:** automation means the AI performs a task directly; augmentation means it collaborates with and extends what a person can do.
- **Directive automation:** a pattern where the user tells the AI to complete a task and returns only when it is done, with little back-and-forth.
- **Automation with feedback loops:** a pattern where the AI performs the work but the person repeatedly reviews and refines it. It is much more common in coding (21%) than in non-coding tasks (3%).
- **Vibe coding:** building software by describing the desired outcome in plain language and letting the AI handle the implementation.
- **Lovable / SoftGen:** vibe coding platforms the host uses to prototype applications.
- **HBR "How People Are Really Using Gen AI":** Marc Zao-Sanders's annual ranking of generative AI use cases, based on public forum discussions and articles.
- **Personal and professional support:** an HBR theme covering therapy, companionship, life organisation and finding purpose. It grew from 17% to 31% of the top 100 use cases.
- **Lilli:** McKinsey's in-house generative AI chatbot, connected to the firm's body of intellectual property.
- **KPMG Pulse survey:** KPMG's quarterly survey of senior leaders at $1B+ revenue companies on AI adoption.
- **Agent readiness audit:** Superintelligent's service, which uses voice agents to interview staff and identify where AI agents would add the most business value.
- **SDR agent:** an AI agent that performs sales development work such as prospecting and outreach.
- **Interpretability:** the ability to understand how an AI model reaches its outputs. The episode presents it as necessary for using AI in high-stakes business areas.

## Summary

The host argues that AI usage is growing in both breadth and depth: more people use it, and existing users rely on it more. Anthropic's analysis of 500,000 coding conversations shows that agentic tools like Claude Code are used mainly for automation, mostly to build user-facing apps, and mostly by startups and individuals rather than enterprises. It also shows that people stay in the loop far more for coding than for other tasks. The HBR study is less rigorous because it relies on public forum posts, but it finds a strong shift towards personal uses: therapy and companionship, organising one's life, and finding purpose. Code generation and code improvement both rose sharply, which agrees with Anthropic's data. Consulting firms such as McKinsey, EY and KPMG show enterprise adoption maturing fast. Superintelligent's experience is that companies use agents confidently in important but lower-stakes work, such as sales, content, customer service and internal knowledge. They hold back in mission-critical areas where anything short of near-100% reliability is unacceptable. The host's closing message is that AI now has use cases for almost everything; the specific uses will keep changing, but the direction is clear.
