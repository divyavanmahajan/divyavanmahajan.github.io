## Overview

This is a bonus episode of the **AI Daily Brief** podcast. It republishes the last of seven episodes in **You Can With AI**, a series the AI Daily Brief made with KPMG as a primer on enterprise AI. The host, Nathaniel Whittemore, founder of Superintelligent, talks with **Steve Chase**, Vice Chair of AI and Digital Innovation at KPMG. Chase runs KPMG's enterprise innovation team and its AI program, in the US and globally.

The episode looks ahead. It does not try to make firm predictions. Instead it asks which questions enterprise leaders should be working on now and over the next few months. The main argument is that AI agents change the conversation. It is no longer only about productivity. It is about redesigning how the business works. The gap between leading and lagging organizations is widening, and the speed of change is still increasing.

- Source video: no URL was provided. The full series is at www.kpmg.us/AIpodcasts.

## Prerequisites

- A basic idea of generative AI assistants ("copilots") and how they differ from **AI agents**, which are semi-autonomous systems that carry out multi-step tasks.
- Some familiarity with how large organizations adopt new technology: proofs of concept (POCs), moving to production, change management and training.
- Awareness of new agent standards such as **MCP** (Model Context Protocol) and **A2A** (Agent-to-Agent).
- A general sense of the 2024–2025 model landscape, for example OpenAI and Google Gemini model releases.

## Main Points

### 1. The current state: "agents, agents, agents" and a widening gap
- Leadership conversations are dominated by agents. Chase compares it to Jan Brady's "Marsha, Marsha, Marsha", except the word is "agents". Many people still do not really understand what agents are.
- Leading organizations have prepared, are running pilots and are moving agents into production. Lagging organizations have not yet put basic AI tools in their employees' hands.
- Chase says no executive doubts this is the most transformative technology they will adopt. Even so, the leaders are pulling clearly ahead of the laggards.
- The leaders are asking: What is our agent strategy? How does it fit the business strategy? How do we help our people build an "AI-first mindset"?
- Chase describes agents as a way to bring "intelligence at scale horizontally across the organization."

### 2. From productivity to business redesign
- Whittemore argues that agents move the discussion away from simple productivity gains. The new focus is changing the structure of the business: redesigning operations, creating new products and services, and opening new revenue streams.
- Chase compares the change to the arrival of the Internet, and thinks it may go deeper. Companies are seriously asking whether their current org charts, and even their business models, will survive.
- He also says the productivity angle still matters. The most valuable employees could go from "10x" to "100x" performers.
- The question is changing. Leaders used to ask "how do we make finance close the month faster?" Now they ask how finance, procurement and operations might combine into a different kind of organization altogether.

### 3. The human side: AI-first mindset, non-adopters and mandates
- The KPMG Pulse Survey found that daily use of copilot-style tools rose from 22% to 58% between Q4 and Q1. Whittemore's reading is that copilot use is becoming table stakes.
- Building an AI-first mindset is hard. Some CEO memos have helped by telling staff to first ask "how could I solve this with AI?" before doing work the traditional way.
- Chase says the value of a person's work will shift. The person who only "pushes the button" on an agent adds little. The value is in the parts of the work only that person can add. People will manage agents, and in some processes agents will also manage the work people deliver.
- In many organizations, somewhere between 10% and 50% of employees do not use AI tools even when they have them. Chase suggests organizations will focus more on their front-runners rather than slowing everything down for the slowest adopters. He asks, "How backward compatible do we need to make the things that we're talking about?"
- Leadership messaging is getting firmer. Examples given:
  - Norway's sovereign wealth fund moved from encouraging AI use to making it mandatory.
  - Some companies run "soft hiring freezes": before hiring, they must be confident AI cannot do the job.
- Chase's view on change programs: they still need to run, but the content has moved to new skills and consistent techniques. "We're not in the experimentation phase."

### 4. Experimentation now assumes deployment
- About 65% of companies in the Pulse Survey, roughly two-thirds, have agent experiments or POCs running.
- Whittemore notes that these pilots are no longer testing *whether* to use agents. They are working out *how* to prepare for broad deployment.
- Chase lists what is slowing the move to production:
  - Control frameworks for semi-autonomous agents are immature.
  - Companies are unsure how many agents they actually want.
  - Each agent behaves like custom software, almost a product of its own, that has to be maintained.
  - User experience has to work across a mix of vendor-supplied agents and in-house agents.
  - Companies are still learning what new pieces such as MCP and A2A make possible.

### 5. Standards are converging unusually fast
- Agent infrastructure companies and protocol standards (MCP, A2A) are moving quickly to solve deployment problems.
- Whittemore contrasts this with past standards fights, such as email standards, which took about a decade of turf wars. With agent infrastructure, the industry has mostly lined up behind whichever standard was leading, because the prize of actually deploying agents is bigger than any fight over who owns the standard.

### 6. What enterprises must work through now
- **Ecosystem and technology choices:** Build on existing investments. Old systems are a real obstacle, because vendors are not adding agent features to them. Be willing to "go long" on some ecosystem bets by piloting them.
- **Change management and training:** These are often the first things cut from budgets, but rewriting business processes means more training, not less. Chase warns of a coming "trough of disillusionment" for companies that cut the enablers a "silver bullet" depends on.
- **Data versus knowledge:** Data remains the most expensive area, although solutions are improving. Chase argues that *knowledge* (what the organization actually knows, such as clear policies) is now more valuable. For example, putting a chatbot in front of customers often reveals that policies are less clear than people assumed. Companies should run POCs where good data and knowledge already exist.
- **Clear central vision:** Chase supports distributed experimentation ("brush fires"), but only with a way to bring the results back to the center. Without that, organizations get stuck with POCs that never scale. He recommends a strong central vision covering what the organization is trying to achieve, why, and on what time horizon. He expects mandates in some cases, and wants a high "clock speed" set as an expectation.

### 7. The pace of change is accelerating
- Chase agrees that the pace is speeding up. One client mapped the coming quarters and found more real capability arriving than they had delivered to users in five or six years.
- Enterprise teams are now working with vendor technology almost on release day. Chase says this has not happened since the late 1990s. Models and features ship too often to wait for the big vendor conferences.
- Enterprises are built for stability and are not set up to absorb change this fast.
- **Alignment drift** is a risk. Testing cannot be done by hand. Organizations need test harnesses and evaluation frameworks that cover both new and existing deployments.
- Organizations need to run at a different clock speed and use automation and agents to manage the change that AI itself causes. Chase says leaders who are actually doing this work see it as one of their most important investments, and often an underfunded one.
- Whittemore notes that model upgrades differ a lot in importance. The jump from GPT-4o to o3 opened up new use cases, while the move from Gemini 2.5 to 2.5 Pro was a smaller improvement focused on coding. Most organizations have no structure for absorbing that kind of distinction.
- Chase's answer: build a research and signaling function. KPMG uses a lab environment for this.
- On cost: new models are expensive at first, are not needed for every use case, and fall in price quickly. Systems, not individual users, should handle choosing the right model, and KPMG is building that "situational awareness" into its agents.

### 8. Are organizations ready?
- Chase says KPMG has used the last few years to rethink its services, products, delivery and training.
- He worries about organizations that think they have "done enough" and will commit capital later. He calls that "catching up to a speeding bullet."
- Pre-built agents from enterprise software vendors (for example, for resume screening) are table stakes. Companies with sound SaaS investments will get them.
- The real change is something like a "universal agent" that works across all systems and handles everything an employee might need. No single vendor is likely to provide it, and most organizations are not ready for that kind of agent-based thinking.
- "Time is running out on this topic." Disruption reshuffles winners and losers in every industry. This is already visible in big tech, where some companies that seemed unbeatable are struggling with their AI story.

### 9. What will set the winners apart
- **Chase:** Vision, plus the willingness to pursue it boldly and quickly. KPMG's internal phrase is "bold, fast and responsible." Every leader has to own the mission, rather than handing it to a separate AI group. AI has to be part of the business strategy, not a separate add-on.
- **Whittemore:** Willingness to engage with the world as it is, at the speed it is changing, even if that means giving up long-held assumptions.

### 10. Horizontal scale: the "Doctor Strange" thought experiment
- Microsoft's framework describes a path toward everyone becoming an **"agent boss"** who manages swarms of agents. Today, most organizations are still learning to work with agents as coworkers, having first learned to use copilots as assistants.
- **The Doctor Strange idea:** If you were not limited by hiring, onboarding or labor costs, and could have 100 versions of a worker doing the same job in different ways, would you do the work differently?
- **Marketing example:** Agents imitate the 100 greatest writers to draft campaign copy. Review agents then act as synthetic audiences and give feedback. People may become curators who choose the best output.
- **Chase's "outcome redesign":** Many current processes are workarounds for old limits. "Why sample? Do 100%. Why batch? Do it real time."
- **KPMG example:** KPMG has an assessment product that helps companies decide which agents to deploy. It uses voice agents to interview *every* employee in a department at length and analyzes the results in hours rather than months. Interviewing everyone would never have been practical before.
- **Caveat (Chase):** Do not become so focused on the technology that you forget the people. The point is to free up time to spend with clients and colleagues.

### 11. Different products and services: customer service
- AI customer service agents have clear advantages: they are always available and never impatient or bored.
- Whittemore predicts customer service will split into two tiers. AI agents will handle the bulk of the work, and human service at the top end will become a prestige role, like a Ritz-Carlton concierge, and a way for companies to stand out.
- Chase agrees, pointing to a car dealership his family uses because of its customer service.

## Key Concepts

- **AI agent:** A semi-autonomous AI system that carries out multi-step tasks for a user or organization.
- **AI-first mindset:** The habit of asking first how a task could be done with AI before doing it the traditional way.
- **Copilot:** An AI assistant built into everyday tools. The speakers see copilot use as becoming standard practice.
- **Jagged frontier (of adoption):** The uneven and widening gap between organizations that lead and those that lag in AI adoption.
- **KPMG Pulse Survey:** KPMG's recurring executive survey. Cited figures: daily copilot use rose from 22% to 58% (Q4 to Q1), and about 65% of companies are running agent experiments.
- **MCP (Model Context Protocol):** A standard for connecting AI models and agents to tools and data sources.
- **A2A (Agent-to-Agent):** A protocol that lets agents communicate and coordinate with each other.
- **Control framework:** The governance and risk controls an organization needs before it is comfortable letting semi-autonomous agents operate.
- **Trough of disillusionment:** The disappointment that follows hype. Here it is expected for companies that deploy AI without investing in training, change management and knowledge.
- **Knowledge vs. data:** The difference between structured organizational knowledge (for example, clear policies) and raw data. Chase sees knowledge as currently more valuable.
- **Clock speed:** How fast an organization makes decisions and absorbs change.
- **Alignment drift:** AI system behavior slowly moving away from what was intended as models and components change. It requires continuous automated evaluation.
- **Test harness / evaluation framework:** Automated tooling that checks AI behavior across both new and existing deployments.
- **Universal agent:** A hypothetical agent that works across all of an organization's systems to handle anything an employee needs.
- **Agent boss:** Microsoft's term for the future role of a person who manages a team or swarm of agents.
- **Doctor Strange thought experiment:** Imagining that a job could be done by 100 parallel versions of a worker, to see how unlimited intelligence would change processes.
- **Outcome redesign:** Redesigning how an outcome is reached, rather than automating existing steps, and dropping workarounds such as sampling or batch processing.
- **Bold, fast and responsible:** KPMG's internal motto for how to pursue AI transformation.

## Summary

Steve Chase and Nathaniel Whittemore argue that agents have moved enterprise AI past productivity tools toward a basic redesign of how businesses operate, and that the change is speeding up. Leading organizations are pulling away from laggards. Copilot use is now table stakes, AI adoption is becoming mandatory rather than optional, and agent pilots are run on the assumption that they will be deployed. Several things still stand in the way of full deployment: immature control frameworks, legacy systems, underfunded training and change management, weak organizational knowledge, and the lack of a clear central vision. Organizations also need new capabilities, such as research functions, automated evaluation, and a faster organizational clock speed, to absorb change at a pace they were not built for. The speakers believe the winners will be organizations with a bold vision pursued quickly and responsibly, owned by every leader, and built into business strategy. They also urge leaders to imagine what nearly unlimited, parallel intelligence makes possible, while keeping people at the center. Their parting advice is to focus on the underlying principles and mindset changes rather than on specific details that will soon be out of date.
