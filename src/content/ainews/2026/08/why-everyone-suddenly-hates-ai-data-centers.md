## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, hosted by **Nathaniel Whittemore**, is an end-to-end primer on why public opposition to AI data centers has risen so quickly in the United States, and whether there is a constructive way forward. The episode was prompted by the midterm elections, where data centers have become a rare bipartisan target. Whittemore argues that the backlash has many causes at once: quality-of-life worries, bad messaging from the AI industry, years of declining trust in big tech, and opaque dealmaking. Underneath all of these, he says, the core issue is communities' sense of agency and control over their own future. His conclusion is optimistic: this may be "the most winnable value-creating political battle of all time," as long as policy moves toward clear rules and community benefits and away from moratoriums.

## Prerequisites

- A basic picture of what data centers are and the role they play in AI training, AI inference and ordinary internet services.
- A general sense of the 2025–2026 AI infrastructure build-out and the hyperscaler capital spending behind it.
- Familiarity with US political structure: state governors, county and municipal zoning, and the midterm election cycle.
- Basic understanding of opinion polling, including net support or opposition and "strongly" versus "somewhat" responses.
- Awareness of debates about utility pricing, grid interconnection and the local property tax base.

## Main Points

### 1. Why this topic matters now: the midterms and collapsing poll numbers

- Data center opposition has "escaped containment" and become a midterm election issue.
- In a Gallup poll from May, 71% opposed data centers in their area, 48% of them strongly.
- Heatmap News found opposition to nearby data centers rose from 51% in February to 75% this month. Strong support fell from 9% to 4%.
- Ramez Naam pointed out that strong opposition went from 24% to 61% in 12 months. Lobbyist Justin Slaughter said opponents are turning supporters into opponents, not merely into neutrals.
- An Echelon poll found Americans would rather have a nuclear plant nearby than an AI data center. Respondents were also about twice as likely to accept a nearby coal plant as a data center. Whittemore takes this as evidence that the opposition is not purely about water or pollution.

### 2. A rare bipartisan issue, amplified by politicians and the media

- In August 2025 Republicans were net supportive, independents were at −5% and Democrats at −8%. Independents and Democrats have since fallen to roughly −65% and −75%, and even Republicans are now net 43% opposed.
- Governors who once backed data centers, including Greg Abbott (Texas) and Josh Shapiro (Pennsylvania), have reversed course. A National Republican Senatorial Committee memo framed data centers as a political liability.
- Examples from campaigns:
  - Michigan's Tom Barrett is running ads against "big tech billionaires" even though he previously voted in favor of data centers.
  - In Wisconsin, Tom Tiffany is attacking David Crowley's renewables-powered data center plan.
- Matthew Zeitlin's quip summarizes the situation: every statewide race is now two former supporters accusing each other of supporting data centers.
- The result is a feedback loop. Public sentiment gets picked up by politicians, and the media then amplifies both the anger and the politicians' change of position.

### 3. What a data center actually is

- A data center is a warehouse-sized computer: racks of chips and servers powered by utility lines or dedicated generation, with heat removed by air, evaporative towers or liquid cooling loops.
- Data centers run AI training and serving, but they also run everyday services such as Gmail and Netflix.
- Nick Carter suggests some of the backlash comes from people discovering that "the cloud" has a physical footprint: it needs land, power and cooling.

### 4. The stated local concerns (Puck News poll)

- Too much strain on the local power grid: 78% said definitely or probably true (45% definitely).
- Excessive use of the local water supply: 75% (44% definitely).
- Lower property values: 59%.
- Constant disruptive noise: 59%.
- Too few local jobs to justify construction: 54%.
- Higher local taxes: 48%, even though there is no clear mechanism by which data centers would raise them.

### 5. Competing explanations for the anger

- **Foreign influence campaigns ("China psyop").** A.G. Hamilton, Frank Fleming, Trey Stephens, Rob LeKirk and Kevin O'Leary have all claimed China is behind the opposition. O'Leary made the claim about local opponents of his Utah project. Whittemore thinks bot farms probably do add to the discord on any hot-button topic, but calls the explanation "way too convenient." Treating it as the whole story would stop people from dealing with the real issues.
- **The AI industry's own messaging.** Senator Chris Murphy said the industry promised to destroy jobs, "and it feels like people are just believing them." Polls show that AI data centers specifically draw more opposition than data centers in general. Brendan Steinhauser and the poster "Zerathustra" describe the story ordinary people hear: AI takes your job, billionaires get richer, and you get nothing back. Chamath Palihapitiya says frontier AI leadership failed to paint a positive picture.
- **Not enough real value delivered.** T Greer argues that the problem is substance, not messaging, because AI has not yet delivered tangible broad benefits. Citing Ben Thompson's point that consumers don't care about productivity, Whittemore adds that when layoffs are blamed on AI, "productivity" sounds to workers like giving away more of their labor.
- **Accumulated distrust of big tech.** Gavin Purcell estimates that at least 75% of the negativity is really about big tech over the last decade: election manipulation, children's screen time, radicalization, the damage to the media business, and so on. In his view, data centers are the first physical target for that anger.
- **Institutional distrust and secrecy.** Gallup puts average confidence in nine major US institutions at a record-low 27%, and confidence in big business at 17%. Deals made behind closed doors and under NDAs (the subject of an NBC report) make that mistrust worse.

### 6. The heart of the matter: agency and control

- Technology moving forward means change people can't control, and that reduces their sense of agency. Secret deals take away even the chance to see how decisions get made.
- Jasmine Sun's reporting found reflexive skepticism toward every claim ("Closed-loop cooling systems, I don't believe them…"). The pattern resembles dark money in politics: "It's not Skynet, it's the oligarchy."
- Jonathan Keeperman calls data centers a proxy for concerns that are hard to put into words. Two populist intuitions drive them: value that is abstracted away from tangible production is suspect, and if something is opaque, "the average guy" is probably getting screwed. He adds that the concerns are often real but misdirected.

### 7. The current policy response: moratoriums

- Interconnected Capital tracks 97 county bans, 93 city bans and 28 town bans.
- New York became the first state to impose a statewide moratorium.
- Governors are now competing to have the strictest rules.

### 8. Fighting misinformation, and why facts alone don't convince people

- Many water-use memes leave out context or are simply wrong. Karen Hao's *Empire of AI* overstated water use by a factor of 1,000. She has acknowledged the error, but the figure is still in circulation.
- Analyses have found no clear statistical link between where electricity prices rise most and where data centers are located. Whittemore says the actual effect depends on how each project is designed.
- Because of the trust gap, factual rebuttals don't land well (Puck News):
  - The correction of the water math error: 8% very convincing, 20% somewhat convincing.
  - "Thousands of high-paying jobs": 12% very, 23% somewhat.
  - Tax revenue for schools and roads, the strongest argument tested: 14% very, 28% somewhat.

### 9. Better messengers and better evidence

- Messaging about AI and jobs has improved, which Whittemore calls likely "too little too late." He argues that evidence matters more than messaging, citing Ford rehiring "graybeard engineers."
- Trade unions are credible messengers. The International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW) warned that bans in New England would cost billions in investment and many jobs for its members, and called for strong labor standards and apprenticeship programs instead.
- Congressman Don Bacon (Nebraska) said data centers "can be built smartly to conserve water and expand energy production" and have benefited Sarpy County.

### 10. Proof points: communities that benefited

- **Dominion Energy (Virginia):** some research shows customers saw slightly lower rates because of data center load growth.
- **Quincy, Washington:**
  - A farming town that has added data centers since 2006 and now has 30 of them.
  - About 900 direct jobs, plus 4–6 indirect jobs for each direct job.
  - Data centers pay 57% of property taxes, which have funded a $15M pool, a $120M high school and a sports complex.
  - Poverty fell from 29.4% in 2012 to 6.2% in 2024, which a former city administrator called the "Quincy Miracle."
- **Loudoun County, Virginia ("Data Center Alley"):**
  - More than 200 facilities, about 14% of global capacity, on roughly 3% of the county's land, funding about 40% of its budget.
  - About 74,000 Virginians work in the industry, earning $5.5B in labor income.
  - It is the wealthiest US county, and it has cut its property tax rate every year for 10 years to 0.8%.
  - Data centers provide about 42% of local tax funding, up from 32.8% in 2024.
  - Compared with Fairfax, Fairfax charges about 35% more in property tax while spending about 45% less on services.

### 11. "Just give people stuff": community benefits as the equation

- Polymath ("start building parks") and Lulu Cheng Meservey ("Just give people stuff") argue for tangible benefits over persuasion.
- Whittemore frames the whole issue as one equation: does the community's benefit exceed its cost? When it doesn't, the answer is to negotiate for more.
- Mike Solana says aesthetic, noise and property-value concerns are legitimate and should be addressed.
- Industry moves:
  - Meta has launched a $1B local-initiatives fund.
  - OpenAI announced $40B for community-defined priorities at its Pike County, Ohio site, plus $84M in Codex credits for about 844,000 Ohio students.
  - Microsoft has stopped using NDAs with local governments.

### 12. Politicians as intermediaries, and why moratoriums fail

- West Virginia is exploring using data center revenue to eliminate its income tax. Grover Norquist suggests every state direct data center taxes toward cutting property and income taxes.
- Back-of-the-napkin math by "Kremyonx" for a 20,000-person town: a data center below the 250 MW hyperscale threshold could replace all of its property taxes.
- Moratoriums "kick the can down the field." They address no problems and create no opportunities.
- Morning Consult found voters prefer rules to bans:
  - 57% favor clear rules versus 23% for a moratorium.
  - For a project that meets disclosure, cost and public-review requirements, 58% would allow it versus 24% who would stop it.
  - 78% support requiring companies to pay for grid upgrades; 13% oppose.

### 13. Templates for a path forward

- David Crowley (Wisconsin):
  - Communities decide, and a "no" is honored.
  - If a community says yes, the developer must: ban NDAs, use 100% renewables, cover all grid and infrastructure costs, contribute to the Focus on Energy fund, use union labor with project labor agreements and community benefit agreements, and protect water.
- Vivek Ramaswamy (Ohio): residents near a data center would pay no electricity bill and lower property taxes, and the facility must meet all air and water standards.
- Whittemore expects smart politicians of both parties to write checklists that developers can actually meet, built on community agency.

## Key Concepts

- **AI data center:** a warehouse-scale facility of networked servers and chips, with power and cooling systems, that trains and serves AI models along with general internet services.
- **Hyperscale threshold (~250 MW):** the power capacity cited in the episode as the dividing line for "hyperscale" data centers.
- **Moratorium:** a temporary or indefinite ban on new data center construction at the town, city, county or state level.
- **Net support/opposition:** support minus opposition in polling, used to track how sentiment shifts within each party.
- **NDAs (non-disclosure agreements):** confidentiality agreements developers use with local governments, which the episode identifies as a major driver of mistrust.
- **Community benefit agreement (CBA):** a binding commitment by a developer to provide specific local benefits in exchange for approval.
- **Project labor agreement (PLA):** a pre-hire agreement setting union labor terms for a construction project.
- **"China psyop" thesis:** the claim that foreign influence operations are driving opposition, which Whittemore treats as partly plausible but overused.
- **Quincy Miracle:** Quincy, Washington's economic transformation, including a sharp fall in poverty, attributed to data center investment.
- **Data Center Alley:** Loudoun County, Virginia, the world's densest concentration of data centers and a major source of the county's tax revenue.
- **Focus on Energy fund:** a Wisconsin program that Crowley's plan would fund with developer contributions to lower utility rates statewide.
- **Cost–benefit equation:** Whittemore's framing that acceptance depends on whether a community's perceived benefits outweigh its costs.

## Summary

Whittemore argues that the rapid, bipartisan collapse in support for AI data centers is not just misinformation or foreign manipulation. It combines real local concerns (power, water, noise, property values) with the AI industry's alarming messaging about jobs, a lack of visible benefits for ordinary people, a decade of distrust in big tech, and secretive, NDA-driven deals. Together these leave communities feeling they have no say over changes imposed on them. Factual rebuttals do little to overcome that trust gap, and the moratoriums now spreading across the country solve nothing. Polling shows that voters prefer clear rules to outright bans, and places like Quincy and Loudoun County show that data centers can lower taxes, reduce poverty and fund public services. He therefore sees an unusually winnable compromise. Developers give up secrecy, pay their own way and share real benefits, while politicians write achievable checklists grounded in community consent. He expects public perception may get worse before it improves, but believes politicians who adopt these templates can turn the issue around.
