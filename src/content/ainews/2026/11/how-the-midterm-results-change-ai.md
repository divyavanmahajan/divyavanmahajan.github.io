## Overview

**Speaker:** NLW, host of *The AI Daily Brief* (and of a new history podcast, *How We Got Here*).
**Source URL:** not provided in the supplied video details (the `URL` and `Channel` fields were blank).

The talk analyses the 2026 US midterm election results — a decisive Democratic win the speaker calls a "blue wave" — and asks what they mean specifically for the AI industry. The central thesis is that **AI itself was not on the ballot; AI data centers were**, and that the election largely *confirmed* a political direction already underway rather than creating a new one. Voters' hostility to data centers was real but ranked last among hot-button issues; the practical consequence is not a federal shutdown of the build-out but a shift of power to state and local officials, plus incremental federal rules in areas of bipartisan consensus. The speaker argues that more capable models would have produced more regulation regardless of who won, and that the data-center fight is unusually "winnable" for all sides because a workable compromise — community agency, no NDAs, developers paying their own way — is already emerging.

This matters because the AI build-out is, in the speaker's framing, "pretty much all we've had going for us" economically for the past couple of years, making the regulatory posture toward data centers a macroeconomic question as much as a technology-policy one.

## Prerequisites

- **US electoral structure** — midterms, House/Senate control, veto-proof supermajorities, gubernatorial races, down-ballot ballot measures, and the division of authority between federal and state government.
- **Who sets data-center policy** — the speaker's key structural point is that it is primarily determined at state level, with governors having final say.
- **The AI data-center build-out** — gigawatt-scale campuses, grid interconnection, electricity and water demand, tax subsidies, and host-community agreements (including the use of NDAs).
- **Recent AI policy history** — California's SB 1047 (2024) and its veto; the spread of state-level AI regulation; export controls on AI chips vis-à-vis China.
- **AI safety vocabulary** — existential risk ("X-risk"), frontier model pre-release testing, recursive self-improvement, bioweapon misuse risk.
- **AI finance terms** — "neoclouds" (specialist GPU cloud providers) and the general bubble/overbuild debate.
- **Named context** — Pew Research polling, NBC exit polling, Trump's "Big Beautiful Bill," flock cameras, the "Mamdani playbook" in New York politics.

## Main Points

### 1. The result: a genuine blue wave, broad but not record-breaking

- Democrats recorded an **11-point swing across national averages** and will take control of both the House and the Senate, though they are unlikely to reach a veto-proof supermajority.
- In absolute seat counts the result does not match historic wipeouts such as the 2018 or 2010 midterms.
- Its distinctive feature is **reach**: Democrats flipped unlikely seats in GOP strongholds including Texas, South Carolina and Kentucky.

### 2. AI was not on the ballot — data centers were

Pew Research polling from early October, share of voters calling the issue "very important":

| Issue | Very important |
|---|---|
| Economy | 84% |
| Future of democracy | 73% |
| Healthcare | 68% |
| Education | 62% |
| Immigration | 59% |
| Foreign policy | 57% |
| **AI** | **44%** |

- AI skewed Democratic as a priority: 54% of Democrats called it very important versus 34% of Republicans.
- Pew itself noted that labelling the issue "AI" was misleading — the real issue was data centers. **61% of voters said they dislike or strongly dislike candidates who support building data centers locally** (75% of Democratic supporters, 44% of Republican supporters).
- Almost nobody liked pro-data-center candidates: just **5% of Democratic supporters and 14% of Republican supporters**. Supporting data centers was a vote loser for both parties.

### 3. How data centers were campaigned on: cost of living, corruption, oligarchy

- Data-center opposition was never standalone — it was fused with economic themes: utility bills, cost of living, and the wealth divide between ordinary people and what candidates called the tech "oligarchy."
- Ohio Democrat **Sherrod Brown**: "Ohioans shouldn't be forced to subsidize these costs, and Ohioans, not John Husted and his billionaire friends, should get to decide if data centers are built in their community."
- In Michigan, **Abdul El-Sayed** ran attack ads tying flock cameras and AI data centers to opponent Mike Rogers' 2001 support for the Patriot Act — framing data centers as surveillance infrastructure.
- Republicans were constrained by President Trump's vocal pro-AI stance, including his late-August Truth Social claim that communities rejecting data centers would end up "backwards and poor." That was an opening for Republicans in tight races to break with him, but **few took it**; most offered status quo positions (Husted: data centers "are and should remain a local decision").

### 4. Exit polling: important enough to measure, last among hot-button issues

NBC News exit polling, share calling the issue a "crucial or major factor" in their vote:

| Issue | Crucial/major factor |
|---|---|
| Government corruption | 90% |
| Cost of living | 89% |
| War in Iran | 74% |
| Immigration enforcement | 73% |
| **Data centers** | **56%** |

- Democrats were **twice as likely** as Republicans to cite data centers as a major factor.
- The speaker's reading: data centers mattered enough to earn a line in exit polls, but among hot-button issues they finished **last**.

### 5. Three races where AI policy was genuinely central

**California's 11th district (San Francisco)** — State Senator Scott Weiner versus Board of Supervisors member Connie Chan, for the seat Nancy Pelosi had represented since 1987, in the district containing both OpenAI's and Anthropic's headquarters.
- Both are progressive Democrats; Chan held Pelosi's endorsement and the party apparatus. The race was uncalled at recording time, but Weiner held a commanding lead with roughly half the votes tallied.
- Weiner was lead sponsor of **SB 1047** (2024), which included a mandatory kill switch, developer liability, and a definition of frontier AI covering all models larger than Llama 3. The bill passed the House but was vetoed by Governor Newsom; many of its ideas resurfaced in later California legislation that became a blueprint for other states.
- Being tough on AI was a campaign cornerstone — in an October debate he said he "went to war with big tech to pass AI regulation in California." Chan attacked him for taking $3 million from Public First Action, an Anthropic-aligned pro-regulation PAC, but the speaker judges it hard to cast Weiner as industry-captured; he is positioned to become a champion for AI safety advocates focused on existential risk.

**Ohio** — Sherrod Brown defeated John Husted.
- Ohio has roughly 200 data centers, mostly small, but **46 new projects are planned totalling 16 gigawatts** — including Meta's Prometheus facility outside Columbus (described as the world's first full-gigawatt data center) and an OpenAI campus planned for Pike County at 10 gigawatts.
- Brown made data centers the central theme, labelling Husted "the face of data centers" in one ad and running another consisting of nothing but the loud hum of a data center.
- He returns not as a freshman but as an 18-year Senate veteran, unseated in 2024 partly by crypto-PAC money — arriving with a mandate to block data centers in his state and renewed anger at an industry that spent tens of millions against him.

**Texas** — the race the speaker thinks matters most for the industry.
- James Tallarico defeated Ken Paxton for the Senate, the first Democrat to represent Texas since 1993; more consequentially for AI, **Gina Hinojosa defeated three-term incumbent Greg Abbott** in the gubernatorial race.
- Texas has been ground zero for the build-out, drawn by cheap West Texas gas and fast renewable deployment. In September, Abbott halted all new data-center permitting pending an audit by Texas energy and environmental regulators — at a point when proposals exceeded **470 gigawatts**, roughly three times the current grid. The speaker judges it too little, too late.
- Hinojosa's platform: a moratorium until regulations are written, an end to tax subsidies (which she called a "corruption tax on citizens"), and guarantees that electricity and water are not diverted from local communities.

### 6. The state and local shift is the real change

- Because data-center policy is set by states with governors having final say, state results may matter more immediately than Washington.
- After the election, **nine of the ten largest states are run by Democrats**; Ohio and Georgia flipped red to blue, leaving **Florida as the only top-ten state with a Republican governor**.
- Scale framing: with almost **$3 trillion in GDP**, Texas is larger than Canada or Russia. The speaker quotes Breaking Points: "the eighth largest economy in the world is experiencing regime change."

### 7. Two figures to watch heading into 2028

- **Senator John Ossoff (Georgia)** cruised to a second term, easily dispatching Trump-backed Mike Collins in one of the night's first calls after Collins' support collapsed late. Ossoff was among the most effective at making data centers a symbol of big-tech oligarchy, political corruption and cost of living: "no Georgia family should pay a dime more to subsidize the energy consumption of these data centers," plus the claim that Trump's Big Beautiful Bill "gave $70 billion of your money to data center developers," tied to rising healthcare costs and hospital closures. He launched an investigation into data-center electricity use in April. The speaker's assessment: so far largely "toothless investigations that make for good headlines," but with 2028 presidential talk rising, that could change.
- **Abdul El-Sayed (Michigan)** beat Mike Rogers to win the Senate seat — the first Muslim senator in US history, running a campaign borrowed heavily from the Mamdani playbook. Where Ossoff represents centrist Democrats, El-Sayed represents the rising socialist left. His campaign conflated data centers with flock cameras and AI-powered warfare in the Middle East to paint a dystopian picture. On *60 Minutes*: "Had you asked me five years ago, I would have said healthcare… right now, we are barreling toward a world where the most important issue I hear about every day is AI." He cited job loss, economic disruption, loss of control, and bioweapon risk — "these are issues that people are deathly afraid of." As a freshman with no establishment ties, his actual policy positions are a wild card.

### 8. Ballot measures: a clean sweep against data centers

- **41 down-ballot proposals** related to data centers appeared across the country, and as of the night of recording **all 41 looked set to pass**.
- The speaker reads this as AI companies having "completely lost the narrative war" around the build-out.
- Brad Carson, co-founder of Americans for Responsible Innovation: "What we just witnessed was an AI earthquake. Voters sent a clear message that they want rules for AI and data centers."

### 9. Likely federal policy: no grand framework, plenty of low-hanging fruit

- Comprehensive regulation is unlikely, partly because Trump told Fox News before the election that if Democrats won, "I guess I'll become Mr. Veto." A top-down federal moratorium on data-center construction or frontier model training is therefore improbable.
- Changes the speaker does expect, in areas with more cross-party consensus than it first appears:
  - **Whistleblower protections** for lab employees
  - **Codifying** the executive branch's voluntary pre-release testing of frontier LLMs
  - **Incident reporting** rules
  - **Stronger statutory liability** for model builders
  - Possible movement on **data sovereignty** rules
- **Export controls are genuinely uncertain.** Thinking on China has never split cleanly along party lines, so a Democratic win does not straightforwardly imply more or less restriction — but it will be a live debate, tied to discussion of pausing, pacing and potential prohibitions on specific training techniques such as AI building AI via recursive self-improvement.
- Expect many AI executives hauled before Congress for hearings, but — barring Democrats deciding to target Elon Musk — the speaker does not expect those hearings to be particularly impactful.

### 10. The deeper driver: capability, not the election

- The speaker argues a shift toward the left's "more rules" position was inevitable regardless of the outcome.
- The reason is partly political winds, but also simply that **models are getting more powerful**: for many people it is common sense that rising capabilities — including capabilities for harm — warrant rising controls.
- AI safety advocates still argue fiercely about *how early* and *how far ahead of capabilities* rules should run, but for many the correlation between capability ramp and rules ramp is self-evident.
- His stated source of optimism is how much of the likely near-term regulation is common-sense, consensus material that can serve as foundations to build on — which does not mean the remaining debates won't be extremely contentious.

### 11. The carve-out: "I don't hate data centers"

- On data centers specifically, the election "confirmed what we already knew": the era in which developers did not have to court or care about local communities is over — and developers had already received that memo over the course of the year.
- Changes already visible: **moratoriums on NDAs**, commitments to pay their own way on infrastructure and electricity, and more community sweeteners and incentives to win individual and community buy-in.
- Some constituencies will oppose data centers under any terms, however transparent. But the speaker expects many new leaders to recognise the economic importance of data centers and to find it in their interest to write **hard but achievable rules** that give communities agency while leaving a path forward.
- The pivotal quote is Hinojosa's, from an August press conference: "I don't hate data centers. I just hate the way they're taking advantage of Texans." The gap between those two positions is the political and practical space the speaker expects to be occupied.
- He adds a first-principles argument: asked cold what the relationship between data centers and host communities *should* look like, most people would describe the no-NDA, community-agency, pay-your-own-way version rather than the arrangement of a couple of years ago. On that basis he calls the build-out "just about the most winnable political issue I've ever seen" — winnable not for one side, but for everyone.

### 12. The disaster scenario, stated as a causal chain

For completeness, the speaker lays out the downside case as an explicit sequence:

```
data-center moratoriums gain momentum
  → rapid executive action from new governors
  → Wall Street withdraws funding
  → data centers can't get funded
  → the AI build-out halts
  → neoclouds go bust
  → spillover into a broader downturn
  → the downturn gains its own momentum and ravages the economy
     (because the AI build-out is "pretty much all we've had going for us")
  → 2028 is contested over AI jobs, cybersecurity and X-risk
     AND in the aftermath of a burst bubble
```

- He considers this "certainly not outside the realm of possibility," and believes developers know it — which is why he expects them to work overtime to find whatever accommodation keeps projects moving.

## Key Concepts

- **Blue wave** — A broad, decisive swing to the Democratic Party across many races; here an 11-point national-average swing delivering both chambers.
- **Veto-proof supermajority** — A legislative majority large enough to override a presidential veto; Democrats are unlikely to reach it, making Trump's "Mr. Veto" posture decisive.
- **Data-center moratorium** — A temporary halt on new data-center permitting or construction, typically pending regulation or an audit; the form data-center opposition took on 41 ballot measures.
- **SB 1047** — Weiner's 2024 California AI bill (mandatory kill switch, developer liability, frontier-AI definition covering models above Llama 3 size); passed the House, vetoed by Governor Newsom, but influential on later state law.
- **Public First Action** — An Anthropic-aligned pro-AI-regulation PAC; source of $3 million to Weiner's campaign.
- **Americans for Responsible Innovation** — AI policy advocacy organisation whose co-founder Brad Carson called the result "an AI earthquake."
- **Gigawatt-scale data center** — A campus drawing a gigawatt or more of power; Meta's Prometheus (Columbus) cited as the first, OpenAI's Pike County campus planned at 10 GW.
- **Flock cameras** — Automated license-plate-reading surveillance cameras, used in Michigan ads to bundle data centers into a surveillance-state narrative.
- **Oligarchy framing** — The campaign device of casting tech wealth and its political influence as the beneficiary of data-center subsidies, linked to cost-of-living and corruption concerns.
- **Corruption tax** — Hinojosa's label for data-center tax subsidies borne by ordinary citizens.
- **Neoclouds** — Specialist GPU/AI cloud providers whose failure is the transmission mechanism in the speaker's downturn scenario.
- **Recursive self-improvement** — AI systems building more capable AI; named as a training technique that could face prohibition proposals.
- **X-risk (existential risk)** — The AI safety concern with catastrophic, civilisation-scale outcomes; the constituency Weiner is positioned to champion.
- **Incident reporting** — Mandatory disclosure of AI failures or harms; flagged as likely bipartisan, with details decisive.
- **Export controls** — Restrictions on shipping advanced AI chips or models abroad, especially to China; identified as the issue least predictable from party control.
- **Mamdani playbook** — A New York left-populist campaign template that El-Sayed borrowed from in Michigan.

## Summary

The 2026 midterms produced a broad Democratic victory, and for the AI industry the speaker's verdict is that things changed "sort of, and potentially yes — but mostly it was a confirmation of the direction things were already headed." Polling shows AI itself ranked low as a voter priority (44% in Pew; 56% and last among hot-button issues in NBC exit polls), while opposition to *local data centers* was near-universal across both parties and fused with cost-of-living, utility-bill and anti-corruption anger — enough that all 41 data-center ballot measures appear to have passed and that pro-data-center positions were a liability for Republicans and Democrats alike. The practical consequences fall less in Washington, where Trump's veto makes a comprehensive framework or federal moratorium unlikely, than in the states: nine of the ten largest are now Democrat-run, and Texas in particular — now under Governor Hinojosa, with proposals totalling over 470 gigawatts — intends to slow the build-out. Federally, the speaker expects incremental, largely bipartisan measures: whistleblower protections, codified pre-release testing, incident reporting, stronger developer liability, perhaps data sovereignty, with export controls genuinely unpredictable. His broader claim is that this tightening would have come regardless of the result, because rising model capability makes rising controls common sense to most people. And his closing optimism rests on Hinojosa's line — "I don't hate data centers, I just hate the way they're taking advantage of Texans" — which marks out a compromise space (no NDAs, community agency, developers paying their own way) that he thinks makes the data-center build-out the most winnable political issue he has seen, winnable for everyone rather than one side. He concedes the alternative: if moratoria cascade into withdrawn financing, a halted build-out and a burst bubble, and if the new crop of leaders concludes that hard opposition to AI is a winning issue, he may be eating those words by 2028.
