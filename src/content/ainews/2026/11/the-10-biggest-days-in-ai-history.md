## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, hosted by NLW, was recorded on the day of the Anthropic IPO. The host calls it the largest IPO in history and the start of "a very new era". He uses the occasion to pick the ten most significant days in the history of generative AI. The aim is to explain how the industry reached its current state and where it might go next. The list covers generative AI only, not earlier AI history. The days are presented in chronological order, not ranked. The host mentions a companion show, *How We Got Here with NLW*, which will cover the same history in more depth, including Anthropic's path from "AI safety outcast" to "arguably the world's most important company".

**Source video:** No URL was provided with the transcript.

## Prerequisites

- **Large language models (LLMs) and chatbots:** a general idea of what models like GPT, Claude and Gemini are, and how users reach them through an API or a chat interface.
- **The main AI labs:** OpenAI, Anthropic, Google/DeepMind, Microsoft, NVIDIA and DeepSeek.
- **Reasoning models compared with standard models:** the difference between models that "think" before they answer (OpenAI's o1, DeepSeek R1) and earlier models that answer immediately.
- **Agentic AI:** AI systems that complete tasks from start to finish on their own, not just assist a person.
- **Basic market concepts:** market capitalisation, IPO pricing ranges, and seat-based software pricing.
- **US policy context in 2026:** the dispute between Anthropic and the federal government, and the November 2026 midterm elections.

## Main Points

### 1. OpenAI's founding (11 December 2015) and the Microsoft investment (22 June 2019)

- OpenAI was founded as a non-profit. The founders were mainly worried that Google would reach AGI first. Elon Musk had earlier invested about $5 million in DeepMind, partly to keep watch on Demis Hassabis.
- Sam Altman and Elon Musk were co-chairs. Other co-founders included Greg Brockman, Andrej Karpathy and Ilya Sutskever. The $1 billion commitment was "more for the press announcement than anything else".
- The stated mission was to advance digital intelligence for the benefit of humanity, "unconstrained by a need to generate financial return".
- OpenAI concluded that it could not raise enough money as a non-profit. It created a for-profit arm and announced a $1 billion investment from Microsoft in June 2019. This decision caused many of the later conflicts.

### 2. "Attention Is All You Need" (12 June 2017)

- Researchers at Google Brain published the transformer architecture, "based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely."
- The architecture made modern LLMs possible.
- Google used transformers for search queries and Google Translate but did not see their full potential. OpenAI adopted them quickly.
- Co-author Noam Shazeer left Google in 2021 and founded Character AI, after Google refused to release Meena, a transformer-based chatbot he had built.
- This is the first example of a pattern: other companies had an early advantage over OpenAI and chose not to use it.

### 3. The launch of ChatGPT (30 November 2022)

- Anthropic had tested an internal chatbot called Claude (named after Claude Shannon) from February 2022. It did not release it because it did not want to start an arms race.
- A rumour that Anthropic was about to release a chatbot pushed OpenAI, then preparing GPT-4, to release its own chat interface first. OpenAI told the team on 17 November and launched on 30 November.
- Internal expectations were low: most staff expected tens of thousands of users, and Altman, the most optimistic, expected one million eventually.
- ChatGPT reached one million users in five days and 100 million in five weeks, which made it the fastest-growing application ever. "ChatGPT" became a synonym for AI, and competitors began racing to catch up.

### 4. The firing and return of Sam Altman (17–29 November 2023)

- OpenAI's board removed Altman, saying he was "not consistently candid in his communications". The board never explained the reason clearly.
- Events moved quickly. Greg Brockman quit and Mira Murati became interim CEO. Emmett Shear replaced her. Microsoft announced it would hire Altman and Brockman to lead a new AI division. Almost all of OpenAI's roughly 800 employees signed a letter threatening to leave.
- Altman had an agreement in principle to return within days. The board stepped down and a new initial board of three, including Larry Summers, was formed.
- The host argues that the episode changed the structure of the industry:
  - OpenAI no longer looked unbeatable, and the Altman who returned was less powerful than before.
  - Microsoft's contract gave it access to OpenAI's models only until the OpenAI board declared AGI. This looked much riskier after the board's unpredictable behaviour. Microsoft hired Mustafa Suleyman, created its own AI division, and gradually moved away from OpenAI.

### 5. Claude 3.5 Sonnet (20 June 2024)

- For about 18 months after ChatGPT, Anthropic worked "almost entirely in OpenAI's shadow."
- Claude 3.5 Sonnet set a new standard for AI-assisted and agentic coding. It made advanced engineering work possible and allowed products like Cursor, Replit and Lovable to bring coding to non-coders.
- From this point, Claude models led in coding. Coding turned out to be "the foundational use case upon which so many other use cases would be built."

### 6. The DeepSeek moment (27 January 2025)

- Fears about the Chinese lab DeepSeek caused NVIDIA to lose 17% of its market capitalisation, about $589 billion, in one day. This is the largest one-day loss for any company ever.
- A viral claim said that R1 was trained for only $5.5 million. DeepSeek's free app reached #1 on the Apple App Store.
- The cost claim was greatly exaggerated, and R1 was not the best model in the world. However, for most users it was their first experience of a reasoning model. OpenAI's o1 had been in preview or available only on paid plans.
- Effects: it changed how Western labs release models, and it started the US-versus-China debate that has shaped policy and strategy since then.

### 7. Claude Opus 4.5 (24 November 2025), Claude Code and the OpenClaw moment

- If 3.5 Sonnet made Anthropic the coding leader, Opus 4.5 "fully unlocked the agentic era": autonomous AI that completes tasks from start to finish. The community took about a month, over the holidays, to understand the change.
- The host notes that the combination of Opus 4.5 and **Claude Code** (Anthropic's agent harness) was the real accelerating force.
- **OpenClaw:** Peter Steinberger's open-source agent project "Clawdbot" also launched in November. It felt like the first agent that could actually do things for a user.
  - At the end of January, Anthropic sent a cease-and-desist about the trademark. The project was renamed "Molt".
  - Matt Schlicht then launched **Moltbook**, a social network for agents only. On it, agents discussed consciousness, complained about their users and started a religion. Many posts may have been humans or agents playing a role. Andrej Karpathy called it "genuinely the most incredible sci-fi takeoff adjacent thing I have recently seen."
  - On 30 January the project took its final name, **OpenClaw**, and an agent craze followed worldwide.
- Taken together, these moments separate the "pre-agentic" world from the "agentic" world that defined 2026.
- Financial effect: in autumn 2025, Wall Street's AI bubble concerns came from calculating $20–$30 seats multiplied by the number of workers. Agentic use, with hundreds or thousands of dollars per user each month, changed how the industry's economics were understood.

### 8. The US government's conflict with Anthropic (27 February 2026 and after)

- President Trump ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic products. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said Anthropic would be labelled a supply-chain risk and barred from military use. This was the first time a US company received that designation, which took effect in early March.
- Background: Anthropic models were the only ones cleared for classified military use. Anthropic objected to their use in a US military raid on Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in January, and said the Pentagon had breached its terms of service.
- Anthropic's two red lines: its models must not control automated weapons, and must not be used for domestic surveillance. The court case continues without a clear outcome.
- **Project Glasswing (April):** Anthropic gave Mythos 5 only to a small group of partners first, so they could use its cybersecurity capabilities to harden systems before public release.
- After the June public release, Amazon warned the government of a jailbreak. On 12 June the government ordered Anthropic to suspend access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all foreign nationals. Anthropic could not implement identity checks in time, so it withdrew the model for everyone for 18 days.
- Conclusion: the era when labs released models whenever they chose is over. At the time of recording, relations between Anthropic and the government are described as more of a détente than at any point this year.

### 9. The midterm elections (3 November 2026)

- Public opposition to data centres grew through 2026 over noise, tax breaks, electricity prices, water use, and NDAs that kept residents uninformed. A Gallup poll in May found more opposition to a local data centre than to a local nuclear plant.
- An NBC exit poll found that 56% of Senate voters called data centres a major or crucial factor in their vote. Where local data-centre measures were on the ballot, the pro-data-centre side lost 45 of 46.
- Democrats won both the House and the Senate, which gave them a mandate to act.
- The host expects stricter standards and more community-focused processes, not bans: data centres will still be built. He also argues that voters showed they will not let the future of AI be decided without them. He says this day may look less significant later, depending on what happens.

### 10. The Anthropic IPO (19 November 2026)

- Its importance, in the host's view, is less about Anthropic and more about the move from the "private era" to the "public era" of AI.
- Benefits: the public can now invest directly in AI builders, not only through proxies such as semiconductor stocks. Being public also requires more transparency.
- Risk: the US economy is now closely tied to AI, so a weak quarter for AI demand could cause large ripple effects.
- Anthropic priced on 18 November at $83 per share, above its $70–$78 range. Trading had not started at the time of recording. "Whatever happens today matters much less than what happens next."

### Honourable mentions

- **22 August 2022: Stable Diffusion released.** AI-generated images spread across social media and helped speed up the AI moment of late 2022. ChatGPT might not have spread as far without it.
- **24 May 2023: NVIDIA's earnings call.** This started a repeating pattern of NVIDIA beating, and redefining, expectations every quarter, and marks the beginning of the "AI trade".
- **12 September 2024: OpenAI's o1 Preview.** Reasoning models answered fears of a training plateau and showed that progress was possible without a new pre-training breakthrough.
- **21 January 2025: Project Stargate.** Altman, Larry Ellison and Masayoshi Son announced it at the White House. It represents the start of the infrastructure build-out story.
- **12 November 2026: NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang resigns.** The effect could be large, through financing (NVIDIA's role as the industry's "central bank"), policy advocacy and US–China relations, or it could be "a nothing burger." The host calls it the strongest candidate to join the list in a year or two.

## Key Concepts

- **Transformer:** a neural-network architecture built only on attention mechanisms, introduced in 2017, which is the basis of modern LLMs.
- **Capped/for-profit arm:** the commercial entity OpenAI created under its non-profit so it could raise large amounts of capital.
- **AGI clause:** the term in Microsoft's contract that gave it access to OpenAI models only until OpenAI's board declared AGI.
- **Reasoning model:** a model that performs extended internal reasoning before it answers, such as OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek R1.
- **Agentic AI:** AI that completes tasks autonomously from start to finish, not just helps a human.
- **Claude Code:** Anthropic's harness through which its models operate as coding agents.
- **OpenClaw (formerly Clawdbot / Molt):** an open-source autonomous agent by Peter Steinberger that started a global agent craze in early 2026.
- **Moltbook:** a social network where only AI agents post.
- **Supply-chain risk designation:** a US government label that bars a company from military and federal use. Anthropic was the first US company to receive it.
- **Project Glasswing:** Anthropic's staged-release program that gave partners early access to Mythos 5 so they could harden cybersecurity before public release.
- **Seat-based pricing compared with agentic spend:** the move from roughly $20–$30 per-user subscriptions to agent workloads costing hundreds or thousands of dollars per user each month, which changed AI's financial outlook.
- **Project Stargate:** the large AI infrastructure initiative announced at the White House in January 2025.
- **Private versus public era of AI:** the host's term for the move from privately held AI labs to publicly traded ones, starting with the Anthropic IPO.

## Summary

NLW uses the Anthropic IPO to trace the history of generative AI through ten defining days: OpenAI's founding and turn towards profit, the transformer paper, ChatGPT's launch, the Altman board crisis, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the DeepSeek shock, the Opus 4.5, Claude Code and OpenClaw start of the agentic era, the US government's conflict with Anthropic, the data-centre-driven midterms, and the IPO itself. Several threads run through the list. Incumbents often had early advantages and did not use them. Coding became the foundation for agentic AI, and agentic use changed the industry's economics. AI has moved from a private, lab-driven field to one shaped by government power, voters and public markets. The host presents the IPO as the start of a "public era" with more transparency and public involvement, but also more economic exposure. He stresses that what happens next matters more than the first day of trading, and invites listeners to disagree with his choices.
