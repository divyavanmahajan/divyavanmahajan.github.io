## Overview

**Title:** AI Won't Make Our Minds Useless, According to 400 Years of History
**Source:** *The AI Daily Brief* (Long Read Sunday), hosted by **NLW** — episode dated 2026-11-01. *No source URL was supplied with this transcript; the episode is a podcast/video edition of The AI Daily Brief rather than a conference talk, and the host notes the original essay is linked in the show notes.*

The episode is a "Long Read Sunday" instalment in which the host reads and comments on substantial excerpts from an essay called **"Cognitive Mercantilism"** by writer and strategist **Venkatesh Rao**. The material therefore has three attributional layers, which this document keeps distinct:

1. **Joyce Appleby**, *The Relentless Revolution: A History of Capitalism* — the historical source Rao draws on.
2. **Venkatesh Rao** — the essay's argument, which maps Appleby's account of pre-capitalist mercantilism onto present-day anxiety about AI.
3. **NLW** — the host, who reads the excerpts and interleaves his own previously developed concepts (demand elasticity, the "infinite backlog") plus a closing caveat.

The central thesis is that today's AI panic is structurally the same error as 17th-century mercantilism: it treats intelligence as a fixed, finite stock that must be hoarded, rationed, and guarded, and therefore reads cheap abundant intelligence as a threat that will leave most minds economically redundant. Rao's counter-claim is that the demand for intelligence is as elastic and historically transformative as the demand for material goods turned out to be — so the likely outcome is not a surplus of useless minds but an enormous expansion of what people want to do with intelligence. It matters because it reframes AI policy and labour politics away from preserving existing cognitive jobs and toward securing access to abundant intelligence.

## Prerequisites

- **Mercantilism** — the pre-capitalist doctrine of national wealth as a finite stock of land, produce, and bullion, secured through favourable trade balances and conquest.
- **Malthusianism** — the claim that population growth outruns productive capacity, condemning most people to subsistence.
- **The lump-of-labour fallacy** — the mistaken assumption that there is a fixed quantity of work, so automation necessarily destroys net employment.
- **Elasticity of demand** — how quantity demanded responds to changes in price, access, or other constraints.
- **The "industrious revolution"** — the pre-industrial increase in voluntary working time and productivity driven by new consumer desires.
- **Basic history of capitalism's emergence** in early modern England: agricultural improvement, expanding international trade, and the rise of a consumer culture.
- Familiarity with current AI discourse on job displacement, open weights vs. closed models, talent competition, and AI safety.

## Main Points

### 1. Framing: AI anxiety is about relevance, not just jobs

- Global worry about AI is not confined to hacking or job loss; it centres on what it means for human relevance when the intelligence long felt to be uniquely ours becomes abundant.
- The host argues this is not the first time humanity has faced this class of question.
- 400 years of economic history, he suggests, points to a different and "quite wonderful" outcome.

### 2. Appleby's thesis: capitalism was a contingent invention, not an instinct

- Rao cites Appleby's argument that capitalism was not the inevitable expression of a timeless human instinct to trade and accumulate, but a historically contingent cultural invention.
- It was enabled by agricultural improvements, expanding trade, and changing ideas about work, wealth, and human nature.
- It required escaping a zero-sum worldview in which wealth meant controlling land, bullion, and the labour of impoverished populations.
- The decisive shift was discovering that wealth could be **created** rather than merely captured, and that ordinary people could be sources of demand and initiative rather than only factors of production.

### 3. The four pillars of mercantilism, mapped onto "cognitive mercantilism"

Rao sets out four interlocking mercantilist beliefs and substitutes *intelligence* for *wealth*. Described as a comparison table:

| # | Mercantilism (17th c.) | Cognitive mercantilism (today) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wealth is scarce and finite — land, produce, precious metals. Statecraft means controlling territory, keeping a favourable trade balance, accumulating bullion. One nation's gain is another's loss. | Intelligence is a fixed resource embodied in human brains and the knowledge products mined from them. Strategy means attracting the best brains, controlling their output, accumulating IP, and maintaining a favourable balance of knowledge trade. The global talent race is a treasure hunt for genius brains — and now for AIs alongside copyrightable culture and patentable inventions. |
| 2 | Ordinary people cannot be trusted with freedom; the poor are unruly and must be governed coercively by elites "for their own good." | Supposedly stupid people cannot be trusted to think for themselves and must be governed by cognitive elites. Democratising intelligence is dangerous because the masses will misuse it, fall for nonsense, or unleash chaos. Intelligence is deemed safe only in certified hands. |
| 3 | Economic thinking is supply-oriented: wealth comes from controlling productive resources and commanding high prices. Aggregate demand, especially from the poor, barely registers; only elites are discretionary consumers. | The supply of intelligence is the central economic fact: control its production, preserve its scarcity, keep prices for brains and knowledge products high. The demand side barely registers; less intelligent people are seen as limited cognitive labourers and passive consumers of what elites supply, not as sources of demand or further production. |
| 4 | Most people can do nothing but agricultural labour. ~80% in inefficient subsistence farming is the natural order; remove them from the fields and you get millions of useless mouths to feed. | Most people are not good for much intellectually. If 80% of humanity does low-grade cognitive labour or produces little intelligence at all, that is natural; automating their cognitive work merely threatens to create billions of useless minds to occupy. |

### 4. The agricultural analogy: liberation, not redundancy

- Agricultural productivity gains reduced the share of population needed to produce food from a large majority to a tiny minority in advanced economies.
- The result was not a world of useless mouths but a world where vastly more people did things other than farm *while* enjoying vastly more food.
- Rao's personal illustration: he does not know how to farm and "can barely keep a houseplant alive," yet can obtain foods in varieties and quantities fantastical to an early modern peasant. His inability to *produce* food is economically almost irrelevant; what matters is that he can consume it, develop taste, and spend his energies elsewhere.
- The analogous AI possibility: most people will no longer need to be significant **producers** of intelligence in order to become significant **consumers** of it — which makes cognitive mercantilism look as quaint as its 17th-century predecessor.

### 5. NLW's interjection: six dimensions of demand elasticity

The host pauses the reading to connect the argument to his own earlier episode, "The New Jobs AI Will Create" (from May, with a companion experience still online).

- That episode tried to go beyond the standard optimist claim that "AI will create new jobs" and name actual job categories that might emerge.
- A core part of his argument: the lump-of-labour fallacy in AI job-loss thinking is paired with an implicit assumption of **no elasticity of demand** — that we already demand as much as we could ever demand. He calls that "crazy and completely ahistorical."
- He holds that human capacity to increase demand is near-limitless, constrained mainly by physical limits like hours in a day and calories a body can absorb.
- The six dimensions of demand elasticity he discussed:
  - **Price** — I wanted it, but it cost too much.
  - **Access** — I wanted it, but I couldn't get it.
  - **Complexity** — I needed it, but the system was too confusing.
  - **Continuity** — I get help occasionally, but I benefit from help all the time.
  - **Personalization** — I get the generic version, but I value something made for me.
  - **Relational** — I want this to be more human, meaningful, and trusted.

### 6. "The Revolution in Desire": new demand preceded the steam engine

- Appleby emphasises new kinds of demand emerging in Britain *before* the familiar story of steam engines and factories.
- As international trade expanded, ordinary Britons developed tastes for former luxuries — tobacco, tea, sugar, colourful Indian cottons — creating a new consumer culture.
- People wanted things that were neither subsistence necessities nor traditional aristocratic status markers, and were willing to work harder voluntarily to get them.
- Annual days worked rose from roughly **250 to 300** before the Industrial Revolution. A significant part of this was voluntary rather than coerced: people worked more days, adopted more productive practices, entered new trades, and sought cash income.
- A virtuous cycle formed: productivity gains → surplus income → expanding consumer demand → further productivity gains.

### 7. The historical inversion in labour politics

- Labour politics is usually understood as resistance to exploitation — escaping servitude, coercion, and surplus extraction. Rao accepts this as an important part of the story.
- Appleby's account suggests an equally fundamental second force: the **desire to participate in an expanding universe of consumption opportunities**.
- Modern labour politics can thus be read partly as the politics of *relative deprivation in a society of expanding possibility*. The question shifts from "how to escape domination" to "how to secure a fair share of the growing surplus."
- This is a demand for freedom *to participate*, not merely freedom *from* coercion — arguably one of capitalism's defining political inventions, and a more interesting model for the AI transition than "automation replaces jobs."

### 8. The "cognitive industrious revolution"

- The whole intellectual economy — education, professional expertise, research, consulting, software development, knowledge work — is organised around intelligence being expensive and therefore rationed. AI is undermining that assumption.
- An ordinary person can increasingly command quantities and varieties of intelligence that previously required teams of trained specialists. The key question becomes not how much intelligence people can *produce* but how much they can **find uses for** — with no obvious upper bound.
- Abundant intelligence changes what people can *want*: it makes previously unimaginable projects feasible, inaccessible domains explorable, and unaffordable curiosities worth pursuing. Demand for intelligence is not a fixed appetite but something that grows as the cost of satisfying it falls.
- On apparent stupidity: many people who seem incapable of thought are simply **unmotivated** to think, given poor returns — why think hard when you lack the knowledge, tools, institutional access, or leverage to do anything with the result? Abundant AI raises the returns on attention and therefore the incentive to pay attention.
- Rao predicts a wave of voluntary cognitive effort mirroring the 250→300 working-day shift: "a cognitive industrious revolution, preceding and perhaps enabling a fuller AI industrial revolution." He reports this has happened to him personally — he is willing to work significantly harder than a year ago because much more is within reach.
- Intelligence does not become irrelevant as a human capacity. Just as abundant food produced elaborate culinary cultures rather than killing interest in cooking, abundant intelligence may stimulate increasingly sophisticated cultures of thought, with people becoming *more* intellectually ambitious because they need not do all the production themselves.

### 9. NLW's "infinite backlog"

- The host offers his own parallel explanation for why AI seems to make us work more rather than less.
- At work there is always a basically never-ending backlog running infinitely into the future. It doesn't overwhelm us because natural limits on how much we can do force constant calibration of how much backlog is appropriate to take on. "Done" is not a fixed natural state — it is a negotiation with your own infinite backlog, based on your goals or your employer's.
- Agents break that paradigm: when you can replicate yourself an arbitrary number of times and those replicas work in parallel without sleep, food, or distraction, there is no longer an "appropriate done." The entire backlog becomes unlockable, and the only constraint is your ability to imagine what's on it.
- He notes his framing addresses the *anxiety* side of AI making us work more, whereas Rao is describing the *excitement* side — "the I can't make myself go to sleep because I just want to build one more thing side."

### 10. The politics of cognitive abundance

- If this plays out, AI politics will evolve analogously to the politics of industrial capitalism.
- The central demand will likely *not* be preservation of existing cognitive jobs — any more than industrial labour's enduring demand was to preserve agricultural employment.
- Instead: a demand for a **fair share of expanding cognitive consumption opportunities** — access to intelligence, the ability to command it, the freedom to pursue projects with it, and the institutional rights to participate in an intelligence-rich economy.
- This would be the labour politics of cognitive abundance rather than cognitive scarcity.

### 11. "AI Malthusianism"

- Rao notes mercantilism and Malthusianism are not the same doctrine and belong to different intellectual moments, but share a family resemblance: both imagine economic life as fundamentally constrained by fixed resources.
- The mercantilist worries about controlling a finite stock of wealth; the Malthusian worries that population growth will outrun productive capacity. Both struggle to imagine technological innovation and expanding demand transforming the relationship between population, production, and consumption.
- **AI Malthusianism** is the contemporary analogue: intellectual production expands so fast that humans become economically redundant and surplus intelligence turns toxic.
- This is a peculiar inversion of classical Malthusianism — instead of population outrunning subsistence, the supply of intelligence outruns the supposedly fixed capacity of human minds to absorb it. The conclusion is the same: collapse.
- Both errors assume demand for intelligence is fundamentally limited. Rao's question: what if that demand is as elastic and inventive as demand for material goods proved to be — and the apparent surplus of human minds is an artifact of our failure to imagine what people might want to do in an intelligence-rich world?

### 12. The demand side of AI (flagged as underdeveloped)

- Rao gestures at a more radical extension: AI as not solely a supply-side technology but one that also *produces* demand, noting that many products and services exist primarily because the market demands them, with humans as secondary consumers.
- The host flags this section as deserving an entire second essay and offers his own reading: not that AIs develop consciousness and want things, but that the systems AIs architect — and the systems architected around them — create pathways and needs for further intelligence consumption.

### 13. "Mercantilism Was Not for Merchants"

- The historical irony: mercantilism was not a philosophy of, by, or for merchants. It was largely the philosophy of militaristic statesmen, aristocrats, and rentier elites who saw commerce through the lens of territorial power, unearned privilege, and accumulation.
- Actual merchants, entrepreneurs, and innovators kept discovering possibilities the prevailing political imagination could barely comprehend, and fought mercantilism's self-limiting tendencies — giving birth to capitalism.
- Today's cognitive mercantilists stand in a similar relation to intelligence, starting with the fact that most are not the merchants of AI. Rao adds that even AI-sector business leaders behave as cognitive mercantilists **in direct proportion to their desire to be more than mere merchants**.
- Across the political spectrum, elites remain attached to intelligence as a scarce, dangerous resource to be managed by credentialed custodians, kept expensive, used for conquest and treasure, and denied to rivals. Democratic abundance — global diffusion, open weights — is therefore experienced primarily as a threat to expertise, cultural and political authority, status, social order, military dominance, and geopolitical leverage.
- Rao's distinction: where elite political and cultural adventuring by CEOs distorts behaviour beyond what actual risks justify, it is **mercantilist rather than capitalist**.

### 14. Closing taxonomy: three stances on abundant intelligence

Rao's concluding comparison, with the quantitative framing he attaches to it — from a world where ~80% of human brains are engaged in "subsistence cognition" to one where ~95% are engaged in satisfying previously unfulfillable cognitive demands:

- **The cognitive mercantilist** — sees a world threatened by an oversupply of intelligence and an impending surplus of useless minds.
- **The AI Malthusian** — sees intelligence outrunning humanity's capacity to absorb it, leaving a growing population of economically redundant minds primed for revolutionary explosion.
- **The cognitive capitalist** — sees almost limitless expansion of demand and a world of minds whose potential has barely begun to be explored.

Rao's closing parallel: the most important consequence of the agricultural revolution was not that farming became more efficient, but that most people were liberated from the necessity of farming, letting entirely new kinds of economic and cultural life emerge. Likewise, the most important consequence of the AI revolution may not be more efficient intelligence production, but that most minds — human and artificial — are liberated from producing intelligence merely to survive, and become free to discover what they actually want to do with it.

### 15. The host's closing commentary and caveat

- The host observes the essay is in a way both describing and enacting its own point: provocative writing pulls readers in and makes them use their minds for something beyond answering the memo at work or the question at school, letting thought "roam out into the cognitive ether."
- He frames Rao's core question as: do most people avoid traversing those fuzzy, foggy areas because they lack capacity and interest, or because the surplus of time and justification has never been available? Rao's optimistic answer is clearly the latter — yielding a world whose defining condition is not having been replaced but having been *allowed to become explorers*.
- **Explicit caveat:** he states that none of this is a direct argument for or against any particular AI safety consideration, at least not in this version of the essay. There is no mutual exclusivity between being excited and optimistic about this possibility while also being concerned about the transitional period in which AI does work previously done by human brains.
- Transitions between orders, even when the resulting order is good, are never without cost — but it looks quite different whether the response is to address the challenges of the transition or to try to stop the transition from happening.
- He notes he did not read the entire essay and recommends reading the original, linked in the show notes.

## Key Concepts

- **Cognitive mercantilism** — Rao's coinage for treating intelligence as a finite stock to be hoarded, rationed, credentialed, and denied to rivals, by analogy with bullion-hoarding mercantilism.
- **Cognitive capitalism** — The contrasting stance: intelligence can be created rather than captured, and demand for it can expand almost limitlessly.
- **AI Malthusianism** — The belief that intelligence production will outrun humanity's fixed capacity to absorb it, rendering most minds economically redundant.
- **Mercantilism** — The pre-capitalist doctrine that national wealth is a finite stock of land, produce, and precious metals, secured by trade surpluses and conquest.
- **Malthusianism** — The doctrine that population growth outruns productive capacity, holding most people at subsistence.
- **The Relentless Revolution: A History of Capitalism (Joyce Appleby)** — The historical account Rao builds on, arguing capitalism was a contingent cultural invention rather than a timeless instinct.
- **The industrious revolution** — The pre-industrial rise in voluntary working days (roughly 250 → 300 per year) driven by newly accessible consumer goods.
- **Cognitive industrious revolution** — Rao's projected analogue: a wave of voluntary cognitive effort triggered by newly accessible intellectual consumption, potentially preceding a fuller AI industrial revolution.
- **The revolution in desire** — Appleby's point that new consumer demand (tobacco, tea, sugar, Indian cottons) emerged before, and helped enable, industrialisation.
- **Subsistence cognition** — Rao's term for low-grade cognitive labour performed merely to survive, mapped onto subsistence farming.
- **Lump-of-labour fallacy** — The mistaken premise that there is a fixed amount of work, so machine labour necessarily displaces human labour.
- **Demand elasticity (six dimensions)** — NLW's framework for how AI unlocks latent demand: price, access, complexity, continuity, personalization, and relational.
- **The infinite backlog** — NLW's concept that work extends infinitely into the future, so "done" is a negotiated calibration rather than a natural state — a negotiation agents dissolve by removing the capacity constraint.
- **Returns on attention** — Rao's mechanism for why abundant AI increases thinking: when a modest investment of attention unlocks large possibilities, the incentive to think rises.
- **Labour politics of cognitive abundance** — The projected shift from demanding preservation of cognitive jobs to demanding a fair share of access to, and rights to use, abundant intelligence.

## Summary

The episode presents Venkatesh Rao's essay "Cognitive Mercantilism" as an original reframing of AI anxiety. Drawing on Joyce Appleby's history of capitalism, Rao argues that today's fears about abundant machine intelligence replicate, almost point for point, the four self-limiting beliefs of 17th-century mercantilism: that the valuable resource is a finite stock to be hoarded, that ordinary people cannot be trusted with it, that only supply matters, and that most of humanity is fit for nothing better than the drudgery it currently performs. Capitalism emerged when those assumptions broke — when wealth turned out to be creatable rather than merely capturable, and when ordinary people's expanding desires proved to be an engine of voluntary effort and productivity rather than a burden. Rao's wager is that intelligence will follow the same path: just as agricultural abundance liberated most people from farming rather than producing useless mouths, abundant AI may liberate most minds from producing intelligence to survive, letting them become prodigious *consumers* of it and, in the process, far more intellectually ambitious than before. The limit, on this reading, is not the supply of intelligence but our imagination about what people would want to do in an intelligence-rich world — which makes the likely political demand not the preservation of cognitive jobs but a fair share of access to intelligence. The host endorses the optimism and connects it to his own work on demand elasticity and the infinite backlog, while closing with an explicit caveat: this is not an argument for or against any AI safety position, since one can be optimistic about the destination and still concerned about the transition — and responding to the costs of a transition is a very different posture from trying to prevent it.
