## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, a daily podcast and video about AI news, is titled "A Field Guide to AI Market Freakouts." The host is not named in the transcript. The episode argues that investor anxiety about AI follows a small set of recurring patterns. The host says that seeing these patterns, and the context around them, shows why each round of fear tends to pass. He also argues that the market's constant fear of a bubble actually makes a runaway bubble less likely.

The episode opens with the latest round of concern. US officials have accused Chinese AI labs, Moonshot in particular, of distillation attacks and of evading export controls. Investors worry that cheap Chinese models could undercut the revenue of OpenAI and Anthropic as both prepare to go public. The episode then sorts past market freakouts into types, adds caveats, and gives the host's view on how this round will be resolved.

The topic matters because AI is now a structural part of the US economy and the stock market. Around half of the S&P 500 is AI or AI-exposed stocks, so even passive index investors and 401(k) holders have significant AI exposure.

## Prerequisites

- **Basic market concepts:** capital expenditure (CapEx), earnings reports, revenue growth, stock momentum, and how investors use narratives to price stocks.
- **The AI industry:** the main frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic), hyperscalers (Google, Meta, Amazon), and chip suppliers (NVIDIA).
- **Model training and serving:** the difference between pre-training, reasoning models, and inference, and what "tokens" means as a unit of AI use and billing.
- **Model distillation:** training a model on the outputs of another model.
- **Export controls and the entity list:** US tools for restricting foreign companies' access to technology such as NVIDIA GPUs.
- **Historical reference points:** the dot-com bubble of 1999–2000, the 2022–2023 Fed rate-hiking cycle, and the January 2025 "DeepSeek moment."

## Main Points

### 1. The current round: Chinese AI, distillation, and US policy
- Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Chinese AI companies could face sanctions and entity-list designations for "covert industrial-scale distillation attacks." In his words: "open source is not open season on American IP."
- White House OSTP Director Michael Kratsios alleged that Moonshot AI distilled Anthropic's Fable to build its Kimi K3 model. He said Moonshot used an internal platform that could switch between access methods to avoid detection. He also said Moonshot got access to GB300 servers, including GB300s in Thailand.
- Anthropic's head of public policy, Sarah Heck, confirmed that Anthropic is working with the administration. The Information reported that the Commerce Department is investigating Moonshot and other Chinese labs for getting around export controls.
- The administration is reported to be split. According to Wired, parts of the White House want stricter controls, while the Commerce Department sees restrictions as unworkable. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick reportedly prefers to compete, including with incentives for US labs to open-source their models.
- Critics (Signal) argued that blocking Chinese models would raise costs for American businesses without stopping distillation. AEI's Ryan Fedasiuk called it possibly "the most important week for US AI policy so far in 2026."

### 2. Why markets are so sensitive to AI
- Since ChatGPT launched in late 2022, during the Fed's rate hikes, the market has been "AI versus everything." AI investment continued even while rates kept rising.
- Bloomberg reports that AI investment is 25% of US GDP growth, the largest contribution from any single sector in history.
- JP Morgan analysts say that since ChatGPT's release, AI has driven 75% of S&P 500 returns, 80% of earnings growth, and 90% of capital spending growth.
- As a result, the host describes the US economy, in a simplified way, as a bet that AI will produce enough revenue to justify the infrastructure build-out. This makes talk of an AI bubble a central part of market analysis, not just a headline.

### 3. Freakout type: cheap models undercutting the premium labs
- This is the current concern. It goes back to the DeepSeek moment in early 2025, when markets believed a Chinese lab had built frontier AI for a few million dollars.
- The DeepSeek story fell apart over time. People learned the difference between training compute and inference compute, some of DeepSeek's claims were qualified, and R1 turned out not to be ahead of US models. It was simply the first free reasoning model, while OpenAI's o1 was behind a subscription.
- Kimi K3, preceded by GLM 5.2 while Fable was offline because of the US government, has created a new DeepSeek-style moment. It arrived after months of stories about US companies cutting token budgets.
- K3 is cheaper, but not dramatically so. It costs about a third of Fable's price, or half of Opus's. That is a real saving, but "not pennies on the dollar."
- What is real is that US companies are trying to manage AI budgets, especially as agentic use cases grow.

### 4. Freakout type: circular financing
- This peaked from late summer to fall of last year, when diagrams spread showing NVIDIA's many deals across the industry. One focus was a reported deal for NVIDIA to invest up to $100B in OpenAI.
- The fear was that NVIDIA's investment would come straight back to NVIDIA as chip revenue, inflating revenue all along the supply chain.
- The deal closed in February at $30B, alongside $50B from Amazon and $30B from SoftBank.
- The dot-com comparison: companies like Cisco financed equipment sales to startups and were left with losses when those startups failed. That practice was later made illegal.
- Key differences this time: NVIDIA is not doing vendor financing, because OpenAI pays cash for GPUs. If OpenAI failed, NVIDIA would hold devalued stock, not bad debt. OpenAI and Anthropic are also nothing like pets.com in scale, legitimacy, or revenue.

### 5. Freakout type: revenue not keeping up with spending (and its twin, CapEx growing too fast)
- This concern returns every quarter at hyperscaler earnings. Early in the year, some sign of AI return on investment was enough. In January, Meta's 24% revenue growth sent its stock up 10%.
- Investors now want more. Google reported 24% overall growth and 82% year-over-year cloud growth, but the market focused on CapEx guidance of $200B. The Wall Street Journal wrote that Alphabet had found the limit of Wall Street's tolerance. Brian Mulberry of Sachs Investment Management called $200B "the do-not-cross line." The stock fell 1.2% overnight.
- Google had guided around $185B at the start of the year, so $200B is only about an 8% increase, mostly from supply-chain cost pressure. The host reads the reaction as partly a psychological response to a big round number.
- Hyperscalers have raised CapEx guidance every quarter for three years. Combined CapEx may exceed $1 trillion next year. The more CapEx grows, the steeper the hill revenue has to climb.
- These are uncharted waters. Companies have gone from $1B to $30B in revenue in under two years, which has no precedent.
- The bubble story of late 2025 calmed because of agentic use cases. Employees began spending hundreds or thousands of dollars a month, which broke investor models that just multiplied knowledge-worker seats by a SaaS-style $20–30.

### 6. Freakout type: limits on AI spending
- Last year's version was the MIT report claiming 95% of generative AI pilots fail. The host calls the study "absolute garbage social science," but notes the headline reached every desk on Wall Street.
- This year's version has more basis in reality: companies are capping token use.
- The actual policies are milder than the headlines. Uber caps users at $1,500 a month. Tesla allows $200 of tokens a week, and workers can ask for more.
- Most knowledge workers are nowhere near those levels, so there is still a lot of room to grow even if caps become common. Still, the market expects everyone to look for ways to control token budgets, which adds pressure toward cheaper inference.

### 7. Freakout type: performance plateaus
- The logic: if performance stalls, AI can do less, companies spend less, revenue grows more slowly, and CapEx becomes harder to justify.
- This was the main fear in fall 2024, the "pre-training scaling wall," with rumors that Anthropic and OpenAI had abandoned flagship pre-training runs.
- Reasoning models (o1) soon showed that pre-training was not the only way to improve performance. Pre-training has since clearly not hit a wall either; the host says Fable V is "an entirely different beast" from Opus III.
- The host describes that fear as "quaint" in hindsight. He criticizes commentators such as Gary Marcus and Ed Zitron for pushing it.

### 8. Caveat: seasonality
- AI fear, uncertainty and doubt (FUD) follows a consistent seasonal pattern. Summer doldrums are well documented, and research shows momentum breaks down in summer, especially when momentum stocks such as semiconductors lead the rally.
- Morgan Stanley noted momentum stocks were down 40% this month, the worst month on record, beating 29% in early 2021.
- Goldman Sachs reported record hedge fund selling of tech stocks. The host says the drawdown is not just a market quirk, but the fears should be read in light of this seasonality.

### 9. Caveat: constant bubble talk prevents a bubble
- The host says the market's constant bubble logic, which he partly blames on a generation shaped by *The Big Short*, works as a pressure release whenever prices get ahead of themselves.
- There is none of the frenzy of 1999–2000, when boom-and-bust IPOs with 100x gains and huge losses happened every other day.
- The bubble talk of late 2025 faded because agents changed the economics and the numbers showed up in Anthropic's results: rational concern, followed by evidence against it.

### 10. How this round may resolve
- **Room to run:** We use only a tiny fraction of the intelligence we will eventually demand, so token caps matter much less than they seem to.
- **Capacity can't outrun demand:** Data centers take a long time to build. Half of the data centers announced this year have been canceled or delayed. The host says that reflects permitting difficulty and local opposition, which builders have handled badly, not weak demand. He says there is "literally zero evidence" for the weak-demand reading.
- **Compute constraints on Chinese labs:** A cheap model doesn't matter without the inference capacity to serve it. Moonshot ran out of compute on its opening weekend. The host doubts all Chinese labs together could serve even a small fraction of US labs' users.
- **A flood of new companies:** Routers, fine-tuning and post-training companies (many specialized by industry), and alternative model architectures are emerging to meet demand for cheaper inference, even before any US open-source incentives.
- Investor Nick Carter's view: the government does not owe the big labs a business model. If token economics fail, enterprises, hyperscalers, neoclouds, internet companies, and consumers will still do well from falling costs. Only OpenAI and Anthropic, in their current form, would have to adapt.

### 11. The host's base case
- For at least the next five years, every frontier token will sell at a premium, even as cheaper workloads come online. Demand for state-of-the-art tokens will exceed supply.
- Alternative architectures spread AI's revenue gains more widely, so OpenAI and Anthropic don't have to carry the whole market.
- Enterprise inertia and the long timeline of infrastructure build-out give the economy more time to adapt than model capability alone would.
- New rounds of FUD will keep coming, often matching market seasonality. These patterned freakouts ultimately make a full bubble less likely.

## Key Concepts

- **AI market freakout / FUD:** A recurring wave of investor fear, uncertainty and doubt about whether AI investment will pay off.
- **Distillation (adversarial distillation):** Training a model on a stronger model's outputs. US officials call covert, large-scale distillation against US models IP theft.
- **Entity list:** A US Commerce Department list that restricts named foreign organizations from getting US technology.
- **Export controls:** Rules limiting the sale or transfer of advanced chips (e.g., NVIDIA GB300s) to certain countries or companies.
- **DeepSeek moment:** The early-2025 market shock when investors believed a Chinese lab had matched frontier AI at a tiny fraction of US costs. The term now describes similar cheap-model scares.
- **Circular financing:** A setup where an investor's money flows back to it as revenue (e.g., NVIDIA investing in OpenAI, which buys NVIDIA chips), raising doubts about whether the revenue is real.
- **Vendor financing:** A supplier lending customers the money to buy its products. It contributed to the dot-com crash and is distinct from the current cash-based deals.
- **CapEx (capital expenditure):** Spending on long-term assets such as data centers and chips. Investors now watch it closely against AI revenue.
- **Hyperscalers:** Very large cloud providers (e.g., Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta) that build the infrastructure AI runs on.
- **Neoclouds:** Newer cloud providers focused on GPU compute for AI workloads.
- **Token caps / token budgets:** Company limits on how much employees can spend on AI model usage, measured in tokens or dollars.
- **Agentic use cases:** AI agents doing extended, autonomous work. This drives much higher spending per employee than chat-style use.
- **Pre-training scaling wall:** The fall-2024 idea that making pre-training bigger had stopped improving performance.
- **Reasoning models:** Models such as OpenAI's o1 that improve results by spending more computation at inference time, offering a way to improve beyond pre-training.
- **Momentum breakdown / summer doldrums:** The seasonal tendency for leading momentum stocks to sell off in summer.
- **Router (model router):** Software that sends each request to the most suitable or cheapest model.

## Summary

The host argues that investor fear about AI comes back again and again in a handful of forms: cheap competitors (especially Chinese models) undercutting the premium labs, circular financing, revenue failing to keep up with ever-larger CapEx, limits on AI spending such as token caps, and performance plateaus. Because AI now drives a large share of US GDP growth, stock returns, and capital spending, markets are especially nervous about any sign of reversal. The current round, set off by US allegations that Moonshot distilled Anthropic's Fable and evaded export controls, is the latest version of a pattern going back to DeepSeek. The host says these fears should be read in light of summer seasonality and the evidence that has repeatedly answered past concerns: agentic spending, the arrival of reasoning models, and the continued gains from pre-training. He expects this round to fade as people recognize several things: demand for intelligence is far from saturated, infrastructure cannot be built faster than demand grows, Chinese labs lack the compute to serve at scale, and a wave of new companies is spreading AI's economics more widely. His conclusion is that new freakouts will keep coming, but this constant, patterned skepticism is itself one of the strongest protections against a true runaway bubble.
