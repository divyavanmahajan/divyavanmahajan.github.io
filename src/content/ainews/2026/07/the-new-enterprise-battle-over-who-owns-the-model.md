## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, a daily podcast about AI news, argues that the enterprise AI market is moving away from a simple choice between OpenAI and Anthropic. The new question is **who owns and customises the model**. The main story is the release of **Inkling**, the first large language model from **Thinking Machines Lab (TML)**, the lab founded by former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati. On benchmarks, Inkling is competitive but not state-of-the-art. The host says it matters because it was built as an open-weight base model for fine-tuning on TML's **Tinker** platform. It addresses two enterprise concerns: data sovereignty and rising token costs.

The headlines share the same theme. Cursor is aiming to become a frontier model developer. Microsoft is training its sales teams to sell its in-house MAI models against Claude. Apple is looking to buy an AI chipmaker. NVIDIA has released a small open model for physical AI.

## Prerequisites

- **LLM basics:** parameters, context windows, multimodality, and reasoning or "thinking" modes.
- **Open-weight vs. closed models:** the difference between models whose weights can be downloaded and run on your own infrastructure, and models that are only available through a vendor API.
- **Fine-tuning and post-training:** adapting a pre-trained base model with extra data, including supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning.
- **Distillation:** training one model on outputs generated by another model.
- **Common AI benchmarks:** Humanity's Last Exam, SWE-Bench Pro, Terminal Bench, GDPVal, and the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index.
- **Enterprise AI buying concerns:** data governance, token costs, vendor lock-in, and regulatory or geopolitical limits on where models can run.
- **The "bitter lesson":** Rich Sutton's argument that general methods using more data and compute tend to beat approaches built on hand-crafted human expertise.

## Main Points

### 1. Cursor wants to become a frontier model lab
- *The Information* reported on a Cursor all-hands meeting in May. There, CEO Michael Truell said Cursor would aim to be a top-tier model developer and not only an AI coding company.
- His goals were a state-of-the-art model by the end of the year, and by 2027 a significant compute advantage and real progress at the frontier.
- The meeting came a few weeks after the SpaceX deal was announced. That deal was a compute and training partnership with an option to acquire. Truell acknowledged that some staff were uneasy about it. SpaceX wants to use Cursor's brand, enterprise relationships and go-to-market team.
- Grok 4.5 is the first SpaceX AI model trained with Cursor. Leaving aside a controversy over data retention, it has been well received. Cursor is also rumoured to be building a competitor to Claude Cowork, which would be its first big move beyond coding.
- The host calls this "more evolution than revolution", because Cursor had already decided by late last year that it would need to build models.

### 2. SpaceX stock and what it means for AI IPOs
- SpaceX traded at $133, below its $135 IPO price, for the first time. It closed at $135.27. The stock is 33% below its all-time high, and Elon Musk is no longer a trillionaire. Bears point out that insider lockups do not start for another month.
- The bigger effect may be on other AI companies planning to go public. As of late June, OpenAI was reportedly leaning toward waiting until next year, because advisors doubted it could reach Sam Altman's $1 trillion target valuation.
- Bloomberg reports that Anthropic has appointed banks to lead its offering, is setting up investor meetings, and has added a revolving credit facility of a couple of billion dollars. It is targeting a September or October IPO. The next few weeks of investor meetings will test whether that is viable.

### 3. NVIDIA Cosmos 3 Edge and physical AI
- Cosmos 3 Edge is a 4-billion-parameter open model built to run on edge hardware inside robots. The transcript renders this hardware as "Jensen", most likely a mis-transcription of NVIDIA's Jetson line, which was also refreshed this week.
- It can work both as a **world model** and as a **vision-language model**.
- It follows Nemotron 3 Ultra, released last month, which met demand for powerful, cheap US-based open models as token budgets grow.
- Related news: 1X previewed a new version of its Neo humanoid robot. NVIDIA expanded its partnership with Toyota from self-driving cars to robots, smart cities and automated factories.
- As an aside, the host highlights **Chip** (chipmotors.com), a small AI-enabled autonomous family vehicle. It can run errands or park itself on its own, which the host sees as an early sign of AI moving into the physical world.

### 4. Apple looks for an AI chip acquisition
- *The Information* reports that Apple has talked to bankers about financing deals and has approached semiconductor startups.
- Apple rarely makes big acquisitions. Its largest so far is Beats in 2014, for about $3 billion.
- The reason given is that Apple runs its AI servers on M2 Ultra chips, which may not be good enough. Its own server chip, codenamed **Baltra**, has reportedly been delayed. Apple already uses Google for the models behind Siri and rents Google Cloud capacity running on NVIDIA chips.
- There are few proven targets. NVIDIA paid $20 billion to take Groq off the market. Cerebras is valued at $40 billion. Broadcom, at about $1.8 trillion, would be a merger rather than an acquisition and would draw heavy antitrust scrutiny. Tenstorrent, led by former Apple chip designer Jim Keller, was mentioned as a natural fit, but there are no solid rumours.

### 5. Microsoft sells its own models against OpenAI and Anthropic
- According to Bloomberg, Microsoft told its sales teams at the start of the new fiscal year to push the efficiency, cost-effectiveness and vertically integrated stack of its in-house **MAI** models.
- The first target is Claude. Rather than compete on overall model quality, sales staff are told to say that inside Office, Claude is "slower and less accurate and lacked the proper security integrations."
- Microsoft has already moved some Copilot functions to MAI to cut costs, and customers may not have noticed.
- This fits Satya Nadella's recent posts suggesting that companies should not trust OpenAI or Anthropic with their data, because those labs have an incentive to build competing services.
- The host's view is that this gets MAI into accounts first, so Microsoft can then upsell customer-specific fine-tuning through **Microsoft Frontier Tuning**, announced last month by Mustafa Suleyman. TML's Tinker API, launched at the end of last year, was the first offering of this kind.

### 6. Inkling: TML's first large language model
- **Architecture:** mixture of experts, with 975 billion total parameters and 41 billion active. It has a 1-million-token context window and natively handles text, images and audio.
- **Family:** Inkling is the first of several planned models. **Inkling Small**, with 12 billion parameters, was released in preview the same day.
- **Size comparison:** smaller than DeepSeek V4 Pro (the transcript says "1.6 billion", most likely 1.6 trillion) and larger than Z.ai's GLM at 750 billion.
- **Training lineage:** pre-trained from scratch. Apart from a small bootstrap distilled from Kimi K2.5, post-training used TML's own human-created and synthetic data.
- **TML's own framing:** "Inkling is not the strongest overall model available today, open or closed." TML presents it as a good open-weight base for customisation because of its multimodality, efficient thinking, and availability on Tinker.

### 7. Inkling's benchmark results

| Benchmark | Inkling | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Humanity's Last Exam | 29.7% without tools, 46% with tools | Ahead of Nemotron 3 Ultra; about level with Kimi K2.5; about 10 points behind K2.6, GLM 5.2 and GPT-5.6 Sol; about 20 points behind Fable 5 |
| SWE-Bench Pro | 54.3% | Ahead of Nemotron and K2.5; behind K2.6; well behind GLM 5.2, 5.6 Sol and Fable 5 |

- The host notes that benchmarks "don't usually tell much of a story."

### 8. Early reactions are mixed
- **Negative:**
  - Ethan Mollick found it "pretty rough" and "not close to frontier Chinese open weights models." It failed a test that every frontier model has passed since DeepSeek-R1 and Sonnet 3.5, and its chain of thought "goes crazy" on simple requests.
  - Dreams ASI called it a less accurate GPT-5.2 with heavy corporate guardrails and unnecessary refusals.
  - Another commenter thought it looked rushed out ahead of Kimi K3 and the next DeepSeek release.
- **Optimistic about the future:**
  - Yunfang Zhang expects TML's talent, funding and compute to reach Opus or Fable 5 level soon.
  - Another commenter liked that the benchmark results were mediocre, because it suggests TML did not cut corners with distillation and is not "benchmark maxing."
- **Positive about the release itself:**
  - Ben Burtenshaw praised how openly TML described what the model can and cannot do, and that it shipped tools for serving and post-training.
  - Jack Morris called it the only open-weight model trained without distilling from OpenAI or Anthropic. A Community Note on his post pointed out the Kimi K2.5 bootstrap, and that models like Llama 3.1 were also trained from scratch.
  - The host's point: for enterprises looking at open-weight models, an **American model that is not mainly distilled** from closed models could soon become significant.

### 9. The business model: open weights plus paid fine-tuning
- Nathan Lambert: Inkling "was somewhat inevitable once Tinker took off." He called it one of the best open-model business stories to date.
- Jeffrey Emanuel's analysis:
  - Big companies are increasingly worried about leaking their competitive edge (their "alpha") to frontier labs.
  - Open weights let them run models on their own infrastructure.
  - Selling inference is a commoditised race to the bottom.
  - So TML makes money from fine-tuning on each company's internal data, with the benefits kept exclusive to that company.
- TML's launch post closes with a "Customizing Inkling" section. It argues that many real-world problems need fine-tuning on specialised organisational knowledge, and that Inkling was trained broadly so it can adapt to different workflows.
- Daniel Kaplan described a two-tier go-to-market, which he called the rise of the **"forward-deployed fine-tuner"**:
  - Companies with their own AI research teams use Inkling and Tinker self-serve.
  - Companies without one pay TML a lot to send fine-tuning experts.

### 10. Microsoft Frontier Tuning compared with Inkling
- Both Microsoft and TML are telling enterprises to own their learning infrastructure.
- Microsoft Frontier Tuning on MAI still requires trusting Microsoft with company data. Microsoft's decades of enterprise trust make that easier for many customers, but it is different from open weights.
- Some enterprises will mainly want lower costs and be happy with Microsoft. Others will want more independence and sovereignty. Once legal and risk teams get involved, a US-origin, non-distilled model stands out.
- The host suggests a closed-model fine-tuning offering may end up "in between", while TML's open-weight approach covers both token cost and data sovereignty, even if only for a niche market.

### 11. The counter-argument: a bet against the bitter lesson
- Simon Smith asked whether TML is "basically a bet against the bitter lesson" and said he was not convinced. His points:
  - Fine-tuning takes far more ongoing effort than people expect: new data, edge cases, and model updates.
  - Fine-tuned models can lose capabilities or develop unexpected problems.
  - A large general model with a bit of context, such as skills files, often beats a fine-tuned model, and then the cost of that capability falls sharply.
  - Cost comparisons often count only cost per token. They leave out data collection and curation, training pipelines, and infrastructure administration.
- The host's view: recognising the problems of data sovereignty and token cost does not mean the first answers on the market (Frontier Tuning, Tinker) will turn out to be the right ones.

### 12. The broader trend: open models are having a moment
Sriram Krishnan, former White House AI advisor and a16z investor, listed six factors:
1. Near-state-of-the-art performance can now be reached with a clear training lineage.
2. Several well-funded, talented teams are building open-weight models in the US and abroad.
3. Organisations want control over how their data is used and fear frontier labs becoming competitors. They will give up some frontier capability to get that control.
4. Open source works like a slider: you can bring your own harness, evals and business context, and pick whichever model you want.
5. Companies have moved from "how do we get people to use tokens" to worrying about token costs growing without a clear link to revenue.
6. Countries see open-weight models as a way to get frontier-level tokens inside controlled environments.

### 13. What enterprises should do
- A few months ago, the enterprise decision looked like "OpenAI, Anthropic or both." Now there is much more choice: models, harnesses, multi-model architectures and fine-tuning approaches.
- Not every experiment will succeed. The large players may adopt the best ideas within 6 to 12 months and narrow the options again. Even so, this experimental period will shape the solutions that come out of it.
- There is real opportunity for small teams and more nimble enterprises.
- Enterprises do not need to rush to sign up for Tinker or Frontier Tuning, move everything to OpenRouter, or start building on GLM 5.2. But enterprise buyers who are not experimenting with these options, or at least watching them closely, are "missing quite a bit of opportunity."

## Key Concepts

- **Inkling:** TML's first large language model, an open-weight multimodal mixture-of-experts model with 975B total and 41B active parameters, built as a base for customisation.
- **Inkling Small:** a 12B-parameter sibling of Inkling, released in preview.
- **Tinker:** TML's fine-tuning and post-training API and platform, launched in late 2025, on which Inkling can be customised.
- **Thinking Machines Lab (TML):** the AI lab founded by former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati.
- **Mixture of Experts (MoE):** an architecture in which only a subset of the parameters (the "experts") is active for each token, which lowers inference cost.
- **Open-weight model:** a model whose trained weights are published so organisations can run and modify it on their own infrastructure.
- **Distillation:** training a model on outputs from another, usually stronger, model. Here it is used as a marker of training lineage and independence.
- **Microsoft MAI models:** Microsoft's in-house foundation models, increasingly used in Copilot and sold against Claude.
- **Microsoft Frontier Tuning:** Microsoft's service for fine-tuning MAI models on customer-specific data.
- **Forward-deployed fine-tuner:** Daniel Kaplan's term for vendor experts embedded with a customer to build custom fine-tuned models.
- **Data sovereignty:** an organisation's control over where its data lives and how it is used, including the fear of helping a future competitor.
- **The bitter lesson:** the idea that general methods using more data and compute beat approaches that depend on human expertise. It is cited as a challenge to the fine-tuning thesis.
- **Fully loaded cost of fine-tuning:** the total cost of a fine-tuned model, including data curation, pipelines, retraining and infrastructure, not only the per-token price.
- **Cosmos 3 Edge:** NVIDIA's 4B-parameter open model for robots, working as both a world model and a vision-language model.
- **World model:** a model that learns how a physical environment behaves, used in robotics and physical AI.
- **Baltra:** Apple's delayed in-house AI server chip.
- **Harness:** the software layer around a model, such as agents, tools and context, which can be combined with different models.

## Summary

The host argues that enterprise AI is moving from a simple choice between OpenAI and Anthropic toward a contest over who owns, controls and customises the model. Inkling, TML's first large language model, is the central example. It is broad, multimodal and token-efficient, but behind the frontier on benchmarks, and early reviews are rough. Its importance comes from being an American open-weight model that was not mainly distilled from closed models, and from being built as the base for paid fine-tuning on Tinker. That combination addresses two growing enterprise worries: data sovereignty and rising token costs. Microsoft is pushing a similar message through its MAI models and Frontier Tuning, but that still requires trusting Microsoft with company data. Cursor's push into model building and NVIDIA's steady open releases point the same way. Critics such as Simon Smith warn that the full cost of fine-tuning and the bitter lesson may favour general models. The host does not predict which approach will win. The message is that this is a period of real experimentation and choice, and that enterprise buyers who are not testing or closely watching these options are missing significant opportunity.
