# How to Go From AI Power User to AI Leader

## Overview

This is an "Operator's Cut" practical episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, a daily podcast and video about news and discussions in AI, released 2026-10-12 (recorded the prior week, as the release date fell on a US federal holiday). The host is joined by a recurring guest, **Nufar** — no surname or employer is given in the transcript; the host introduces her as someone who has run this type of engagement with dozens of companies in person, and she and the host are launching an **AI Champions Bootcamp**.

Source video URL: not provided with this transcript. The host states he will post the accompanying presentation on the episode's companion website, **aidailybrief.ai**.

The central thesis: in nearly every organisation, a subset of people have become de facto AI power users — they try every new model and product and report back on what works. Across the speakers' enterprise experience, **the single highest-ROI AI transformation activity is structuring these people into a well-run internal "AI champions" network**. The talk argues that this is one of the only interventions that consistently works, and it matters because the common failure mode is otherwise universal: one person finds a great way to do something, demonstrates it, and it never becomes something the whole team uses. The champion's job is to close that gap.

The talk covers three things: what the role actually is and how to define it; how a company should establish a champion network; and what the best champions need to know how to do.

## Prerequisites

Concepts the talk presumes the listener already understands:

- Hands-on use of mainstream LLM chat tools, including shared/reusable setups (system prompts, instructions, attached reference files).
- The distinction between a chat workflow, a deterministic automation, and an agent — and roughly what each costs to build and maintain.
- Agentic coding tools, and the idea that non-engineers can now use them to produce working software.
- The POC-to-production lifecycle: that a working demo is not a deployed solution.
- How enterprise IT, security, and central platform/AI teams act as gatekeepers for tools, access, and approvals.
- Basic ROI and KPI framing — cost of a problem, value of a fix, measurement.
- The term **FDE (forward deployed engineer)**, which has existed in the industry for years and is reused here with a specific meaning.
- Basic product/UX thinking: requirements, target users, specs, acceptance criteria.

## Main Points

### 1. The role exists everywhere, under many different names

- Across teams the speakers have worked with, the title varies: evangelist, leader, adoption support, ambassador. "Champions" (or some variant) is the most common, and is the term used throughout.
- Many companies have already formalised such programmes with varied degrees of success — but in the guest's view, almost always with a net-positive outcome.
- The material is not only for large enterprises. The host argues that once you pass basic/intermediate skill into the advanced category, you will be pushed into the champion role in some form — at minimum for friends and family, and very likely for any team you work with.
- Inhabiting the role exercises a different muscle than using AI: it requires communicating *how* to use it and thinking in process terms rather than only implementation terms.

### 2. Definition: the champion is "peer plus one"

- The AI champion is the person in a team or environment **responsible for moving AI adoption and value forward** — the person colleagues phone when they want to know how to get something done with AI.
- "Peer plus one": they sit inside the team and do the team's actual work, but are one step ahead on AI — either because they are the most motivated and capable user, or because they were appointed. Official appointment is not required; de facto counts.
- They demonstrate what AI can do **on the work their peers actually do** — not theoretical use cases, but ones from the team's day-to-day.
- They connect outward to other champions and to central AI teams to build and use what the company offers.
- They move people forward on adoption **without having authority over them**.

### 3. A spectrum of champion configurations

Described textually, the spectrum runs left to right by how much the champion is permitted to build:

```
Ambassador  ──────────  Ambassador-Builder  ──────────  Internal FDE
(best user,             (builds within a                (professional builder
 showcases,              defined scope; acts             embedded in a business
 trains, voices          as product manager              team; idea → production,
 team needs)             beyond that scope)              incl. planning and
                                                         value realisation)
```

- **Ambassadors** — the best AI users. They drive adoption, showcase real examples, do part of the work, train peers (officially or unofficially), and articulate what tools and internal builds the team needs.
- **Internal FDEs** — defined here simply as a professional builder who sits inside a business team and builds what that team needs, end to end. Some come from software/AI backgrounds; many the guest has seen were subject matter experts who made a genuine career pivot into building for their teams.
- **Ambassador-builders** — where most companies land. They build for themselves and their immediate team within a well-defined scope of what they can and cannot do; beyond that scope they behave like a product manager, gathering requirements and handing them to the right vendor or internal team.

### 4. The four questions companies are really asking

Whenever a company raises AI champions, four questions surface together — they are not champion-specific, but they always arrive with the topic:

- **Who gets to build?** The democratisation question — who consumes what others build, and who builds for others as well as themselves.
- **What do we expect from everyone else?** Champions have a job, and so does every other user; this raises expectation-setting for regular users versus champions.
- **How does AI spread from the field?** How people on the ground actually pick up what was built for them.
- **How do champions work with central teams and vendors?** The owners of the company's AI tools, plus IT, security, and other decision makers.

Even with no one holding a "central team" title, a company lives with these questions; answering them is what separates an opinionated organisation from one still asking.

### 5. Company side, stage one: define the goal and the scope — in writing

- The guest's opening question to companies is "what are you planning to accomplish by establishing a champion network?" — and very often they struggle to answer.
- The second, more productive question is **where on the ambassador-to-FDE line you want your champions to sit**: what they will be allowed to do, what they will be expected to do, and what stays with central teams or professional vendors.
- Goals, responsibilities, and expectations should be written down and agreed with everyone involved, including IT and security.
- This discussion should be revisited periodically, because companies refine their position as they gain experience with democratisation.
- Worked examples: one company concluded that what it primarily wanted was for champions to be a **funnel of real use cases** from the ground up to a central team blind to day-to-day team reality — a completely different role and training path. Others changed their minds after a while and chose to grow champions into full builders, effectively breeding AI engineers from the ground up.

### 6. Company side, stage two: nomination

Three primary signals of a good champion:

- They are among the most sophisticated AI users **without being told to use AI** — they just do.
- **Their peers already come to them** for questions and guidance.
- **Their manager will actually give them time** to work on AI for others — the point where good nominations most often fall short.

Secondary traits: ability to influence without authority, knowing the business ins and outs, initiative, and high curiosity (because everything changes constantly).

The guest also notes a retention angle: being an AI champion is a significant perk and makes a person highly desirable on the market, so nominate people you want to retain and promote.

Four nomination anti-patterns, called out as extremely common:

- **Senior people nominating themselves** to improve their own AI skills or gain influence over what gets built. Senior people belong in an AI committee — a dedicated steering function making strategic decisions — not in the role chartered to build for and train peers.
- **The too-busy star** — a strong candidate on every dimension who is also the busiest person on the team and has no bandwidth to build and train.
- **Nomination as a reward** — a strong performer who is not actually a good enough AI user or lacks the other traits.
- **The brand-new hire** — identifying use cases requires knowing the business ins and outs, so someone who started yesterday cannot do the job well.

### 7. Company side, stage three: training on their own work

- Training can be internal (if you have strong enough people), a concentrated bootcamp, or a gradual programme.
- Even great users should be trained on specific things — particularly the champion-side skills in the second half of the talk.
- Whatever the format, **train them on their own work**. The guest's favourite test: every champion takes one real use case from their team and shows how they built or advanced it by the end of training. If they can, they're ready; if not, there's more work to do.
- The host adds a broader observation: AI training doesn't fit prior corporate upskilling models, is more all-encompassing, and has no agreed standards or best practices yet, which makes investment sizing genuinely hard. Champions are the exception — training a small group that then disseminates the most contextually relevant parts to the business is an unusually high-ROI category, and a good starting point for companies that have done no formal AI training at all. The guest agrees: a relatively small number of people with large impact, versus training everyone and losing the signal in too many participants and variants.

### 8. Company side, stage four: the day after certification

- Training typically ends with a kickoff or certification and a lot of pride and excitement — and that is where the real test begins, and where many initiatives fail.
- **The biggest predictor of success is what happens the day after you certify someone.**
- A successful network needs **two sponsors**:
  - A **community sponsor** — from HR, the people team, or anyone who can run an internal community effectively. They keep the people side alive.
  - A **professional sponsor** — someone AI-savvy, working closely with the chief AI officer if one exists. They steer champions toward the company's AI adoption strategy, set priorities, bridge to the internal teams champions depend on, and keep the path clear (approvals, access, tools).
- Positive example: one of the most successful networks the guest has seen runs on a "dynamic duo" of an L&D expert and an AI expert who keep a hand on the pulse — where champions sit, what they need, what they're building, what the state of the industry is — and continually bridge the gap.
- Negative pattern: in **every** company where an excellent champion course never materialised into business value, the missing piece was continuation. No one owned making sure champions were used effectively and kept learning, so most reverted to working the way they always had.

### 9. Company side, stage five: run the network as a living entity

- Standard community mechanics apply: routines, regular meetings, easy sharing of what people are doing, learning together, and celebration of accomplishments that matter to the company.
- **Underrated: work with the champions' managers.** A short session explaining what the champion knows, what they're expected to do, and how to leverage them prevents unrealistic expectations, knowledge gaps between champion and manager, and mutual frustration.
- Not everyone nominated will become a champion — expectations diverge, people leave, work gets too busy, and some discover the role simply isn't for them (the guest has seen this many times). So the network must be a living entity: easy to leave and hand over to someone else on the team, easy and clear for new people to join, with education that never stops.
- **Reviving a tired network** — start here: name your two sponsors; verify every champion still has roughly one to two days or otherwise sufficient capacity; re-establish the commitment with their managers if needed; let people who can't do the work step out, thank them, and keep nominating new people. The most tired networks are missing one of these elements.

### 10. Champion side: know the whole road from idea to production

This section applies equally to power users who are not formally champions — it is what separates a regular power user from one geared toward business value. Whether a pure ambassador or a full builder, a champion must understand the entire path a use case travels, in three stretches:

- **Before the build** — know your ground: what the company permits, and where the use cases live. Decide **if**, **what**, and **how** to build.
- **The build** — POC to production: creating the agent, automation, or capability. The proportion of this a champion personally does depends on their skills, their permitted scope, and their tools.
- **After the build** — value realisation, deployment, and retiring a solution when needed.

Four skills to do this well:

- **Use case identification** — find problems in your domain worth solving, where the cost justifies the investment. Be conservative: assume the investment will be higher than anticipated and the value lower on day one than hoped. Articulate who suffers from the problem, how often, and what "done" looks like. Then check your idea against what already exists, so the company builds a thing once rather than a hundred times.
- **ROI** — articulate what it's worth versus what it costs; be the realistic person. Ask how it will be used, whether it moves a critical company performance indicator, and what it must do to be measurable against its goal.
- **Deployment** — get the built thing into the right hands, frequently enough, in the right usage mode. Where does it live, who approves it, and who supports it on day two, not just day one when everyone is excited.
- **Evaluation** — how do you know what you built is good? How do you know when you can and cannot trust it? What are the risks of its mistakes? The guest calls this one of the most important champion skills: being able to sign off on whether the thing is good enough to put in users' hands.

### 11. Worked example: the same use case, three champion types

A team spends hours every week assembling a summary of a customer renewal.

- **Ambassador** — spots the use case, describes it clearly, and makes the case ("this is what it costs us today, this is why I think it's solvable"), then hands it to a central team or vendor as a clean request a professional can execute. Once the solution ships, they ensure value is realised and the team uses it properly. Involved at the beginning and the end.
- **Ambassador-builder** — additionally builds the version that works for their own team, within the company's permitted scope. A second team may then take it over to fully productise it; the champion returns for deployment.
- **Internal FDE** — can take it all the way, including the build.

### 12. The builder's / champion's checklist: three questions

**Question one: should we build this at all?** People are often surprised how rarely this is asked before building starts.

- Check how often the need comes up (is it a concrete use case?), how much it's worth, and how costly a mistake would be.
- If value and error tolerance justify action, the decision is then between **reuse** something existing, **buy**, **build**, or **add to the backlog** for a future state.
- There is a huge bias toward building, in individuals and companies alike; a good champion decides from a grounded, company-connected point of view, not as a passion project.

**Question two: what should it be?** Keep it simple — build the simplest solution that solves the problem. A good champion holds a mental model of the right technology for the job. Described as a complexity ladder, with justification required for each step rightward:

```
Shared chat setup  →  Skill  →  Automation  →  Full agent
(prompt +             (playbook   (same steps    (built for the
 instructions +        run on      every time)    team, broadly
 files the team        demand)                    connected)
 reuses)
```

Start at the left and move right only when there is true justification — otherwise you end up with something very hard to build, maintain, and test.

**Question three: define the core properties before building.** These are written down as requirements or a spec; each carries real weight:

- **Who is it for?** "Everyone" is not an answer — designing for everybody is designing for nobody.
- **What must it know?** This is often where you discover an entirely separate project: curating the relevant knowledge before you can even start on the original idea.
- **How will people use it?** So it is actually used, and not an application nobody remembers the URL for.
- **What must it never do?** Concrete, detailed guardrails. Starting from what the solution must never do often produces more clarity about what it should do.
- **Who checks the output?** Where human judgment is enforced in the loop, and how business value is balanced against quality and safety.
- **How do you know it works?** Define the cases it must be tested against as early as possible. Doing so surfaces critical conversations about edge cases and about how hard evaluation will be — and it can also kill a project before it's built, because quality cannot be assured, which is often the right outcome.

This spec is what the champion hands over — to a vendor, to the central team, or to themselves.

### 13. Closing: playbooks are durable, tools are transient

- Unlike much of AI, these playbooks are not transient. Tools change every quarter, and a good champion network does teach the latest tools and building techniques — but the guest deliberately spotlighted what stays valid as the technology races forward: you still need the right people with the time to do the work; you still need to decide and write down where they sit between ambassador and builder; they still need to know the whole road from idea to production; and someone still needs to own the day after.
- For the individual, the champion seat is one of the best in the company: you see problems across the team, you work with teams or build yourself, and you learn the beginning and the end of the build road, where most people only know the middle.
- The speakers are launching an **AI Champions Bootcamp**, opinionated toward this view of the role, open to prospective champions, nominated champions, and sponsors sending cohorts.

### 14. Closing thread: the non-technical FDE and agent containment

- The host raises a variant of the internal FDE that has been lurking all year and is becoming more urgent: someone not technical by definition who has figured out how to use code and agentic coding tools to do parts of their job.
- This is the best current example of AI's power and challenge at once. The power is obvious; the challenges are knowing what to use it for and how to think in those terms, plus security and guardrails.
- Critically, the guardrails problem is not only people doing it wrong: **agents are now powerful and tenacious enough that they tend to escape containment quickly** and work their way into many different systems.
- This makes the champion role more mission-critical, not less — it isn't merely an under-optimised opportunity; roles are changing fundamentally and generating new challenges that have to be solved to move forward.
- The guest agrees, and argues the response is enablement and infrastructure rather than shutting less professional builders down — and training *more* subject matter experts, because they will work on the right use cases. She considers this harder than mastering the technology itself.
- On the training split between AI skills and the discipline of transmitting them internally: both are needed, but the building part is the easier half and is not unique knowledge — people learn it by trial and error. The differentiator is what nobody stumbles upon accidentally, evaluation being the example. The bootcamp emphasises what cannot be picked up from a YouTube video or by playing with a tool powerful enough to tell you what to do or do it for you: the in-between business/technology/people layer, where many of the playbooks are still being written and which most companies cannot train their own people on.

## Key Concepts

- **AI champion** — the person in a team responsible for moving AI adoption and value forward; the one peers call for AI help.
- **Peer plus one** — the guest's framing for a champion: inside the team doing the team's work, but one step ahead on AI.
- **Ambassador** — the lighter-weight end of the spectrum: best user, showcases real use cases, trains peers, voices team needs; does not build at scale.
- **Ambassador-builder** — builds within a defined permitted scope and acts as a product manager beyond it; where most companies land.
- **Internal FDE (forward deployed engineer)** — here, a professional builder embedded in a business team who builds what the team needs from idea to production; often a subject matter expert who pivoted into building.
- **AI committee / steering function** — where senior leaders belong: strategic AI decisions, as distinct from the champion's build-and-train charter.
- **Community sponsor** — an HR/people/L&D owner who keeps the champion community's people side alive.
- **Professional sponsor** — an AI-savvy owner, close to the chief AI officer, who sets champion priorities and bridges to IT, security, and platform teams.
- **The day after** — the post-certification period whose ownership is the single biggest predictor of whether a champion programme produces business value.
- **Influence without authority** — moving peers' adoption forward with no reporting line over them.
- **Use case funnel** — one possible charter for champions: surfacing real ground-level use cases to a central team that lacks day-to-day visibility.
- **Value realisation** — the post-build work of ensuring a shipped solution is actually used and delivers the claimed value.
- **Evaluation / sign-off** — deciding whether a built thing is good enough, trustworthy enough, and low-risk enough to put in users' hands.
- **Reuse / buy / build / backlog** — the four outcomes of the "should we build this at all?" decision.
- **Simplest-solution-first ladder** — shared chat setup → skill → automation → full agent, moving right only with justification.
- **Skill** — a reusable, on-demand playbook; one rung on that ladder.
- **Must-never-do guardrails** — concrete prohibitions written into the spec; defining them clarifies what the solution should do.
- **Core properties spec** — the handover artifact: who it's for, what it must know, how it will be used, what it must never do, who checks output, how you know it works.
- **Escaping containment** — the tendency of capable, tenacious agents to spread into systems beyond their intended boundary.
- **AI Champions Bootcamp** — the speakers' training programme built around these playbooks.

## Summary

The speakers argue that the highest-ROI AI transformation activity they have observed across enterprises is building a properly structured internal network of AI champions — "peer plus one" practitioners who sit inside a business team, demonstrate AI on the work their colleagues actually do, and are accountable for moving both adoption and realised value forward. Getting this right has a company side and a champion side. The company must write down what the network is for and where its champions sit on the spectrum from ambassador to internal forward deployed engineer; nominate on genuine signals (unprompted sophistication, peers already asking, and a manager who will actually free up their time) while avoiding the common traps of self-nominating executives, the too-busy star, reward nominations, and brand-new hires; train them on one real use case from their own team; and then — the decisive step most programmes skip — own the day after, with both a community sponsor and a professional sponsor, manager briefings, and a network treated as a living entity people can join and leave. The champion, in turn, must know the whole road from idea to production rather than only the middle: identifying use cases worth the cost, arguing honest ROI, deploying so the thing is actually used and supported on day two, and above all evaluating whether what was built is good enough to trust. Three questions mark the line between a power user and a champion — should this be built at all, what is the simplest form that solves it, and what are its core properties before a line of work begins. The closing message is that while tools will churn every quarter, these playbooks will not; and with non-technical colleagues now building with agentic coding tools that readily escape their intended boundaries, the champion's combined business-technology-people judgment becomes more mission-critical, not less.
