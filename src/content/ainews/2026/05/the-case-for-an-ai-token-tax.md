## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, a daily podcast about AI news, looks at the growing debate over an **AI token tax**. The idea is to charge a fee on AI model usage, measured in tokens, to fund public programmes as AI takes over work that humans used to do. The host does not give his name in the transcript. He recorded the episode in advance while travelling, so it covers only the main topic and skips the usual headlines segment.

Two items prompted the episode. Senator Elizabeth Warren published an op-ed in *Time* titled "Why We Need to Tax AI". Michigan U.S. Senate candidate Mallory McMorrow released a worker-protection policy that includes a token tax. The host sets out a first-principles case for the tax, then the main arguments against it, and ends with his own position. His position is that the industry should engage openly with new policy ideas instead of rejecting them out of hand.

**Source:** The transcript does not include a video URL. The show is at aidailybrief.ai and patreon.com/aidailybrief.

## Prerequisites

- **Tokens:** the units of text that large language models process and that providers use to price inference.
- **Inference pricing:** how AI providers charge for API usage, usually per million input and output tokens.
- **Basic public finance:** payroll taxes, income taxes, excise taxes, consumption taxes (VAT and sales tax), and capital gains taxation.
- **Labor substitution:** the idea that AI systems or agents may do tasks that human workers currently do.
- **Tax base:** the economic activity a tax applies to, such as wages, consumption or capital.
- **Intermediate vs. final goods:** inputs used to produce other goods, compared with goods and services that end consumers use.

## Main Points

### 1. Why the industry should engage with new policy ideas
- The host argues that if AI is starting a categorically different economic period, policies that worked for decades may no longer fit.
- He says the AI industry should engage openly with new proposals rather than dismiss them. Engaging does not mean ending up in favour of them.
- He says it is usually not hard to tell good-faith proposals from bad-faith ones.

### 2. Recent political proposals
- **Earlier coverage:** *El País* published "If AI Replaces Workers, Should It Also Pay Taxes?" in November of the previous year, so the idea is not new.
- **Mallory McMorrow (U.S. Senate candidate, Michigan):** her plan includes:
  - an AI Workforce Reinvestment Fund, paid for by companies that automate jobs away, to support apprenticeships and retraining
  - a "modest fee" on commercial AI usage. She argues that "a fraction of a cent charge per token" across billions of queries a day becomes a sustainable funding stream "without raising taxes on a single American worker."
- **Elizabeth Warren (*Time* op-ed):**
  - Revenue from taxing AI could fund universal health care, free education, apprenticeships, a jobs guarantee and stronger unemployment insurance for displaced workers.
  - She proposes "going to the source," starting with an **excise tax on the energy data centers use**. The tax would scale with size: "the bigger the data center, the more they pay."
  - Her justification is that AI was trained on human creativity, funded in part by federal research and runs on American land and the shared electric grid.
- The host says he rarely agrees with Warren on innovation policy. Between the Sanders/AOC data center moratorium and Warren's "cut everyone in on the benefits" approach, he prefers Warren's.

### 3. Proposals from tech and business figures
- **Mark Cuban:** proposes a federal tax at the provider level of less than $0.50 per million tokens. He claims it would:
  1. push providers to optimise tokenization, caching, routing and localization
  2. reduce energy use, saving providers more than the tax costs them
  3. raise about $10B a year at first, growing 30–100x over ten years
  4. fund federal debt reduction or responses to unexpected harms from AI.
  He accepts that providers will pass the cost on to customers, who can switch providers or run open-source models locally.
- **Reactions to Cuban:**
  - Steven Sinofsky: "Imagine a bit tax in 1995."
  - Palmer Luckey: providers already have strong economic reasons to optimise. A token tax would make foreign models more attractive and would create infrastructure for the government to track all AI usage.
  - Ryan Petersen (Flexport): mocked the claim that the tax would save AI companies money.
- **Gabriel Weinberg (DuckDuckGo):**
  - Proposes collecting the tax now and holding it in a statutory lockbox, outside general appropriations, for displaced workers in the future.
  - Suggests a 10% surcharge on token charges, roughly matching the share employers pay in payroll taxes. This would also reduce the incentive to replace human workers with AI.
  - Says DuckDuckGo would support such bills and pay the tax.
- **Dario Amodei (Anthropic):** in an interview with Axios, floated a tax of about 3% of AI revenue, to be redistributed. He said it was "not in my economic interest" but "a reasonable solution." Axios noted it could raise trillions if AI advances as he expects.

### 4. The first-principles case for a token tax
- **The tax base is eroding:** across the OECD, taxes on the average single worker were **35.1% of labor costs in 2025**. When a human does a task, the resulting income is taxed through income and payroll taxes. When an AI agent does it, the value appears as lower costs, higher margins or capital gains, which are harder to tax and usually taxed less.
- **IMF precedent:** in 2024 the IMF warned that replacing labor could erode the income tax base if capital income is taxed at a lower rate than labor income.
- **The core principle:** the tax base should follow where productive capacity sits. If AI agents become a major class of worker, some public revenue should come from AI work.
- **Why tokens in particular:** providers already meter tokens, so they are one of the easiest units of AI labor to observe. A usage-based surcharge on inference would be simple to apply. Tokens are an imperfect proxy, but so are hours worked. Tax bases are chosen because they can be administered.
- **Tax neutrality example:**
  - Option A: hire a person for $100,000. This brings payroll taxes, income tax withholding, unemployment insurance, workers' comp and compliance costs.
  - Option B: buy $100,000 of AI inference. This carries no labor-tax equivalent.
  - The result is *tax-incentivized* automation rather than automation driven by the market. A token tax, perhaps paired with lower payroll taxes, would remove an artificial fiscal preference for agents that are not taxed over humans that are.
- **Philosophical argument:** if only human labor is taxed, the tax falls on exactly the things society may want to protect: human participation, employment, earned income and bargaining power. A token tax ties the duty to fund society to productive capacity rather than to "human toil."

### 5. The case against a token tax (David Friedman's critique)
- **Tokens poorly measure economic value:** a million tokens could generate spam, summarise a novel, coordinate a supply chain or produce high-value legal analysis. Value per token varies by orders of magnitude, and many tokens are not used for work at all.
- **Different tokenizers count differently** (the "tokenizer endogeneity problem"):
  - Compared with English, Mandarin uses about 2–3x more tokens, source code about 1.5–2x more, and some low-resource languages 10–15x more.
  - A flat per-token tax therefore discriminates between providers and users for reasons unrelated to the harm being taxed.
  - The providers who design the tokenizers are also the ones paying the tax.
- **Prices are falling:** per-token prices have dropped about 200x a year for two years.
  - A fixed tax of $0.50 per million tokens that equals 5% of a frontier price in year one would equal 1,000% of that price by year three.
  - Congress would have to either index the tax downward, which collapses revenue, or leave it fixed, which makes it confiscatory and leads providers to work around it. Friedman argues there is no stable equilibrium that delivers 30–100x revenue growth.
- **Providers can sit anywhere:** a U.S. tax on U.S. providers effectively subsidises foreign inference. Enterprises would route requests through API providers based abroad, hosting platforms in untaxed jurisdictions, or aggregation layers that hide the underlying provider.

### 6. Academic view: Brookings, "Public Finance in the Age of AI" (January 2026)
- AI could erode the two main tax bases of modern tax systems: **labor income** and **human consumption**.
- The paper splits the AI transition into two stages, each needing very different tax instruments:
  - **Stage 1** (labor is displaced but humans still consume): tax at the point of final consumption, through VAT and sales tax systems. Business-to-business use is exempt to avoid cascading taxes. A token tax is acceptable here only in that form.
  - **Stage 2** (an "AGI economy," where AGI is the autonomous producer and consumer): deeper capital taxation of AGI entities.
- **The key distinction is intermediate vs. final use.** Taxing tokens used in business, research, manufacturing or agentic workflows becomes a tax on intermediate production, which distorts investment and adoption.

### 7. The host's own objection: a bias toward known ROI
- Demand for tokens already exceeds supply, which is limited by compute and energy, so token prices are rising. Companies are restricting usage and favouring use cases with clear ROI.
- That favours "efficiency AI": cheaper customer support, faster decks, faster sales emails. These are productivity gains inside existing business models.
- The host argues that most of AI's value will come from *new opportunities*. Another disincentive to experimentation would make it harder for firms to discover the most valuable uses.
- **Incumbents would gain:** large firms can negotiate discounts, reserve capacity, self-host models and spread the cost of experimentation across large budgets. A flat token tax would therefore weigh more heavily on smaller players.

### 8. The host's position
- He accepts that the burden of production may shift from human labor to agentic labor, and that this could require structural changes to the tax base.
- He thinks fears of large-scale labor substitution are "wildly overblown." People will keep working in new kinds of jobs, so wages will remain largely taxable.
- He supports making sure the technology benefits everyone. He favours a realpolitik "buy them off" approach: data center builders and their users could provide tangible local benefits such as jobs, subsidised electricity and utility costs, or public infrastructure.
- He concludes that if the changes are as large as many expect, society must be willing to have "weird, different, uncomfortable conversations." He thinks better options than a token tax exist, but welcomes the debate.

## Key Concepts

- **AI token tax:** a fee charged per token, or as a percentage of token spend, on commercial AI model usage, intended to fund public programmes.
- **Token:** the unit of text that language models process and that providers use to meter and price inference.
- **Data center energy excise tax:** Warren's proposal to tax the electricity data centers consume, so that larger facilities pay more.
- **AI Workforce Reinvestment Fund:** McMorrow's proposed pooled fund, paid for by companies that automate jobs, to finance apprenticeships and retraining.
- **Displaced Worker Fund / lockbox:** Weinberg's proposal to collect token tax revenue now and protect it by statute for future support of displaced workers.
- **Tax base erosion:** shrinking tax revenue as economic value moves from heavily taxed labor income to more lightly taxed capital income.
- **Tax neutrality (human vs. AI labor):** the principle that the tax system should not favour automation over human workers or the reverse.
- **Tax-incentivized automation:** automation driven by the difference in tax treatment between human and AI labor rather than by real efficiency.
- **Tokenizer endogeneity problem:** the fact that different tokenizers produce different token counts for the same content, by language and type, so a flat per-token tax falls unevenly.
- **Intermediate vs. final use:** the distinction between AI used as an input to production and AI consumed by end users. Taxing intermediate use distorts production.
- **Consumption tax (VAT/sales tax) on AI:** Brookings' preferred Stage 1 tool, which taxes AI at the point of final human consumption and exempts business-to-business use.
- **AGI economy:** Brookings' Stage 2 scenario, in which AGI systems act as autonomous producers and consumers, calling for capital taxation of AGI entities.
- **Efficiency AI:** AI deployments that make existing processes cheaper or faster, as opposed to AI that unlocks new opportunities.
- **Known ROI bias:** the tendency, made worse by higher token costs, for firms to fund only AI use cases with predictable returns, which reduces experimentation.

## Summary

The episode argues that the AI token tax, once a fringe idea, is entering mainstream politics through Elizabeth Warren and Mallory McMorrow. It also has surprising support from figures such as Mark Cuban, Gabriel Weinberg and Dario Amodei. The strongest case for the tax rests on fiscal neutrality. Today's tax systems rely heavily on labor income, so shifting work to AI agents both erodes revenue and quietly subsidises automation over human employment. Tokens are an easy, already-metered unit to tax. Critics, including David Friedman and the Brookings working paper, respond that tokens poorly measure value, vary across tokenizers, are falling sharply in price and can be routed to other countries. They also argue that taxing intermediate production distorts investment, and that any AI taxation belongs at the point of final consumption. The host adds that a flat token tax would deepen a bias toward safe, known-ROI "efficiency AI," discourage the experimentation that produces AI's biggest gains, and favour incumbents. He doubts that large-scale labor substitution will happen and prefers giving communities direct, tangible benefits. Even so, he accepts that a major technological shift may require rethinking the tax base, and he urges open, good-faith engagement with unconventional proposals.
