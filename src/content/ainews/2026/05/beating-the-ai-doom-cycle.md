## Overview

The AI Daily Brief is a daily podcast and video about AI news. In this episode the host introduces a framework he calls the **AI doom cycle**. It is modelled on Gartner's technology hype cycle. Gartner's chart describes how a technology spreads through society. The doom cycle instead describes the emotional and mental states people pass through in their relationship with AI. The host argues that most people are stuck somewhere between skepticism, "AI psychosis" and "doom desperation". He says the healthiest place to discuss AI policy and other big questions is the far end of the cycle, which he calls "enlightened anxietment" (also "enlightened excitement"). This episode was one long segment rather than the usual split between headlines and a main story. He used the weekend's news stories as examples of each stage.

## Prerequisites

- Familiarity with **Gartner's Hype Cycle**: innovation trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment, plateau of productivity.
- Basic knowledge of today's AI products and companies: ChatGPT, Claude / Claude Code, Codex, GitHub Copilot, OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek.
- What "tokens" are and how AI subscriptions are priced (flat-rate plans vs. paying per use).
- General awareness of the debate about AI's effect on jobs and of ideas such as Universal Basic Income (UBI).

## Main Points

### 1. The Gartner Hype Cycle as inspiration
- New technologies are said to move from an innovation trigger, up to a peak of inflated expectations (often driven by venture capital), and down into a trough of disillusionment.
- The trough often comes not because the technology is bad, but because the timeline turns out to be much longer than people expected.
- Some technologies never leave the trough (the host names the metaverse). Those that do climb a slope of enlightenment and reach a plateau of productivity.
- Research suggests most technologies do not follow the curve exactly. The host says it still resonates because people tend to badly underestimate how long an impactful technology takes to make its impact.

### 2. The five stages of the AI doom cycle
- The stages are: (1) skepticism and disbelief, (2) AI psychosis, (3) doom desperation, (4) real-world recalibration, (5) enlightened excitement / "anxietment".
- "Anxietment" combines anxiety and excitement. The host credits GPT-4 with suggesting the word to describe the mix of thrill and caution.
- The cycle is about people's mental states, not about the technology itself.

### 3. Skepticism and disbelief (a fading stage)
- Much early skepticism came from people who never used AI, or who only tried limited free versions and never came back.
- The DeepSeek moment in early 2025 is his example. The app gave many people their first free reasoning model (R1), which felt clearly better than what they had used before. OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) had been hard to access and sat behind a paywall.
- Skepticism that remains today usually comes from beliefs that were never updated, or from people whose business model depends on AI skepticism.

### 4. AI psychosis: the "AI can do everything" peak
- The host says he does not mean the term as an insult. He uses it for the stage where someone is fully convinced AI will change everything and may overestimate what it can do in the short term.
- Case study: Citadel CEO Ken Griffin. In January, at the World Economic Forum, he called AI hype meant to justify infrastructure spending and said AI-generated research reports were "all garbage."
- At Stanford Business School he reversed his view: "For the first time, AI is real." He said AI agents now do in hours or days work that people with master's degrees and PhDs used to do over weeks or months, and that Citadel sees a 15–25% productivity gain.
- Griffin said this left him "fairly depressed," which shows how a stronger belief in AI's power can push someone toward the next stage.

### 5. Doom desperation
- People in this stage project current changes in the workforce forward into a future with far fewer jobs. Andrew Yang commented that "finance will quickly follow tech."
- Doom-heavy comments from AI executives resurfaced in the press:
  - Fortune ran a headline saying Mustafa Suleyman gives "18 months for all white-collar work to be automated."
  - The Wall Street Journal resurfaced Dario Amodei's forecasts of 10% overall unemployment and 50% unemployment in entry-level white-collar jobs.
- Some people are skeptical of these motives. One X post argued that this story is what lets Anthropic raise money at very high valuations.
- Deedy Das of Menlo Ventures wrote a post, seen by about 11 million people, describing a frantic mood in San Francisco:
  - About 10,000 people have become very wealthy while everyone else feels shut out.
  - Layoffs are underway, and software engineers feel their skills have lost value.
  - The corporate ladder now looks like "the wrong building to climb."
  - Many people talk about a "permanent underclass."
  - Middle managers feel paralyzed.
  - Even newly rich people say they have lost their sense of purpose.
- A critic, Bucco Capital, pushed back, urging readers never to live their lives that way.
- The host notes that much of this doom comes from the people building AI, which fuels outside anger: "if this thing is going to be bad for us, why are you building it?"
- Commencement booing as evidence:
  - Graduates at the University of Arizona booed Eric Schmidt when he said AI will shape the world.
  - Graduates at the University of Central Florida booed Gloria Caulfield (Tavistock Development Company) when she called AI the next industrial revolution.
  - Derek Thompson said it is very unusual for the builders of a technology to promise it will destroy livelihoods. Trevor Garcia said graduates are not anti-technology but "anti being told to be excited about something that feels like it was built for everyone except them."

### 6. Real-world recalibration
- This stage means stepping away from the loudest and most extreme voices to look at what is actually happening. Sometimes that confirms the concerns.
- **What confirms the concerns:**
  - Meta is laying off about 10% of staff (around 8,000 people). The stated reason is efficiency and offsetting other investments, presumably shifting payroll into infrastructure.
  - Wired reports morale at new lows, made worse by screen-tracking software reportedly used for AI training.
- **Token maxing meets real-world limits:**
  - Companies have run token-use leaderboards. Goodhart's Law applies: people game the metric, for example at Amazon and Meta.
  - Real-world limits are now forcing the AI labs to change their business models:
    - Anthropic moved enterprise seats from the $200 flat-rate plan to a $20 per seat fee with all usage billed.
    - GitHub moved to token-based billing.
    - Claude now bills usage outside Anthropic's own harness per token.
- **A structural compute shortage:**
  - Token demand now exceeds supply, with shortages in electricity, memory and chips. In the short term the only fix is raising token prices so supply goes to whoever will pay most.
  - People report that their employers' AI budgets, previously unlimited, now have caps.
  - Copilot users posted estimates showing large hidden subsidies: $451 → $11,432; $39 → $5,851; $54 → about $1,200 (about 22×).
  - Axios: "AI can cost more than human workers now."
- **The consequence:** companies return to an ROI mindset, which slows how fast automation can realistically replace people.

### 7. Enlightened excitement / "anxietment"
- The host says this is not blind optimism. It means being specific and clear about what is happening, what will happen next and what to do about it.
- It makes room for more nuanced discussion:
  - Weak hiring in software also reflects a correction from hiring during the post-COVID zero-interest-rate period.
  - Jobs AI creates are already visible, such as data-center construction, which matters a lot if the build-out lasts a generation.
  - Blue-collar unions are acting as a bridge between big tech and local communities.
- **Economics beyond headlines:** Alex Imas's essay *What Will Be Scarce* argues that the "relational sector" will grow as automation saves money elsewhere. That is the part of the economy where it matters that a human made the product or gave the service. Ezra Klein discussed the essay in a New York Times piece arguing the AI job apocalypse probably won't happen.
- **The labs' consulting push:**
  - OpenAI and Anthropic are launching large consulting efforts, including partnerships with Accenture, Deloitte and PwC to train and certify 30,000 PwC professionals on Claude.
  - This shows that AI capability runs into institutional and human inertia when companies try to adopt it.
  - Inside the labs, the long-running debate between research and enterprise focus has been settled by real-world limits.
- **Other ways to read the booing:**
  - Joanna Stern calls the pushback "encouraging" because it shows debate is happening.
  - Jensen Huang's Carnegie Mellon commencement speech was described as "anxiety-relieving." He argued AI doom is not destiny and pointed to concrete jobs in re-industrialization.
- **Industry messaging is shifting:**
  - Since early May, Sam Altman has said the goal is "tools to augment and elevate people, not entities to replace them."
  - He spoke of helping developers "Pokemon evolve into superheroes."
  - He described starting Codex tasks, playing with his child, and coming back to find the work done.

### 8. Better policy conversations
- If AI inevitably takes all jobs, policy shrinks to "UBI or die." If disruption is specific and gradual, there is room for more targeted, better policy.
- Example from Matthew Yglesias: allow data centers only if 20% of the GPUs are set aside as affordable compute for low-income users.
- Example from Mark Cuban, a federal token tax at the provider level (under $0.50 per million tokens). He argued it would:
  - push labs to make tokenization, caching and routing more efficient;
  - reduce energy use;
  - raise about $10B a year, possibly growing 30–100× over a decade;
  - fund debt reduction or responses to AI's side effects.
- The host does not endorse either proposal. He says these are more useful conversations than talk of a "permanent underclass."

## Key Concepts

- **Gartner Hype Cycle**: a model of how technologies move from hype through disillusionment to productive use.
- **AI doom cycle**: the host's five-stage model of how people's emotional and mental relationship with AI develops.
- **Skepticism and disbelief**: dismissing AI, usually because of outdated or very limited experience with it.
- **AI psychosis** (used without negative intent here): being at peak conviction that AI will change everything, possibly overestimating its short-term abilities.
- **Doom desperation**: projecting current AI disruption into widespread job loss and hopelessness.
- **Real-world recalibration**: testing stories about AI against real evidence, whether that evidence confirms or contradicts them.
- **Enlightened anxietment / excitement**: a balanced state of cautious enthusiasm grounded in specific, evidence-based expectations.
- **Anxietment**: a blend of anxiety and excitement, a word the host credits to GPT-4.
- **Token maxing**: encouraging employees to use as many AI tokens as possible, for example through leaderboards, to drive experimentation.
- **Goodhart's Law**: once a measure becomes a target, people game it and it stops being a good measure.
- **Usage-based (token-based) pricing**: billing by the tokens actually used instead of a flat subscription.
- **Structural compute shortage**: a lasting gap between token demand and the electricity, memory and chips needed to supply it.
- **Relational sector**: Alex Imas's term for economic activity where human origin or human service is part of the value.
- **Permanent underclass**: a fear, common among young people, of being permanently locked out of wealth created by AI.
- **Token tax**: Mark Cuban's proposed small federal tax on tokens, charged at the provider level.

## Summary

The host argues that public reaction to AI follows a predictable emotional arc, similar to Gartner's hype cycle. It starts with disbelief, rises to a conviction that AI will change everything, and then falls into doom desperation. Much of that doom is fueled by AI builders themselves, and it is reflected in executives' job-loss forecasts, Silicon Valley's malaise and graduates booing AI at commencements. He says the way out is real-world recalibration: looking at actual evidence. Some of it confirms the fears, such as layoffs at Meta. Much of it complicates them: a structural compute shortage that is ending subsidized token use, AI that can cost more than human workers, and labs forced into consulting because companies adopt AI slowly. These recalibrations lead to "enlightened anxietment," a state that is neither naive nor fatalistic and allows specific, nuanced debate. Examples include economic analysis such as Imas's essay, hopeful messaging such as Jensen Huang's and Sam Altman's, and concrete policy ideas such as compute set-asides or a token tax. His closing message is that most people are still stuck in the first half of the cycle, and the faster they move to recalibration and enlightened anxietment, the better off everyone will be.
