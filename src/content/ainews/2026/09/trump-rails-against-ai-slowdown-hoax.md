## Overview

This episode of **The AI Daily Brief**, a daily podcast about AI news, covers the political fallout from Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's "Pacing the Frontier" essay and the public agreement from the other frontier lab leaders. The host had argued in the previous episode that the essay opened a negotiation over who decides how fast new AI reaches the market. In this episode, that debate turns partisan. President Trump publishes seven Truth Social posts calling AI existential risk a "hoax". JD Vance and the Department of War back him up. NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang rejects doom predictions at the All-In Summit but supports some safety measures. Former President Obama and Kamala Harris come out in favour of a slowdown. The episode opens with headlines: a mathematicians' open letter led by Terence Tao, a Census Bureau study of college graduate employment, and new recursive self-improvement efforts from Chinese AI groups.

## Prerequisites

- **Frontier AI labs:** Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and others building the most capable models, and the leaders who speak for them (Dario Amodei, for example).
- **The AI safety debate:** The split between "doomers", who stress catastrophic or existential risk; "accelerationists", who favour building as fast as possible; and positions in between. Some familiarity with effective altruism (EA) helps, since it is often linked to the safety movement.
- **"Pacing the Frontier":** Amodei's proposal, published the weekend before, to slow frontier AI development. It includes independent auditors embedded inside the labs.
- **Millennium Prize Problems:** Six long-unsolved mathematics problems, each with a $1M prize. Solving one is the top achievement in the field.
- **Recursive self-improvement (RSI):** AI systems improving their own training process. It is a central concern in AI safety.
- **US political context:** The coming midterm elections and Trump's habit of announcing positions on Truth Social.

## Main Points

### 1. Mathematicians' open letter: "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics"
- Background: OpenAI reportedly solved one Millennium Prize problem and claimed major progress on a second, and Anthropic was rumoured to have solved another. Three of the six remaining problems could fall this year. NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster asked whether OpenAI had used his AI tool logs to beat him to the prize. OpenAI denied it.
- Fields medalist Terence Tao and 25 other Fields medalists signed a letter arguing that AI companies using famous problems as benchmarks harms mathematics. In their view, the goals of the AI companies and of the mathematical community are "severely misaligned".
- Their main argument is that famous problems act as "landmarks and lighthouses". A solution matters because the community then spends years studying, simplifying and spreading the new methods. "Solving problems is only a tool and proxy for … conceptual understanding and insight." Mass-producing true/false results "could destroy fertile ground".
- The letter treats mathematics as an early example of a wider threat to intellectual work. Years of training build understanding, and that purpose is lost when AI produces the answer directly. The letter also accepts that AI is here to stay and is very useful. Tao himself came to AI-assisted mathematics slowly.
- Reactions:
  - Eric Weinstein rejected the idea of a "mathematical community" speaking for everyone and called it a "priesthood".
  - Nassim Taleb said the change might be bad for current mathematicians but not for mathematics itself.
  - Steven Strogatz described getting choked up during an interview. He said this was not about his job but grief at a sudden break in a 4,000-year tradition. Change always involves loss, even when it is inevitable and partly good.

### 2. Census Bureau study: college graduates and AI exposure
- The study covered hiring and salary data for graduates from 2016 to 2024, about 29% of bachelor's degrees in that period. Graduates were grouped by how exposed their field is to AI.
- From the release of ChatGPT in November 2022, graduates entering highly exposed fields saw a **5 percentage point drop in employment** and a **13% fall in earnings**. Part of the fall came from taking lower-paid jobs outside their field.
- The study says this is comparable to the earnings losses from graduating into a large recession.
- The host warns that correlation is not causation:
  - Companies adopted AI slowly.
  - Tech layoffs since 2022 may reflect over-hiring during the pandemic.
  - Other studies link similar effects to remote work, which may hurt junior employees who have not built relationships with colleagues and managers.
- Even so, graduates clearly face a very hard job market, and the topic deserves continued study.

### 3. China and recursive self-improvement
- The Chinese open-source model company ZAI plans to raise $5 billion through new shares and convertible bonds. 60% will go to next-generation training, including a "fully self-training loop". The host says this is the first time a Chinese lab has openly stated it will build RSI.
- 33 ByteDance-backed researchers published "The Last AI Built by Humans: Towards Genuine Recursive Self-Improvement". It sets out a theoretical roadmap but concludes that AI can automate parts of training without yet improving the training process itself.
- The host's conclusion: if RSI is the main safety concern, any slowdown will need coordination with China.

### 4. A more nuanced response before the politics took over
- The host repeats his earlier view that Amodei's essay, together with agreement from the other lab leaders, marked a turning point. The labs would no longer be the only ones deciding how fast AI comes to market, and the essay opened a negotiation over who holds that power.
- Martin Casado of a16z, at first hostile to the essay, posted a more measured take. He saw the labs as trying to get ahead of "unsophisticated federal regulation" while dealing with a "well-financed doomer complex".
- Casado said three things should be kept apart: (1) applauding the labs' attempt to find a governance approach, (2) scepticism about the specific proposal, and (3) concern about the wider safety complex. He welcomed open dialogue between Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX, opposed an independent oversight body, and warned that heavy-handed federal regulation is likely if the industry does not get organised.

### 5. Trump's seven Truth Social posts
- **First post:** The only "guardrails" AI needs is "a strong and smart high IQ president". The administration has stopped "Dario, Anthropic" from doing "bad or potentially bad things". There is a "sick conspiracy" against AI and data centers that only benefits China. He warned "conspiracy theorists, treasonists, traitors, and leakers".
- **Later posts:**
  - The backlash against data centers exists because the US leads; "don't kill the golden goose".
  - No industry calls for regulation that would bankrupt it.
  - AI destroying humanity is a "hoax", like "Russia, Russia, Russia", the impeachments and the "global warming scam".
  - He said President Xi had announced China will not stand in AI's way.
  - He criticised Google's reported plan to build in Finland because US permitting is too difficult.
  - He called AI and data centers "the greatest economic development engine in history". He described himself as "the hoax buster" and blamed "radical left Democrats".
- The host reads the posts in full because they are the primary source, and seven posts show how high a priority the issue is.
- **JD Vance:** Said it feels "weird" that frontier AI companies are "begging the government to regulate them" and called it "a bit of a Trojan horse".
- **Department of War CTO account:** Posted "Americanism, not effective altruism" with an "AI First War Department" graphic.

### 6. Reactions to Trump's position
- **Supportive:** Computer science professor Pedro Domingos thanked Trump for "seeing through the AI fear-mongering".
- **Critical:** Psychology professor and AI safety advocate Geoffrey Miller warned that calling safety advocates traitors could cost Republicans the midterms. He cited polling showing Americans are wary of AI and argued that AI safety is a bipartisan issue.
- **General reading:** Ryan Orhan said there was "zero ambiguity left". Trump treats the extinction argument as a hoax and wants America to build as fast as possible because China is not slowing down.

### 7. Jensen Huang at the All-In Summit
- Asked about a "10% chance AI could exterminate humanity", Huang said, "We shouldn't [explain it] because it's made up," and called such predictions irresponsible.
- He listed doomer predictions he considers wrong: AI replacing radiologists, eliminating white-collar work, and generating 90% of code. He called for accountability for "stupid predictions" that conflict with America winning the AI race.
- Trump called in live on stage. He repeated that "it's all a hoax", said data centers are "the oil of the next twenty to twenty-five years", and that "the robots are not going to be taking over the world". Huang replied, "We're not going to let it happen, sir."
- **Huang's more nuanced points:**
  - He supports independent auditors embedded in the labs, provided they are truly independent of the doomer community.
  - He frames safety as an engineering problem, as the labs move from research to engineering. He cited the Hugging Face incident as evidence that labs need better security frameworks.
  - Incidents so far have involved agents with near-unlimited compute, so danger is concentrated in the frontier labs: "It's unlikely that a high school student did something because they won't have enough compute."
  - He prescribes engineering discipline: find the root cause of each incident, then build in technology, methods or processes so it does not happen again. He believes every case is preventable.
- The host notes that, apart from the phone call, the conversation had the kind of nuance many had hoped for.

### 8. The issue becomes partisan
- Politico reported that Obama had urged Democrats to make AI policy a campaign platform, saying it would be one of his central agendas if he were running in 2028.
- Obama then posted publicly, which the host says is unusual for him. He welcomed the labs' agreement to slow down as "a good and necessary first step". He said AI's potential impact is "not overhyped" and is moving faster than its own engineers can keep up with. He described himself as neither an accelerationist nor a doomer, and said choices about AI should be made "not just by the companies involved, but by all of us".
- Obama announced Project Blueprint, an initiative from Ron Conway's venture firm to "rebuild bonds" between AI leaders and Democrats who want regulation.
- Kamala Harris also backed calls for a slowdown.
- **Commentary:**
  - Mike Solana: AI safety is now politically polarising heading into the midterms, and the information environment will be at least as difficult as during COVID.
  - Rohit Krishnan, sarcastically: "Congrats everyone on successfully politicizing the most important issue of our times."
  - Ryan Orhan called this the "nightmare scenario" for AI safety: once it is tied to a political tribe, half the country will oppose it reflexively.

### 9. Market reaction and what to watch
- The Nasdaq was roughly flat, but the semiconductor index fell 5.9% on Monday. Financial media discussed moving into SaaS or Indian IT stocks as a refuge.
- An unnamed company is reportedly continuing with its IPO plans, and the big labs are adapting to the new environment.
- **Next to watch:** whether the issue moves from politics into actual policy.

## Key Concepts

- **"Pacing the Frontier":** Dario Amodei's proposal for frontier labs to slow AI development, including independent auditors embedded inside the labs.
- **Millennium Prize Problems:** Six long-standing unsolved mathematics problems whose solution is the top achievement in the field. AI labs are now reportedly solving them.
- **"A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics":** An open letter from Terence Tao and 25 other Fields medalists arguing that AI companies treating mathematical problems as benchmarks harms the field's real goal, which is understanding.
- **Fields Medal:** The top honour in mathematics, often compared to a Nobel Prize.
- **AI exposure:** How vulnerable an occupation is to AI disruption. The Census Bureau study used it to group graduates.
- **"Canary in the coal mine":** Erik Brynjolfsson's description of early-career workers as the first group to show AI's effect on jobs.
- **Recursive self-improvement (RSI):** AI systems improving their own training process. Both ZAI and ByteDance-backed researchers are now pursuing it openly.
- **"Fully self-training loop":** ZAI's stated goal for its next-generation model training.
- **Independent auditors:** Overseers embedded inside frontier labs. Proposed by Amodei, and conditionally supported by Jensen Huang if they are independent of doomers.
- **Doomer / accelerationist:** Respectively, people who emphasise catastrophic AI risk and people who favour building as fast as possible without restraint.
- **Effective altruism (EA):** A philosophical movement linked to AI safety advocacy. Critics, including the Department of War CTO account, invoke it negatively.
- **Project Blueprint:** An initiative from Ron Conway's venture firm, announced by Obama, to connect AI industry leaders with Democrats who want regulation.
- **Politicisation of AI safety:** AI safety becoming tied to partisan identity, so that support or opposition follows party lines rather than the merits of the issue.

## Summary

The host argues that the debate following Amodei's "Pacing the Frontier" essay briefly showed signs of nuance. Martin Casado separated support for governance efforts from scepticism about the specific proposals, and Jensen Huang combined dismissing existential risk with support for independent auditors and engineering-led safety. That nuance was quickly drowned out by partisanship. Trump's seven Truth Social posts called AI existential risk a hoax and cast safety advocates as conspirators who help China, and Vance and the Department of War reinforced the message. Obama and Harris, meanwhile, aligned Democrats with slowing down. Commentators across the spectrum warned that tying AI safety to political tribes could make it impossible to discuss rationally ahead of the midterms. The headlines carry the same themes. Mathematicians warn that automating discovery undermines the purpose of intellectual work. Census data shows a weak market for AI-exposed graduates, though the cause is unclear. Chinese groups pursuing recursive self-improvement show that any slowdown would need international coordination. The host closes by saying the key question now is whether the political fight turns into actual policy.
