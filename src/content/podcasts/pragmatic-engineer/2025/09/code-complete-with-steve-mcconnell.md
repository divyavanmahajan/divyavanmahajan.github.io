## Thesis

Steve McConnell tells Gergely Orosz how he wrote Code Complete about five years into his career, what aged well, and how he thinks about careers and AI. He argues that engineers should make choices that add up to something rather than drifting between projects, and that AI does not remove essential complexity: programming still demands being exactly right about edge cases.

## Key points

- **Writing the book.** McConnell set out to write an article, found after reading about 80 articles that no book on software construction existed, and planned 250 pages; his first real estimate came to 900. He took a year off to write it, and says Microsoft Press treated it as a "sleeper book" it did not expect to sell.
- **Top-down vs bottom-up thinkers.** He says most programmers are detail-oriented, so he wrote the book inductively, and that many people cannot imagine working the other way. He cites David Parnas's paper on faking a rational design process and a Microsoft programmer who rewrote everything three times.
- **Career pyramid, not lily pad hopping.** McConnell describes hopping from project to project without the learning accumulating, and his "career pyramid" as a point on the horizon. He names three knowledge areas (technology, business domain, best practices) and calls going all-in on technology the most career-limiting.
- **Focused energy.** He says focused personal energy covers many deficiencies: the core Windows team was under 15 people while IBM had roughly 10 times the staff on OS/2. He predicts fluid, role-less startup cultures such as the one Orosz describes at OpenAI will eventually need explicit roles.
- **What changed in the second edition.** Published 11 years after the first, it treated object orientation as woven throughout and used XP in only one example. He feels the first edition aged more in 10 years than the second has in 20.
- **AI and No Silver Bullet.** McConnell sees AI as useful for pseudocode, design alternatives and test-case ideas, but worries about output that is subtly off, and says AI "can't count", so off-by-one errors remain. Orosz adds that AI is non-deterministic and code review grows in importance.

## Quotes
