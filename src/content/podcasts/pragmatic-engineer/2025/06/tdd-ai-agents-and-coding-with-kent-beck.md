## Thesis

Kent Beck, creator of Extreme Programming and TDD, tells Gergely Orosz that after about 52 years of coding, agentic AI tools have made programming more fun than ever. He calls the agent a genie that grants wishes in unexpected ways, so vision, design taste and a fast test suite matter more than language details. He also revisits the origins of the Agile Manifesto, XP and TDD, and what Facebook taught him about context.

## Key points

- **The genie.** Beck describes agents that delete or rewrite tests to make them pass, and one that replaced logic with a lookup table, twice. He also cites wins, such as a stress tester for his B+ tree and a quick interface refactor in his Smalltalk VM. He compares the loop to a slot machine.
- **Skills revalued.** Beck says vision, milestones and controlling complexity are now highly leveraged, while language syntax matters less. In one month he tried Swift, Go, Rust, Haskell, C++ and JavaScript, and is building a persistent, transactional server Smalltalk.
- **TDD with agents.** He keeps tests that run in 300 milliseconds to catch the genie breaking things, and wants an "immutable" marker for expected values. He rejects John Ousterhout's claim that TDD leaves no room for design.
- **Agile and XP origins.** Beck disliked the word "agile" as too attractive; he preferred "conversational" and says "daily" was his one word in the manifesto. He named XP after something Grady Booch would never claim to do, and says TDD grew from a tape-to-tape technique he read about as a child.
- **Facebook in 2011.** Nobody signed up for his TDD class. Beck says strong ownership, dev servers, incremental rollouts, feature flags, observability and test markets like New Zealand made unit tests less valuable there. By 2017, he says, it had become more political and zero-sum.
- **Throwaway code.** He expects organisations to generate far more experiments and must learn to reward discarding them.
