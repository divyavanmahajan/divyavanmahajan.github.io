## Thesis

Grady Booch, co-creator of UML and IBM's Chief Scientist for Software Engineering, argues that the history of software is one of rising levels of abstraction, and that AI coding assistants are the latest step rather than a break. Software engineering, in his view, is the balancing of technical, economic, human and ethical forces, and code is only one tool for doing it.

## Key points

- **Three golden ages.** Booch dates the first golden age from the late 1940s to the late 1970s, built on algorithmic abstraction for business and numerical work. The second, in the 1980s and 1990s, used object abstraction. The third began around 2000, when the unit of reuse grew to libraries, packages and platforms such as AWS and Salesforce.
- **Innovation at the fringes.** He says much early progress came from defence work, such as Whirlwind and the SAGE air-defence system, which by some reports used 20 to 30% of US software developers, while the software crisis of the 1970s led to the Ada project.
- **Not the first existential crisis.** Booch recalls that compilers and Fortran made assembly programmers fear for their jobs. Today's agents, he says, move developers up a level and free them from tedium, while a new wave of hobbyists builds throwaway software, much like the early PC era.
- **Response to Dario Amodei.** Asked about the Anthropic CEO's claim that software engineering will be automatable in 12 months, Booch, who uses Claude himself, calls it "utter bullshit". He argues the tools mostly automate patterns seen many times, such as web-centric CRUD apps, and that computing is far larger than that.
- **Where jobs shift.** Booch expects the software delivery pipeline and simple app building to be automated, with people there needing to reskill. Engineers who manage complexity at scale will, he says, be in greater demand.
- **Foundations to study.** He recommends Simon's The Sciences of the Artificial, the Santa Fe Institute's work on complexity, Minsky's Society of Mind for multi-agent architecture, and Rodney Brooks' subsumption architectures.
