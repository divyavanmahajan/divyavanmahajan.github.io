## Thesis

Joshua Browder, founder of DoNotPay, argues that large companies already use AI in customer service and debt collection, so consumers should have AI on their side too. He tells Nathan Labenz that regulation should ban clearly harmful uses and place liability on large platforms, while leaving startups and consumers free to use the technology.

## Key points

- **From templates to GPT-4.** Browder started DoNotPay in 2015 to appeal parking tickets and now covers over 200 use cases. Template letters lost effect once companies saw thousands of identical ones; a GPT-4 bot that negotiates bills on Comcast chat pushes back harder than GPT-3 did, and he cites a 10x improvement in success rate.
- **The ethics of not disclosing.** When one utility said it did not accept AI disputes, Browder says the bot replied that it was just a consumer who knew its rights. He opposes general AI-disclosure rules, citing iMessage autocomplete, but accepts disclosure for political calls and human sign-off in serious matters such as federal court filings.
- **Ban for evil, regulate the giants.** Browder wants AI banned in debt collection and criminal sentencing, and points to robocall law and SHAKEN/STIR as a model where carriers carry responsibility. He thinks liability shifted onto platforms would stop about 95% of misuse.
- **Deflation and jobs.** He argues customer service is around 10% of corporate costs, so AI should lower prices broadly. He guesses 30% of white-collar jobs could go within five years and 60% of jobs within 15, and supports redistributing some gains.
- **Fighting the legal profession.** DoNotPay avoids competing with lawyers directly, is seeking an AI assistance law with Congressman Ritchie Torres, and sometimes acts first and asks forgiveness later.
- **Running lean.** With seven staff, OpenAI API spend is among DoNotPay's largest costs. Browder routes simple requests to GPT-3.5 or open models, and describes a trap credit card that identifies robocallers so users can sue them.
