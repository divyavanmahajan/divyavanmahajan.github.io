## Thesis

In a crossover from Dan Shipper's series "How I Use ChatGPT", Nathan Labenz separates two ways of working with AI: copilot mode, where a person stays in charge and pulls in help in real time, and delegation mode, where output must be reliable enough not to need review. Both argue that most current value sits in copilot mode, and Shipper adds that it fails mainly when people treat it as delegation and ask it to do a whole piece of work.

## Key points

- **Two modes and a gap.** Labenz says the missing middle is delegating larger subtasks on the fly, which he expects agents to begin closing within a year. He calls the overall impact largely good as long as humans stay in control.
- **Learning React with ChatGPT.** At Athena, an executive-assistant company where he advises on AI, Labenz built a "prompt coach" module for an in-house chat app. Never having written React, he finished in 2 to 3 hours and estimates an 80 to 90% time saving over working alone.
- **Do not suppress reasoning.** He warns that few-shot question-answer scaffolding, a habit from before instruction models such as text-davinci-002, pushes GPT-4 to answer immediately and skip its step-by-step reasoning.
- **Writing as micro-tasks.** Shipper uses ChatGPT to summarise utilitarianism for an essay on FTX, turn walk recordings into ideas, outline messy drafts and critique intros at 2 a.m. Labenz says he rarely uses it for writing in his own voice and finds autocomplete can derail a half-formed thought.
- **Context and tools.** Shipper's custom instructions list family, colleagues and traits he is working on. Labenz generated Graphviz diagrams for a Waymark patent filing, restarting chats when the model got confused, and used Perplexity to compare used-minivan trim levels.
- **Outlook.** Labenz expects AI to become superhuman in consequential ways without being infallible, citing Google DeepMind's diagnosis results and mixed architectures such as Mamba.
