## Thesis

In this rebroadcast of the Future of Life Institute podcast, economist Samuel Hammond tells Gus Docker that AI trained on human data is on a smooth path to human-level performance, possibly this decade. His main concern is second-order effects: AI collapses the transaction costs that institutions are built around. Western governments that cannot adapt quickly may lose functions to private, "techno-feudalist" providers.

## Key points

- **Timelines.** Hammond cites Epoch AI's "direct approach" forecast (modal 2029, median around 2036). He argues in-context learning lets AI slot into existing human processes, so implementation friction will be lower than in past automation waves.
- **Weak state capacity.** He notes US social security numbers date to 1935 and IRS systems to the Kennedy era. He blames what Nicholas Bagley calls the "procedural fetish", contrasting it with the Manhattan Project and with Estonia's X-Road.
- **Transaction costs and information.** Drawing on Ronald Coase, Hammond says AI cuts monitoring, bargaining and agency costs. Examples include gait recognition, reconstructing keystrokes from audio and decoding fMRI images.
- **Leviathan and open source.** Following Hobbes, he sets out three responses to empowering technology: a stronger Leviathan, adaptation and mitigation, or changing norms. Open-sourcing a 70-billion-parameter model is fine, he says, but a near-perfect lie detector is not.
- **Default timeline.** His scenario runs from a balkanised internet (2024-2027) and easier-than-expected alignment, to robotics solved in 2036-2039, ending with micro-jurisdictions resembling Gulf monarchies. He presents Uber replacing taxi commissions as a small-scale version.
- **Constrained Leviathan.** The alternative is to speed AI adoption inside government, for example an FTC lawyer using Claude 2 on 40,000 subpoenaed emails, plus a Manhattan Project for AI safety. On low interest rates, he says markets are often wrong about technology.
