## Thesis

Trey Kollmer, a Writers Guild member, argues that the guild's AI demands make the most sense in a medium-progress world: weak models change little and very strong models would be hard to hold back. He also tells fellow writers to look at what the models actually do instead of dismissing them. Nathan Labenz agrees, and adds that being easy to trip up does not rule out real reasoning.

## Key points

- **WGA offer.** Kollmer reports that the AMPTP's public offer says AI-generated material cannot count as source material or a first draft, which closes the rewrite-pay loophole. Whether studios can train models on scripts they own as works for hire is still unresolved.
- **Actors' likeness.** On SAG-AFTRA, Kollmer says studios agreed to consent and pay for reusing a scanned likeness, but not for using scans as training data for synthetic characters. He expects stars like Meryl Streep could end up taking more roles.
- **Fine-tuning and creativity.** Labenz says fine-tuning at Waymark was "night and day" better than few-shot prompting. Kollmer suggests models struggle with "the one unusual thing" in comedy because they sample at one fixed temperature.
- **Top-hat test.** Kollmer's GPT-4 puzzle, a softball inside a top hat with the top cut out, always got the answer "dining room table". Labenz describes how abstracting the nouns and breaking problems into sub-problems improved results in his theory-of-mind work.
- **NVIDIA's share of value.** Labenz notes NVIDIA is up 238% this year to a $1.2 trillion market cap, compared with about 40% for ASML ($270 billion) and TSMC at $445 billion. He suggests supply agreements and the CUDA moat as possible reasons.
- **Where value goes.** Labenz expects consumer surplus, cloud compute and frontier model providers to do well, and incumbents like Salesforce and Adobe to win at the app layer unless a product is entirely new, as with virtual friends.
