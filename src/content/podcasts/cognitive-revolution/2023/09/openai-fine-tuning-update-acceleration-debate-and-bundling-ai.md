## Thesis

Nathan Labenz argues that the GPT-3.5 fine-tuning loop, fed with synthetic chain-of-thought data from GPT-4, makes everyday AI apps cheap and fast, and that this is the kind of progress to accelerate. Training beyond GPT-4 should wait until datasets can be cleaned. He also proposes a shared subscription bundle for AI apps. Erik Torenberg doubts that competitors will cooperate. (The published transcript swaps the two hosts' speaker labels; attributions here follow the context.)

## Key points

- **Chain of thought as training data.** For Waymark's video scripts, Labenz first fine-tuned 3.5 on plain inputs and outputs, which was not good enough. He then had GPT-4 write its reasoning before each script and trained 3.5 on that.
- **Cost and latency.** His GPT-4 prompts came close to the 8,000-token limit and cost 30-35 cents per call, taking 60-90 seconds. The fine-tuned 3.5 drops the examples, often answers in under 10 seconds, and cuts cost by roughly 90%.
- **What to slow down.** Labenz supports extra scrutiny only for systems more powerful than GPT-4. He wants labs to filter pathogen-relevant data before training, and calls Meta the biggest wildcard.
- **Copilot versus delegation.** He says GPT-4 makes his coding several times faster but he still drives. Delegation means trusting the output without reviewing it all, such as screening 1,000 job applications.
- **Growth and displacement.** Labenz says OpenAI went from high-twenties millions in 2022 revenue to a $1 billion run rate. Skipped calls to lawyers show displacement is already happening.
- **The bundle.** Waymark pays about 15 cents per trial user, so one $30 subscriber covers about 200 triers. In a $100 bundle, a one-thousandth share would earn $100,000 a month per million subscribers. Torenberg cites Uber and Lyft refusing to merge as a sign that firms will not cooperate. Labenz suggests rival bundles in the style of Sling TV, such as OpenAI-Microsoft against Anthropic-Google.
