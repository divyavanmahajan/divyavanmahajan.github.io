## Thesis

Yohei Nakajima, a VC at Untapped Capital and creator of BabyAGI, argues that building small automations in public is both a learning practice and a source of deal flow, and that simple agent loops already deliver practical value. As an investor he expects incumbents to capture a larger share of AI value than in past waves, but thinks the total pie is big enough for startups to do well.

## Key points

- **Origins of BabyAGI.** Nakajima built it as a weekend prototype of an "autonomous startup founder", inspired by HustleGPT: a task-creation, prioritization and execution prompt in a loop, about 105 lines of code. He chose not to turn it into a company, partly after running an NFT collection alongside his fund.
- **Animal-named forks.** Rather than maintaining one canonical version, he keeps modding his own code into animal-named variants (BabyB AGI, BabyCat, Baby Fox AGI), each testing a novel idea; Baby Fox AGI added a UI that runs multiple tasks at once.
- **Where to start with AI.** He advises applying automation to things you wish you did but don't, not critical workflows. His example: before calls with large companies, an agent researches business units and proposes 3 LLM strategies each, turning 15 minutes of research into a 10-second prompt.
- **Incumbents versus startups.** Nakajima says this wave is unusual because both C-level executives and engineers want AI, so incumbents will move fast; even so, companies that execute poorly will buy from startups or spin out founders.
- **Business models.** He sees wrappers that win through workflow lock-in, LLM-built structured data with higher margins, and "scaled services" that feel like a service but run on AI, citing Obviously AI's data-scientist-as-a-service at 1000 dollars a month.
- **Building in public and risk.** Open source suits founders without networks or those selling dev tools; well-connected enterprise teams have less reason to. On p(doom) he reframes the question around timelines and other risks, and calls himself an optimist.
