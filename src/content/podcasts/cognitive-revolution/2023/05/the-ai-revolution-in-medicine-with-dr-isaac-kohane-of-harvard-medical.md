## Thesis

Isaac Kohane, chair of Biomedical Informatics at Harvard Medical School and co-author of "The AI Revolution in Medicine", argues from months of early GPT-4 access that the model is clinically impressive but alien. He rejects classic drug-style trials and exam scores as the main way to validate it, and favors a "partner" model in which the AI checks the doctor's work while a human stays in the loop. Host Nathan Labenz presents the book as a model for hands-on AI study in any field.

## Key points

- **Early access.** Kohane says Peter Lee of Microsoft Research gave him GPT-4 before ChatGPT launched. On a hard pediatric case it reached a molecular diagnosis of 11-hydroxylase deficiency, which he doubts 1 in 100 doctors would manage.
- **Test it yourself.** He ran hundreds of real scenarios, from screening to billing codes, and urges decision makers to use the tools directly. He started NEJM AI to provide clinical-grade validation.
- **Why trials fall short.** Kohane says a GPT-4 conversation can wander across all of medicine, results will differ between Boston, China and Africa, and the model itself changed between October 2022 and April 2023, unlike a static drug.
- **The partner paradigm.** He wants an AI listening in on visits and reading notes, flagging missed tests, duplicate orders and newer drugs. He expects it could lift the weaker half of doctors toward the top half, and that ambient speech recognition could draft clinic notes and reduce burnout.
- **Limits on discovery.** Kohane says GPT-4 cannot yet form new testable hypotheses, but on Undiagnosed Diseases Network cases it ranked first the gene that researchers had validated, in part through fruit fly experiments.
- **Alignment and data.** He expects separate AIs aligned to insurers, hospitals and patients, and prefers patient-consented data, citing Apple Health's links to 800 hospitals.
