## Thesis

Rachel Woods, founder of The AI Exchange and a former Meta data scientist, argues that business adoption of AI is far earlier than the AI bubble assumes: most companies did not have AI in their 2023 roadmap and many have tried ChatGPT without finding it useful. Her advice is to start with internal operations, where risk is low and feedback loops are tight, and move to customer-facing products later. Nathan Labenz broadly agrees, though he is more bullish on GPT-4 and ChatGPT becoming the default hub.

## Key points

- **Who watches AI TikTok.** Woods says 90% of her TikTok audience is over 25 and 50% over 35, mostly US business leaders, founders and engineers. Within 24 hours of adding a booking link on 1 January, six people bought time with her; she has since done over 100 such calls, including a public pharma company's board meeting.
- **Internal before external.** Woods separates improving internal operations from offering new AI products, and calls the external route far riskier. She recommends internal hackathons to build AI literacy, and patience with tools: no annual plans.
- **GPT-4's leap.** Labenz says GPT-4 rarely needs examples and can handle segmented prompts that confused GPT-3.5. Woods replies that only a small share of the workforce knows such techniques, so to most companies AI as a whole is the step change.
- **AI in her own business.** Woods uses GPT as a simple classifier, running scripts over subscriber forms to find common themes and personalise outreach. A tuned prompt turns TikTok transcripts into LinkedIn drafts that she reviews. She estimates her team uses only 2% to 5% of what the technology can already do.
- **Launching and safety.** Woods favours launching early to learn, with moderation such as OpenAI's endpoint. Labenz, drawing on his GPT-4 red-team work, says Bing's Sydney launch felt rushed and welcomes OpenAI saying it is not training GPT-5.
- **Hopes and fears.** Woods hopes AI lets people work less and more like they do when inspired; her biggest fear is centralised adoption by 1% of people rather than democratised access.
