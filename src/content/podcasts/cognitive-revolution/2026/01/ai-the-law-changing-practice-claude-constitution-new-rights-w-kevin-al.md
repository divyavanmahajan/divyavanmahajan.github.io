## Thesis

Law professors Kevin Frazier and Alan Rozenshtein, hosts of the Scaling Laws podcast, argue that law is largely a cognitive discipline that AI will transform as it is transforming programming, only more slowly because lawyers control the rules of their own guild. Beyond practice, they see AI forcing new thinking about how laws are written, which new rights individuals need, and what limits to place on government use of AI.

## Key points

- **Models versus lawyers.** Rozenshtein says frontier models are already better than the median lawyer in raw horsepower and bets on a Jevons paradox: cheaper legal services will mean more demand, not fewer lawyers. Labenz notes Claude Opus 4.5 tops the lawyers category in a public arena comparison.
- **Adoption lags.** Frazier says about 70% of top US law firms use Harvey, yet training and usage are thin because the billable hour rewards spending time. Firms now whisper about hiring fewer junior associates, and "secret cyborgs" hide their AI use.
- **Apprenticeship and deskilling.** Rozenshtein uses fewer student research assistants since a Gemini and Claude script triages law review articles for him, and worries about losing the apprenticeship path. He expects low-agency workers to struggle most.
- **Access and outcome-oriented law.** Frazier notes that 95% of litigation happens in slow state courts and that whoever can pay longest tends to win. He points to Arizona allowing non-lawyer ownership of firms, sandboxes in Texas and Utah, and proposes simulating bills against stated goals before passing them, citing NEPA.
- **Rules versus principles.** Rozenshtein reads Claude's constitution as Aristotelian: mostly principles plus a few hard rules. He argues the rules-versus-standards debate is about tuning the mix, which AI now lets us test in silico.
- **New rights and restraints.** Frazier backs the Right to Compute (enacted in Montana) and a "right to share" personal data despite laws like FERPA. Rozenshtein warns of a "Unitary Artificial Executive" in which AI gives the president fine-grained control of the bureaucracy, and expects AI welfare to become a social fault line; Frazier flags mass audio surveillance of public spaces.
