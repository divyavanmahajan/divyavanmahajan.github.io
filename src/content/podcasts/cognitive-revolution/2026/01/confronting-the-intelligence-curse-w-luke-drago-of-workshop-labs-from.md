## Thesis

In this cross-post from the Future of Life Institute podcast, host Gus Docker talks to Luke Drago, co-author of The Intelligence Curse and co-founder of Workshop Labs. Drago argues that when non-human factors of production dominate an economy, states and firms lose the incentive to invest in people, much as oil states neglect citizens. Because people's economic value underpins their political power, AI designed to replace rather than augment workers risks a world where "people just don't matter", unless intelligence is commoditised and people keep control of their own data.

## Key points

- **Pyramid replacement.** Drago expects AI to replace entry-level white-collar work first and then move up the hierarchy. He points to falling employment for 22 to 25-year-olds in exposed fields such as software, and suggests watching inequality, mobility and youth unemployment as warning signs.
- **Why UBI isn't enough.** Pensioners earned their protections through decades of work and bargaining power; a society where everyone is permanently dependent rests on fragile politics, shrinking income-tax bases and, eventually, automated repression.
- **Breaking the curse.** Norway escaped the resource curse because it already had strong, low-corruption institutions, which Drago doubts the US has. His remedies: defensive technologies so safety fears don't justify centralisation (citing tamper-resistant models trained without biology data), commoditised intelligence via open-weights models, and tools that keep humans in control.
- **Open weights.** He is more pro-open-source than most in AI safety, noting Chinese open-weights models are roughly six months behind the frontier, and argues safety work should focus on making such models tamper-resistant.
- **Workshop Labs.** The company bets that tacit and local knowledge is AI's bottleneck and fine-tunes models privately on a user's own data in Nvidia secure enclaves, as a public benefit corporation. Drago calls data "the new social security number" and wants agents loyal to users, with clearly labelled ads and something like Sam Altman's "AI privilege".
- **Career advice.** He urges young people to pursue moonshots and "N of 1" roles, since large employers' entry-level jobs are the first automation targets.
