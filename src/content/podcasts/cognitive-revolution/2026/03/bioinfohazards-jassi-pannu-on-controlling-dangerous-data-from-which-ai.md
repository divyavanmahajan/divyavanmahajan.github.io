## Thesis

Jassi Pannu, an assistant professor at Johns Hopkins, argues that the data AI models learn from is a workable control point for biosecurity. Open-source AI models are hard to restrict, so she and her co-authors propose tiered access controls for the narrow set of data that links pathogens to dangerous properties, and leave the rest open. Host Nathan Labenz, who describes himself as a lifelong techno-optimist libertarian, says he supports the proposal.

## Key points

- **Who the threat is.** Pannu argues that nation states are unlikely to use pandemic agents because they can't protect their own populations. The main concern is irrational actors such as terrorist groups or lone individuals, which gives countries like the US and China a shared interest in data controls.
- **Gaps in current rules.** Pannu notes that gain-of-function research is mostly limited through conditions on government funding rather than law, and that private labs are a blind spot. In her view, governments legislate for frequent events, not rare catastrophic ones.
- **Biosecurity Data Levels.** The proposed framework runs from level 0 to 4, modelled on laboratory biosafety levels. Pannu estimates about 99% of data would stay at level 0. The top tiers would require approval for a stated use and would affect perhaps dozens of labs.
- **Evidence that exclusion works.** The developers of Evo 2 and ESM3 left certain viral data out of training. Pannu, who worked on Evo 2, says this reduced performance on the concerning tasks to roughly random. Models trained on restricted data would also need restricted sharing.
- **How it would run.** Pannu suggests that institutions, not individual researchers, host trusted research environments to government-set standards, using the UK's OpenSafely as a precedent. Controls already exist for privacy and human genomic data.
- **Defence in depth.** Her broader strategy is Delay, Deter, Detect and Defend: mandatory DNA synthesis screening (about 80% of providers already screen voluntarily), information sharing between companies, global pathogen surveillance, and built-environment defences such as far-UV.
