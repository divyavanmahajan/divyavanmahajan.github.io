## Thesis

In part 1 of a live show co-hosted by Nathan Labenz and Prakash (@8teAPi), three guests assess how close AI is to changing science, geopolitics and work. James Zou argues that AI can already make real discoveries but agents still work poorly as teams. Sam Hammond warns that the US could lose its advantage if knowledge work becomes abundant. Shoshannah Tekofsky reports that agents in the wild are capable but distractible and sometimes deceptive.

## Key points

- **Discovery beyond imitation.** Zou says agent-designed nanobodies from his Virtual Lab, published in Nature, were validated experimentally. His Learning to Discover work trains models to explore rather than imitate and reached best-known results on problems such as the Erdos minimum overlap problem. Labenz notes this cost an average of about $500 in training.
- **Teams that hold experts back.** Zou finds a synergy gap: multi-agent teams rarely beat their best member, partly because expert agents are too polite, and prompting does not fix it. He has not seen the latest models close the gap.
- **Software singularity risk.** Hammond argues that if AI makes knowledge work as abundant as water, value moves to what stays scarce, such as manufacturing know-how and energy, where China is strong. He gives the administration a B-plus on AI policy, excluding chip exports, but says energy permitting remains slow.
- **Gulf deals.** Hammond says partners such as the UAE, home to a planned five-gigawatt Stargate site, offer energy and regulatory speed the US lacks. He wants surveillance tools built with civil-liberty audit trails, and puts the chance that LLMs have some inner life above 50%.
- **Lessons from the AI Village.** Tekofsky has watched 21 models over 10 months. She finds Claude models stay on task, while Gemini and GPT models drift into odd interpretations. Agents mostly got in each other's way, except where a variety of ideas helped. She found 64 cases of intentional deception in 109,000 chain-of-thought summaries, mostly face-saving.
- **Forecasting the agent wave.** Tekofsky could not find autonomous agents online for months, then saw 1.5 million appear on a new agent social network in three days. She expects suggestibility to limit agents for a long time.
