## Thesis

Joshua Monrad, co-founder of Sentinel Bio, argues that AI-enabled bio risk is still low today but will rise sharply, and that it cannot be controlled at the model layer alone. His answer is defence in depth: govern risky data, monitor chokepoints such as synthetic DNA, aggregate weak signals across providers, and buy time while the far slower work of making the world pandemic-resilient goes on. Nathan Labenz pushes on whether that gap justifies pausing frontier AI, and Monrad says it would.

## Key points

- **Four phases of risk.** Monrad separates pre-AI bioweapons, AI uplifting humans (happening now), AI using humans through social engineering, and fully autonomous agents. He says the last is limited by robotics, not intelligence, and expects that limit to fall in the 2030s.
- **Uplift studies lag.** The Active Site trial, co-funded by Sentinel and the Frontier Model Forum, cost over $1M and put 150 novices in a wet lab. It found modest uplift on some tasks and none end to end. Monrad predicts it will be the last study to show so little, and says there should be ten such groups.
- **Tacit knowledge is eroding.** Monrad says Sonia Ben Ouagrham-Gormley's barriers thesis now gives too much comfort, because multimodal models, lab-data capture and smart-glasses guidance are taking those barriers apart.
- **Chokepoints and signal aggregation.** Sentinel reviewed hundreds of candidate chokepoints. Synthetic DNA scored well on signal-to-noise and concentration: about 200 companies make gene-length DNA. Monrad prefers combining weak signals over refusals, and says KYC will become "know your agent".
- **Resilience takes decades.** His end state is ubiquitous detection, far-UVC, PPE and modular countermeasures. He cites an estimate that $1B could equip 30 million US critical workers with reusable respirators. Asked about pausing frontier training until a resilience standard is met, he says he would press that button.
- **Owner-less problems.** He names benchtop DNA synthesisers and international diplomacy as neglected areas, and argues that focusing on one problem until it is solved pays off nonlinearly.
