## Thesis

HeyGen founder Joshua Xu argues that AI can become the new camera, letting businesses that cannot film well still make video. He and Benchmark's Victor Lazarte say the goal is engagement and fast iteration, not photorealism. Nathan Labenz singles out HeyGen's layered trust and safety system as the best he has tested.

## Key points

- **Origins.** Xu worked at Snap from 2014 to 2020 on ads and the AI camera, where GANs convinced him generated content could look real. He founded HeyGen in December 2020; it now serves more than 40,000 businesses with $35 million in annual revenue.
- **Create, localize, personalize.** Xu groups use into three pillars. Video translation covers more than 40 languages while keeping the speaker's voice and lip sync; he says it cuts cost by more than 10x versus dubbing at roughly $10 to $20 a minute.
- **Engagement over realism.** Xu says avatars already pass as real in many cases; the open problems are conversation flow and natural gestures. Lazarte, who ran a mobile gaming company with 100 million video views a month, says cheap, fast testing matters more than fidelity.
- **Real-time and personal video.** Lazarte highlights the streaming avatar, a real-time video clone, as the next step after ChatGPT's voice mode. Xu imagines serving each viewer a different version of a video instead of one averaged edit.
- **Beyond the avatar.** Xu says HeyGen is not an avatar company: after the A-roll presenter, it is investing in B-roll generation. He predicts the timeline editor will disappear within five years.
- **Trust and safety.** HeyGen blocks political and celebrity figures, requires video consent with a randomly generated code for every avatar, and reviews videos with AI and humans. Labenz says he could not break the main protections.
