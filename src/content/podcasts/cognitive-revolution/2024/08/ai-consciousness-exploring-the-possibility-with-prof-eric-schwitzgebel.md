## Thesis

Eric Schwitzgebel, a philosopher at UC Riverside, argues that every major theory of consciousness forces some bizarre commitments, so nobody can confidently say whether AI systems are or will be conscious. Nathan Labenz leans towards precaution because interpretability research shows concept-like structure inside models. Schwitzgebel answers with his "design policy of the excluded middle": build systems that are clearly not conscious or clearly deserving of moral consideration, not ones in between.

## Key points

- **Define by example.** Schwitzgebel defines consciousness by pointing to pain, sensory experience, imagery and inner speech, not to unrecalled knowledge or the myelination of axons. He separates being conscious from being able to suffer.
- **Every theory bites bullets.** Idealism (Berkeley) is openly strange; substance dualism has to decide whether dogs or snails have souls; Philip Goff's panpsychism still needs a line, Luke Roelofs' version does not. He gives materialism about 50% credence, with property dualism (Chalmers) and Kantian transcendental idealism in the rest.
- **Concepts or correlations?** Labenz cites sparse autoencoders and Golden Gate Claude. Schwitzgebel replies that the same structure wired to pixels would look like a mess, so meaning depends on how a system connects to the world, like one pressure gauge serving as barometer or altimeter.
- **Self-reports prove little.** GPT-3.5 is trained to deny consciousness while Replika is not, so answers reflect design. He expects users to split between "conscious partner" and "complex toaster" views with no theory to decide.
- **Higher-order loops.** Labenz describes a paper, "Unexpected Benefits of Self-Modeling in Neural Systems", where self-prediction simplified internal states. Schwitzgebel notes current LLMs lack such a recursive self-monitoring loop, which some theories require.
- **Costs of precaution.** Treating uncertain AIs as rights-holders could mean saving two GPUs over a human in a fire, and would raise existential risk. He proposes careful research models but simpler feed-forward versions for public use, and rejects the farm-animal analogy as a route to disposable, subservient entities.
