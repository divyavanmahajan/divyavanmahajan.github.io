## Thesis

Nathan Labenz asks Timothy B. Lee, author of the Understanding AI newsletter, where autonomous vehicles really stand. Lee argues the line that matters is liability: Tesla sells driver assistance, while Waymo runs truly driverless robotaxis inside a geofence. He thinks Waymo's lead comes from years spent on rare edge cases, and that its slow rollout is mostly about technology and operations, not regulators.

## Key points

- **Two kinds of vehicle.** Lee finds the SAE levels unhelpful and splits cars into driver assistance, where the human is responsible, and driverless service, where the company is. In his tests, Tesla FSD needed two takeovers in 45 minutes in San Francisco, while nine Waymo rides over about three hours had no mistakes.
- **The long tail is the hard part.** Waymo saw one takeover every 5,000 miles back in 2016 and ordered 60,000 vehicles in 2018, but did not go driverless until 2020. Lee points to emergency scenes and police hand signals as cases that need reasoning, not lane rules.
- **Sensors and data.** Waymo carries 20 or 30 sensors, including lidar for direct distance measurement. Tesla relies on cameras and a huge fleet. Lee expects Waymo's data to be smaller but of higher quality.
- **Safety statistics.** Humans have a fatal crash about once every 100 million miles, and Waymo has driven about 20 million, so fatality data is still thin. Waymo's injury-crash rate looks about five times better than comparable human driving, which Swiss Re backed. Tesla's numbers are harder to trust.
- **Regulation is not the bottleneck.** Lee says carmakers self-certify against federal standards and states regulate drivers. Waymo's limits are freeways, where stopping when unsure is unsafe, and building depots. It grew from about 10,000 to 50,000 trips a week in a year.
- **Lessons for language models.** Lee sees the LLM market repeating early self-driving: second-tier companies may fold, and the last 1% of reliability is much harder than the first 90%.
