## Thesis

Samo Burja, founder of Bismarck Analysis, argues that the US has overinvested in AI compute and underinvested in fundamental AI science, that it needs real industrial policy to compete in hardware, and that it should frame China as an economic competitor rather than a military rival. Nathan Labenz largely shares his worry about an AI arms race.

## Key points

- **Science over scale.** Burja compares AI to physics: a world that funds accelerators but not theorists hits a wall. He points out that diffusion models, not transformers, generate images, and expects a "periodic table" of different kinds of intelligence. Labenz calls this a "mixture of architectures" era and cites state space models.
- **Industrial policy for chips.** Burja says even TSMC faces unit costs 50% higher in America, so the free market will not provide; he proposes a special economic zone, possibly in Virginia, with the government buying domestic compute. He describes America as the industrial peer of Germany, not China.
- **The chip ban backfired.** In his view the export controls came too late (they would have worked around 2010) and act as a subsidy for Chinese chipmakers, who now only need to beat a nerfed NVIDIA product.
- **Other players.** Burja expects a formidable Indian AI giant within 5-10 years, backed by 1.5 billion users, citing Yandex as proof that a walled market can sustain a local ecosystem. He is bearish on Europe.
- **Against weaponisation and the Leopold plan.** Burja argues weaponised AI invites existential risk and that the US cannot credibly offer China benefit sharing. He adds that securing the US tech stack against China would mean securing it against US citizens. Labenz raises research on minimising self-other distinction as a more hopeful safety direction.
- **Personal use.** Burja rarely uses generative AI because it returns consensus views, while his work looks for where consensus is wrong.
