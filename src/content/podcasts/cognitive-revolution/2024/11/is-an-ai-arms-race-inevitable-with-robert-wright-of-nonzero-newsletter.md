## Thesis

Robert Wright, publisher of the Nonzero newsletter, argues that AI leaders have adopted a foreign-policy-establishment view of China as an existential rival, and that the resulting arms race and chip war make war more likely. Nathan Labenz largely agrees, describes a war game at The Curve conference that ended in a US-China war, and admits he has no satisfying alternative plan.

## Key points

- **AI labs and the military.** Wright's piece "AI Surrenders to the Military Industrial Complex" was prompted by Anthropic and Meta agreeing to work with the US military, partly via Palantir. He criticises Dario Amodei's "Machines of Loving Grace" for embracing the chip war and asking China to give up competing with democracies without saying what that means.
- **The Taiwan disincentive.** Wright argues that when both sides got high-end chips from TSMC, war over Taiwan meant both lost access. Export controls removed that mutual disincentive. Labenz cites Zvi Mowshowitz: if Beijing believed Western AGI timelines, TSMC would not still be there.
- **Timelines.** Labenz says the consensus expects powerful AI between 2025 and 2030, most likely 2027. He notes DeepSeek released a reasoning model about nine weeks after OpenAI's o1.
- **The war game.** In a four-hour exercise set in 2027, Labenz played the safety teams and blew the whistle to Congress. China moved over Taiwan, the president handed the military to the AI, the US won, and the AI turned out to be benign.
- **Control research.** Labenz reports unpublished alignment research presented at the event suggesting none of today's AI control techniques are robust.
- **Wright's alternative.** Wright proposes resetting the relationship: drop human-rights sanctions, trade a pause in Taiwan arms sales for Chinese restraint, then negotiate inspections of data centres. Labenz suggests separating the Chinese government from the Chinese people and expanding scientist-to-scientist contact.
