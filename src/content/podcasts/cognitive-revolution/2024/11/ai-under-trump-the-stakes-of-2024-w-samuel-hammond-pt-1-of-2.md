## Thesis

Samuel Hammond, senior economist at the Foundation for American Innovation, makes an AI-first case for Trump. If transformative AI arrives within the next term, he argues, the US needs a disruptive, ideas-driven government that can rebuild state capacity, and the Democratic coalition of interest groups cannot deliver that. Nathan Labenz accepts much of the diagnosis but rejects the bet. He sees Trump as erratic and zero-sum, likely to escalate with China at a moment that calls for stable, evidence-responsive leadership, and he says he will vote for Harris.

## Key points

- **Two kinds of party.** Hammond draws on Grossmann and Hopkins' *Asymmetric Politics*: Democrats are a coalition of stakeholder groups, Republicans an ideological vehicle. He cites Operation Warp Speed, which Chicago School economists at the FDA made possible, as the kind of first-best move only the second type produces.
- **Personnel and counter-establishment.** Hammond points to Balaji Srinivasan's place on the 2016 FDA shortlist and to AI-safety voices on Project 2025 committees. He argues the right is building a "counterestablishment" that can staff agencies. Labenz replies that the actual Republican Congress looks nothing like this.
- **Creative destruction in government.** Hammond says the public sector has no profit-and-loss mechanism, so reform needs deliberate disruption. He gives the example of Jean Chrétien cutting a third of Canada's federal civil service. Labenz warns that chaotic disruption is far more dangerous in a state that holds 5,000 nuclear weapons.
- **Racing China and the chip ban.** Hammond defends export controls as robust across scenarios and as a way to keep US standard-setting power, noting NVIDIA's jump from about half a million H100s to a projected 4 million. Labenz, a self-described China dove, fears "mutually assured AI destruction" and says the risk of a runaway AI comes overwhelmingly from the US.
- **Values and joint ventures.** Hammond expects a lab-government joint venture along Manhattan Project lines and favours an Estonia-style digital state that protects liberty. Labenz says he would sooner trust Xi than Trump to oversee such a partnership.
