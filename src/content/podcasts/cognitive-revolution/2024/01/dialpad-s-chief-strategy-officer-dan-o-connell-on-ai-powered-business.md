## Thesis

Dan O'Connell, Dialpad's Chief AI and Strategy Officer, argues that owning the whole stack, from telephony and GPUs to speech recognition and language models, lets a communications platform deliver real-time AI assistance faster and cheaper than relying on third-party APIs. Nathan Labenz sees Dialpad as a natural home for AI employees; O'Connell agrees on support chatbots but expects voice and sales automation to take longer than Labenz predicts.

## Key points

- **Scale and customers.** O'Connell says Dialpad is north of $200 million ARR with 30,000 customers split roughly evenly across SMB, mid-market and enterprise, including Stripe, Uber and HubSpot.
- **Built in-house.** His TalkIQ startup built real-time speech recognition on Kaldi in 2016; Dialpad now runs NVIDIA NeMo models tuned to its telephony codecs, trains DialpadGPT on 5 billion minutes of opt-in, anonymised calls, and owns its A100 hardware. It still uses OpenAI and Google's Bison for some features.
- **Latency, capacity, cost.** His ranking for AI features: latency first, because an agent-assist card that appears three seconds late is useless; capacity second; cost last, helped by about half a billion dollars in venture funding.
- **Most-loved features.** Real-time transcription, summaries that label call purpose and outcome, agent assist, and inferred customer satisfaction scored across every call rather than via surveys.
- **Personalisation.** Every customer gets a custom speech model for product names and acronyms; per-user models, for example to spell names correctly, are not yet solved.
- **Timelines.** Labenz cites Waymark's progress from unusable to most on-air voice being AI in under two years. O'Connell points to self-driving cars and the complexity of the last mile, doubts auto-drafted emails are worth charging for, and says people like to buy from people.
