## Thesis

Cecilia Ziniti, former general counsel at Replit and lead counsel on Amazon's Alexa team, walks Nathan Labenz through US intellectual property law and applies it to The New York Times' suit against OpenAI. She argues that verbatim regurgitation is the clearest infringement but not the core issue; the case matters because of training, where fair use is a squishy balancing test. She expects a middle ground built from court-imposed "reasonable measures", settlements and commercial licensing deals rather than destroyed models.

## Key points

- **Why IP exists.** Ziniti traces IP to Article 1 of the US Constitution, which grants authors and inventors limited monopolies. Patents are examined and granted through a formal process; copyright attaches the moment a work is fixed in a medium.
- **Patents as detente.** She says Google did patent the transformer but uses patents defensively. Suing OpenAI would look bad in the press, and Microsoft could countersue; she notes that Microsoft collects $10 per Android device under a patent deal.
- **Four-factor fair use.** Fair use weighs purpose and character (including whether the use is transformative), the nature of the work, the amount used and the market effect. Ziniti calls regurgitation the NYT's strongest claim because it makes ChatGPT a substitute, while OpenAI calls it a bug.
- **Remedies and damages.** Injunctions are rare when money can fix the harm. She cites Jamie Thomas, a Napster user who had to pay $400,000, and the NYT's formula of dividing a $100 billion valuation by its share of Common Crawl. OpenAI has promised to indemnify users.
- **Opt-outs and baked models.** OpenAI added a robots.txt opt-out in August, but Ziniti compares trained models to a cake: you cannot extract the flour. Labenz points to Anthropic's influence-function research as one possible basis for attribution.
- **What comes next.** Ziniti predicts "GPT yourself" licensing for creators, watermarking startups bought by big players, and Kindle-style deal-making. She dismisses Nightshade data poisoning as "kind of illegal". Her company GC AI runs on GPT-4 and is aimed at in-house counsel.
