## Thesis

In this rebroadcast of Dwarkesh Patel's interview, DeepMind co-founder and chief AGI scientist Shane Legg argues that large language models have changed something fundamental: we now have a scalable way to build systems with some understanding. He sees the remaining gaps, such as memory, factuality, search and video understanding, as research problems with relatively clear paths, not walls. Nathan Labenz, introducing the episode, says Legg's views match his own read of current model weaknesses and timelines.

## Key points

- **Measuring AGI.** Legg defines AGI as a machine that can do the cognitive things people typically do. It counts as reached when it passes a broad test suite and adversarial testers can no longer find tasks where it falls below human performance.
- **Missing episodic memory.** Current models have a fluid context window (like working memory) and slowly trained weights (like the cortex), but nothing like the hippocampus. Legg expects the fix to be architectural rather than a matter of scale.
- **Search for creativity.** He cites AlphaGo's Move 37 to argue that real creativity needs search through possibilities. Without it, models mainly mimic and blend human data.
- **Alignment as System 2.** Rather than containing powerful systems or relying only on RLHF, Legg wants models that reason step by step about options using a world model and a specified set of ethics, with humans auditing that reasoning. He names Geoffrey Irving's debate-style work as one promising direction.
- **Timelines.** Legg formed his view in 2001 after reading Ray Kurzweil's The Age of Spiritual Machines: exponential compute and data would reward scalable algorithms. In 2009 he gave 2025 as his mode and 2028 as his expected value, and he still holds 50% by 2028.
- **Next landmark.** He expects fully multimodal models, ones that digest video, to make today's text chat feel narrow. On DeepMind's net effect, he concedes it accelerated capabilities but says the counterfactual is hard to judge.
