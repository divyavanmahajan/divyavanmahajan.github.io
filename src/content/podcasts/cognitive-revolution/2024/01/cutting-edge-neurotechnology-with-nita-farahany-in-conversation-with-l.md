## Thesis

Duke law professor Nita Farahany, interviewed by 80,000 Hours host Luisa Rodriguez, argues that neurotechnology could become either the most empowering or the most oppressive technology society has adopted. Most of what she describes already exists, just not at scale yet, and generative AI is speeding up brain decoding. Her answer is a human right to cognitive liberty, covering mental privacy, freedom of thought and self-determination. Nathan Labenz introduces the episode as evidence that the future will look very different even if large language models stall.

## Key points

- **Military research.** Farahany describes research on EEG helmets that catch subconscious target recognition, swarms of drones steered by one brain, and a University of Washington Tetris-style experiment in which three-person teams passed brain-to-brain signals with above 80% accuracy.
- **Brain biometrics and interrogation.** She explains functional biometrics, such as thinking "Nita had a KIND bar for breakfast" to unlock a device. She also says EEG recognition tests have been used to win murder convictions in the UAE and have faced legal challenges in India.
- **Workplace surveillance.** SmartCap has sold EEG fatigue monitoring for over a decade to more than 5,000 companies in trucking, mining and aviation. Farahany says a CEO at Davos told her his firm had tracked over 1,000 employees' brain data and used it in hiring and firing decisions.
- **Mind reading with AI.** She cites an fMRI study that decoded whole paragraphs of imagined stories at 80-plus percent accuracy using GPT-1. She expects better language models to improve decoding further.
- **Big tech and implants.** Meta's acquisition of CTRL-labs, Apple's EEG-in-AirPods patent and Vision Pro eye tracking are her reasons to expect neural sensors in everyday devices. She sees Neuralink as therapeutic for now, because the FDA will not approve devices sold as enhancement.
- **Governance.** Farahany points to Chile's constitutional neurorights, UNESCO and the 2019 OECD principles. She says the US lags, and she wants business models that keep brain data on the device.
