## Thesis

Max Bennett, Blue Core cofounder and author of A Brief History of Intelligence, combines evolutionary neuroscience, comparative psychology and AI to describe five breakthroughs: steering, reinforcing, simulating, mentalizing and speaking. He presents this as a first approximation, not a blueprint for AI. He argues that AI has already replicated some of these steps, but that mammal brains still do something current models cannot: learn continuously without forgetting.

## Key points

- **Steering.** Bennett describes early bilaterians, such as nematodes with 302 neurons, that sort stimuli into good and bad and steer accordingly. Their associative learning works only within a window of about 2 seconds.
- **Reinforcing.** Thorndike's puzzle boxes and Minsky's SNARC showed that reinforcing only recent actions fails because of the temporal credit assignment problem. Bennett says Richard Sutton's temporal difference learning, first applied in TD-Gammon, solved it, and that dopamine bursts track changes in predicted reward in the same way. He adds that the fish brain gets you "70% of the way" to the human brain.
- **Simulating.** Around 150 million years ago small mammals evolved a six-layer neocortex. Bennett links it to planning and suggests that some measure of uncertainty decides when an animal stops to think.
- **Continual learning.** Bennett says his own fine-tuning experiments overfit and lose earlier abilities. Brains may update weights only when surprised and reject information until it fits their world model. Labenz offers Mamba's selective state space models as a partial parallel.
- **Mentalizing.** Primates show theory of mind, imitation learning and anticipation of future needs; in one study squirrel monkeys chose to avoid future thirst and rats did not. Bennett attributes all three to granular prefrontal cortex modelling the brain's own simulation.
- **Implications for AI.** Bennett expects backprop to survive, possibly with sleep-like replay, and predicts robots will run several specialised subsystems rather than one model. He doubts a single general AGI principle exists.
