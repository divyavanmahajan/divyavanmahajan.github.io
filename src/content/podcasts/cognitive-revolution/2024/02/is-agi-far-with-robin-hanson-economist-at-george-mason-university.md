## Thesis

Economist Robin Hanson, author of The Age of Em, takes the outside view: AI researchers have repeatedly believed a new paradigm was nearly the end of the road, and they were wrong each time. He expects human-level AI eventually, plausibly within a thousand years, but not imminently, and he judges progress by jobs and wages rather than demos or benchmarks. Nathan Labenz pushes back, arguing that LLMs have crossed qualitative thresholds such as understanding plain language.

## Key points

- **The dream time.** Hanson says today's rapid growth, wealth and global integration cannot last on cosmological timescales; eventually the world returns to slower change and a more Malthusian, competitive state.
- **Benchmarks mislead.** Labenz cites MMLU scores of high 80s to 90% and a Google DeepMind study where an LLM diagnosed with 60% accuracy against 30% for doctors. Hanson answers that 1960s researchers thought chess required general intelligence, and that 1980s expert systems already matched doctors but were blocked by medical licensing.
- **Steady automation.** In his study of about 900 jobs, Hanson finds automation moved roughly a third of a standard deviation from 1999 to 2019. He models task difficulty as log-normal, so exponential computing growth yields only linear progress; at that pace, AI may not offset population decline and innovation could stall for centuries.
- **Programmer wages as a test.** Hanson argues that if LLMs really doubled programmer productivity, highly elastic demand should push programmer wages up; his son's employer saw no benefit yet. Labenz, citing Replit, expects many more programmers but not higher wages.
- **Rot and merging.** Hanson says large systems rot like legacy software and brains, which is why GPT-4 was trained from scratch, and why no single super AI is likely to last forever.
- **What would change his mind.** Hanson wants visible displacement like tractors replacing farmers or trucks replacing horses, with clear shifts in workers, wages and firms.
