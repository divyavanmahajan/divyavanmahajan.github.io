## Thesis

Nathan Calvin of the Center for AI Safety Action Fund, one of the bill's three co-sponsors, argues that SB 1047 asks frontier developers to do what responsible labs already do: test for catastrophic capabilities and apply reasonable safeguards. Dean Ball of the Mercatus Center argues that regulating at the model layer is premature without real technical standards, and that over time the bill will erode open-source AI. Host Nathan Labenz looks for amendments both sides could accept.

## Key points

- **What the bill covers.** Labenz summarises the harm standard as mass casualties or $500 million or more in damage to critical infrastructure. Models trained with 10^26 FLOPs, or expected to be equally capable, are covered unless the developer can show they are safe. He estimates Meta's Llama 3 400B at around 4x10^25 FLOPs and roughly $200 million in compute.
- **Regulator or liability regime?** Calvin says the new Frontier Model Division has no licensing or rule-making power, and that enforcement runs through the attorney general. Ball replies that the division can adjust thresholds and is funded by fees on the companies it oversees, much like the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, at a time when California has a $60 billion budget deficit.
- **Critical infrastructure is broad.** Ball says the term covers hospitals, financial services and telecoms, most of US GDP. By his estimate, knocking a wastewater plant offline for a few days could reach $500 million in damage.
- **Who shaped it.** Calvin says Senator Scott Wiener's office talked with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and Microsoft, but rejects claims that the labs wrote the bill. Ball accepts that, but argues the effect still favours closed labs over Meta and startups.
- **Proposed fixes.** Labenz suggests a clearer line between derivative and new models, a sunset after about 36 months, and independent third-party testing before deployment. Ball backs a safe harbour for independent researchers and supports the whistleblower provisions. Calvin worries that accredited testers could become checklist firms such as Deloitte.
