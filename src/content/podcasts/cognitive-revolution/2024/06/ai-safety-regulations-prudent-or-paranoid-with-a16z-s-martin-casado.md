## Thesis

Nathan Labenz and a16z general partner Martin Casado debate how far AI capabilities will go before turning to regulation. Casado argues that the universe is heavy-tailed and nonlinear, so models learn the distributions they are given and stall on rare cases. He concludes AI is ordinary software and should be governed by existing software rules, not compute limits. Labenz is more open to models that outperform humans and to some testing before release.

## Key points

- **Self-driving as precedent.** Casado recalls Sebastian Thrun winning the DARPA Grand Challenge while he was at Stanford around 2003. Close to $100 billion later, he says, self-driving unit economics are still 3 times worse than Uber; progress comes from changing the environment, not more general AI.
- **Heavy tails.** His chatbot example: a help-desk bot that answers 90% of tickets may mostly handle password resets, leaving humans with about half of the unique cases. He expects agents to reach about 80% and then stall the same way.
- **Distribution in, distribution out.** Casado describes transformers as kernel smoothing and points to Vishal Misra's work linking in-context learning to Bayesian learning. Labenz cites sparse autoencoder features and Evo, a biology sequence model, as signs of learned abstractions. Casado accepts this for narrow domains and calls it the next step in simulation, not general reasoning.
- **Regulate behaviour, not primitives.** Casado says no one can tell AI apart from software, and he rejects the 10^26 compute thresholds behind SB 1047. He compares open models to Nmap and Metasploit, where misusers, not tool builders, were held responsible. Labenz argues that providers of autonomous calling agents should make them refuse clearly criminal requests, and Casado agrees.
- **Sleeper agents and scams.** Casado says model backdoors are no different from provably undetectable binary backdoors, so app-store-style vetting applies. He describes a voice-clone scam in which callers asked his parents for $10,000.
- **Market structure.** Casado sees a "perverse economy of scale": leaders pay ever more for each gain while followers distil from them, as Nvidia, Databricks and open-source models have shown. He offers a $1 bet that in two years no lab will hold 80% of the market.
