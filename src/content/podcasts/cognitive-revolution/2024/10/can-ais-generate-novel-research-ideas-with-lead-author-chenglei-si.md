## Thesis

Stanford PhD student Chenglei Si walks Nathan Labenz through his paper "Can LLMs Generate Novel Research Ideas?". Expert reviewers, not told where each idea came from, rated AI-generated prompting-research ideas as more novel and exciting than ideas from PhD researchers. Si argues the result is robust, but says the real test is whether the ideas work once they are executed. Labenz sees the study as evidence that AI is nearing a tipping point in research automation.

## Key points

- **Study design.** Si describes paying human participants $300 (with extra prizes for the best ideas), generating ideas with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and rewriting every idea, human and AI alike, into the same academic style so reviewers could not tell the source. Building the agent took two to three months; the human study took about eight.
- **Overgeneration and ranking.** Of about 4,000 ideas generated per topic, only around 200 survived deduplication; Si puts the cost at a couple of dollars per usable idea. Pairwise comparisons ranked ideas better than 1-10 scores, though Si's manual re-ranking still found top ideas the AI ranker had missed.
- **Results hold under three tests.** AI ideas scored significantly higher on novelty and excitement whether the data was analysed per review, per idea or per reviewer, and were slightly weaker on feasibility. Si says 9 of the top 10 ideas by overall score came from the AI, and he found them less grounded in existing work.
- **Limits.** Both agree the AI may have used language that sounds novel, and that the human ideas were roughly average PhD level. Si's next step is to pay people a couple of thousand dollars each to implement both sets of ideas and measure which ones actually work.
- **Milestones.** Si's bar for a "Eureka moment" is an automated project that wins a best paper award. He expects AI-written papers to reach top venues such as ACL fairly soon, and hopes to see the bigger goal met within the three or four years before he graduates.
