## Thesis

Riley Goodside, staff prompt engineer at Scale AI, tells Nathan Labenz that as post-training has improved, the quirky prompt tricks of the GPT-3 era matter less, and prompt engineering now looks more like programming than poetry. His practical advice is to go from a prompt to k-shot examples to retrieval, and to fine-tune only at the end, decomposing the task and capturing explicit reasoning at every step. He also argues that most demand for the skill now sits inside the AI industry, where better prompts produce the training data.

## Key points

- **Order of escalation.** Goodside starts with an instruction prompt, adds k-shot examples until they no longer fit, then works on example selection and RAG. Fine-tuning comes last, and the earlier work is not wasted because expensive prompts produce high-quality tuning data.
- **Reasoning traces matter.** Labenz and Goodside agree that inputs and outputs alone teach too little. Goodside says rationales generated by the model itself and filtered for correct or consensus answers suit that model better than human-written ones.
- **Costs of fine-tuning.** He cites research showing that tuning on new facts raises hallucination in general. He suggests mixing in general chat data, mixing weights, or tuning a base model and applying the weight delta to the instruct model.
- **Generate, then rank.** Goodside recommends pairwise comparisons turned into Elo scores rather than absolute scores, plus domain checks such as unit tests or back-translation. He cites a finding that about 40 GPT-3.5 calls match one GPT-4 call.
- **Tasks, not jobs.** LLMs suit repeatable tasks whose inputs and outputs fit in a spreadsheet. Goodside says they are weak at work that has to be current and competitive, with humour as the clearest example.
- **Safety and open weights.** He points to Anthropic's many-shot jailbreak paper and argues that some defences belong in filters rather than in the weights. Once weights are released, he says there is no free lunch.
