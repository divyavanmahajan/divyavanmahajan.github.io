## Thesis

Michael Boyce, director of the Department of Homeland Security's AI Corps, argues that the main barriers to AI in government are organisational, not technical: building trust, budgets, security plumbing and relationships across a 260,000-person department. He presents the AI Corps as a delivery-focused internal team that partners with DHS agencies, and pitches government service to AI professionals. Host Nathan Labenz probes how decisions get made and where commercial lessons apply.

## Key points

- **Structure of the AI Corps.** Boyce describes three parts: a roaming consultancy of two-to-five-person teams embedded with the 22 agencies, roughly a fifth of staff on scaling adoption (policy, governance, shared infrastructure), and an incubator that owns its own products.
- **Internal chatbot as plumbing work.** Boyce says the hard part of the department-wide LLM chatbot is logging, cybersecurity monitoring and FedRAMP-authorised cloud hosting, so staff can use non-public and personal data safely. Labenz encourages companies to trust enterprise providers rather than self-host.
- **Asylum interview role-play.** One pilot has an LLM play an asylum seeker for officer training. Boyce says it was mostly careful prompting to stop the model citing the Immigration and Nationality Act, and evaluation was largely qualitative.
- **RAG and quality checks.** Boyce describes an investigator search tool combining vector search, lexical search and Llama 3 70B relevance judging, and sees LLMs as a "second pair of eyes" on hand-keyed reports.
- **Metrics and limits.** Boyce is sceptical of inflated efficiency claims, notes AI may not make final benefit decisions at DHS, and cites non-generative wins such as border truck anomaly detection and FEMA aerial-image triage.
- **Hiring pitch.** Boyce reports over 14,000 applicants for 50 roles, 33 on board, and salaries roughly from $150K up to about $230K, recruiting via dhs.gov/ai/join.
