## Thesis

New York Assembly Member Alex Bores, a former Palantir data scientist, argues that his RAISE Act sets a minimal safety floor for frontier AI. It mostly turns commitments the labs already made under the Biden administration into law. Nathan Labenz tests the definitions and enforcement. Bores' position is that companies write and grade their own safety plans, and the law makes sure quarterly profit pressure does not push them to skip their own tests.

## Key points

- **Four requirements.** Bores lists them: a safety plan, a third-party audit of that plan, disclosure of critical safety incidents, and no retaliation against whistleblowers. Critical harm means 100 deaths or $1 billion in damage from CBRN weapons or from crimes committed with limited human intervention.
- **Who is covered.** A frontier model crosses the 10^26 threshold with $100 million spent on training, or is distilled from such a model for at least $5 million. A large developer has spent $100 million in total on frontier models. Bores estimates that is single digits of companies. Academic research is exempt. The distillation clause is aimed at future DeepSeek-like models sold in New York.
- **Open source and internal use.** Bores says the bill is "agnostic" on open versus closed release; companies judge their own risk. Internal use counts as deployment, apart from testing and evaluation.
- **Enforcement limits.** Labenz notes the maximum fine for a repeat offence is $30 million. Bores admits the whistleblower penalty of $10,000 per retaliation is weak, but says it is New York's standard figure.
- **Built through compromise.** Bores sent drafts to five or six major labs twice and says he incorporated roughly 90% of industry feedback. He would welcome federal action and says about six states are coordinating on a group text.
- **Process.** The session ends 17 June. New York's chapter-amendment process lets the governor negotiate changes before signing, so the bill can be adjusted later.
