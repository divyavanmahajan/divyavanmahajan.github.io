## Thesis

Gabriel Weil, law professor at Touro University, argues that AI risk is largely an externality problem: developers impose risks on people who never chose them. Because those risks are hard to measure in advance but relatively easy to attribute afterwards, he sees liability law, rather than prescriptive regulation or a risk tax, as the right tool. Nathan Labenz tests the idea against concrete scenarios.

## Key points

- **Liability 101.** Weil explains negligence (duty, breach, causation, injury), products liability and the "abnormally dangerous activities" doctrine. He notes that courts usually treat software as a service, not a product, and that breach inquiries tend to be narrow.
- **Alignment failures vs. misuse.** Weil wants strict liability when a system does something that would be a tort for a human and the user neither intended nor could foresee it, such as an agent that runs a phishing scheme to "start a profitable Internet business". For misuse he wants a risk-utility balancing test, not automatic liability.
- **Second-party harms differ.** On the Character AI suicide case, Weil says harms to users are subject to market feedback and contract, so they are not the core problem he is trying to solve.
- **Punitive damages for near misses.** Weil proposes that a near miss for an uninsurable catastrophe should carry punitive damages. With a maximum insurable risk of 1 trillion dollars and a 10 trillion dollar harm, warning shots must be 10 times more likely than catastrophes for this to work.
- **State bills.** Weil worked with Alex Boris in New York and Victoria Goo in Rhode Island on bills that leave the buck with developers even when they used reasonable care. They carve out misuse and malicious modification.
- **Compared with private governance.** Weil objects to SB 813's liability shield extending to third parties, warning that lax certification invites a race to the bottom. He sees insurers' underwriting as a more attractive quasi-regulatory channel.
