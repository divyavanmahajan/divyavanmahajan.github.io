## Thesis

Gillian Hadfield (Johns Hopkins) and Andrew Freedman (Fathom) argue that neither top-down prescriptive rules nor laissez-faire will work for a general purpose technology that moves as fast as AI. Their "regulatory markets" idea splits the job: democratic institutions decide which outcomes are acceptable, and licensed private regulators compete to develop and verify the technical standards, under strong government oversight. California's SB 813 is the first attempt to write this into law.

## Key points

- **How the mechanism works.** Freedman explains that under SB 813, developers or deployers can get certified by approved third-party verifiers; certification would count in court as evidence of meeting a duty of care, a rebuttable presumption rather than a full liability shield.
- **Why top-down fails.** Hadfield, who traces the idea to a 2019 paper with Jack, says legislatures and agencies run on a different time scale from the technology. Freedman draws on his time as Colorado's cannabis czar: iterative rules worked, and nobody can predict AI guardrails that still make sense six months later.
- **Not the credit rating agencies.** Hadfield says rating agencies failed because government created demand for them while immunizing them and providing no oversight. Here government's role shifts to overseeing the private regulators. Freedman adds that they must not be funded by the labs and must be able to deny certification and survive.
- **Precedents.** Freedman points to Underwriters Laboratories, later codified into law; Hadfield cites ISO standards and a roughly five-country consortium for medical device quality.
- **Red-teaming the proposal.** Labenz raises reliance on the California attorney general, startups priced out (a concern from a16z's Matt Perrault), and mandatory insurance instead. Freedman would add a commission and a lighter lane for smaller players; both guests say insurers cannot price AI risk without standards to build on, citing Armilla and Lloyd's as a complementary model.
- **Carve-outs for catastrophe.** Freedman would amend SB 813 so harms large enough to be catastrophic fall outside the framework, and argues the overall goal is fewer people harmed.
