## Thesis

Nathan Labenz and co-host Prakash run a year-end live show of short interviews. Zvi Moshowitz argues that AI is close to leaving "normal technology" territory and that much public denial is motivated, while the other guests map what is still missing: sample-efficient learning (ARC Prize), layered memory (Ali Behrouz), and judgment on messy real-world tasks (Elicit).

## Key points

- **Denial and the live players.** Zvi says coding uplift is already two to three times for top AI people and 10 to 100 times for amateurs like him. He ranks OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind as the labs that matter, says Anthropic keeps impressing him, and argues continual learning is largely a prompt-engineering "skill issue" with Opus 4.5.
- **ARC-AGI.** Greg of the ARC Prize says GPT-5.2 scored 90% on ARC-AGI 1, about 390 times cheaper per task than OpenAI's 87% result in December 2024. Small recursive models (HRM, TRM) won paper prizes. ARC-AGI 3, due March 2026, will have about 150 turn-based games scored against human action efficiency; frontier models score under 1% in early tests.
- **Companions and personal apps.** Eugenia Kuyda (Replika founder, now Wabi) proposes human flourishing instead of engagement as the industry metric, criticising ChatGPT's follow-up prompts. Wabi's zero-shot app success rate rose from 10-15% in April to about 80% with Gemini 3 and Opus 4.5. She advises against AI products for young children.
- **Nested learning.** Ali Behrouz (Cornell, Google) presents nested learning as a paradigm with a spectrum of memories updated at different frequencies, while warning that user-to-company knowledge transfer raises privacy issues.
- **Gemini 3 Flash.** Logan Kirkpatrick says Flash beats Pro on several benchmarks at lower cost, and highlights the File Search (managed RAG) and Interactions APIs. He advises developers to target specific markets rather than generic agent builders.
- **AI for science.** Jungwon Hwang of Elicit notes GDPval omits scientific research, and that frontier models score about 70% on Olympiad questions but 25-30% on research subtasks in OpenAI's Frontier Science benchmark. For pharma, she argues the bottleneck is reaching conviction on targets, not generating more ideas.
