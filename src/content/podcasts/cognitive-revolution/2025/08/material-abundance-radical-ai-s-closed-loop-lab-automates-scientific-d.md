## Thesis

Joseph Krause and Jorge Colindres, co-founders of Radical AI, argue that new materials are slow and expensive mainly because experimental data is scarce, unstructured and lost, especially negative results. Their "materials flywheel" pairs a property-driven AI engine with a largely autonomous lab, so every experiment produces clean, labelled data that feeds the next round. Nathan Labenz probes how far the models can go beyond their training distribution.

## Key points

- **The problem.** The founders cite three barriers: cost (over $100,000,000 per new material), time (often 10+ years to commercialisation) and fragmentation between academic research and incremental corporate R&D. Scale-up, from a quarter-sized "button" to a 400-pound piece, is where many materials die.
- **Throughput.** Krause, formerly at the Army Research Lab, says the lab targets 100 experiments per day, against roughly 50 a year for him as a bench scientist. Each run uses active learning to update the next.
- **Customer-first discovery.** Colindres uses the story of Corning's Chemcore, shelved for about half a century until Steve Jobs wanted a phone screen, becoming Gorilla Glass, to argue that materials must be designed against customer-specified properties.
- **Model stack.** Colindres says they use GNN-based MLIPs, generative models, computer vision and language models including the GPT family, but bets most on language models for scientific reasoning. He expects to use a natively multimodal model internally within 6 months.
- **Search space and the lab as eval.** Krause describes high entropy alloys with 5 or 6 elements from about 60 usable ones as a grain of sand on an Earth-sized beach. Colindres calls the physical lab the best possible benchmark, and the founders say models must be built to seek novelty and surprise.
- **Business and defence.** Krause rejects selling software or licensing in favour of selling materials at scale, like 3M, Dow or BASF. Radical AI is working with the US Air Force on high entropy alloys for hypersonics, and Krause expects a materials "move 37" within 12 to 24 months.
