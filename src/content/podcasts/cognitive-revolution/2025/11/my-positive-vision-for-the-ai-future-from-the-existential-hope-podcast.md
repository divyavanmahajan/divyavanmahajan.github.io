## Thesis

In this crossover from Beatrice Erkers' Existential Hope podcast, Nathan Labenz is the one interviewed. He argues that today's AI could already automate most cognitive work once the plumbing is built, and that the upside (safer transport, universal tutoring, cheap expert advice, faster medicine) is largely on track if the downside is avoided. His preferred path is Eric Drexler's "comprehensive AI services": many superhuman but narrow systems rather than one general superintelligence.

## Key points

- **Self-driving as the mundane win.** Labenz says he got bored in a Waymo within minutes, notes Waymo charges more than Uber in the Bay Area, and imagines sleeper cars; he links wide adoption to about 30,000 fewer US road deaths.
- **Second opinions for everything.** He runs contracts through three or four AIs and merges their output, and expects AI to cut search costs in hiring, sales, dating and family weekend plans: hard to find, easy to verify.
- **Fast feedback loops go first.** Software leads because outputs are easy to check, he argues, citing Replit's agent testing its own apps in a browser. Medicine is slower: he mentions MIT's AI-assisted antibiotic discovery but notes clinical trials remain a bottleneck. Alpha School is his education example, with two hours of AI-delivered academics a day.
- **Safety through narrowness.** Labenz says anything in pure form is dangerous and stable systems are buffered. He is uneasy that frontier labs aim to automate AI research, noting OpenAI reported o3 handling 40% of internal pull requests, versus 0-5% before.
- **Insurance and regulation.** Erkers says Foresight's world-building found insurance and liability the likeliest route to tool-like AI; Labenz has made a small investment in an AI underwriting company and favors mandated insurance.
- **Raise expectations.** He wants politicians to promise concrete improvements such as cheap energy and self-driving, and fiction with branching endings, like AI 2027, to show the future is a collective choice.
