## Thesis

DoorDash co-founders Andy Fang and Stanley Tang tell Sarah Guo that DoorDash has treated autonomy as a core bet since 2018, and that the advantage in physical-world AI comes from working back from a specific use case and from real operational data, not from building general technology first. They argue the same logic applies to Ask DoorDash, its natural-language ordering interface.

## Key points

- **Ask DoorDash changes behaviour.** Fang says about 50% of restaurant sessions through Ask DoorDash end in an order from a place the user has never ordered from, and grocery baskets are about 40% larger. Voice was the original bet and did not land; conversational text did.
- **Use case first, not tech first.** Tang says DoorDash spent years partnering with sidewalk-robot and robotaxi companies. Neither fit: the average delivery is 3-5 miles, too far for a 2 mph robot, and a 4,000 lb car is overkill for a few burritos.
- **Dot.** The in-house robot weighs 300 lb, travels up to 20 mph, is one-tenth the size of a car and uses roads, bike lanes and sidewalks. It has delivered in Phoenix for almost two years and went fully autonomous (L4) last year.
- **The last 100 feet.** Tang argues the hard parts are pickup and drop-off; DoorDash knows from 10 billion past deliveries where human Dashers actually left orders, data that is not in Google Maps. Scaling exposed problems such as a boot script taking 30-45 minutes per robot and wheels on leaves needing different torque.
- **Constraints shift to hardware and operations.** Autonomy is becoming less of a blocker than operations, manufacturing and supply chain, so DoorDash partnered with Also, the Rivian micromobility spin-out.
- **AI spend and productivity.** The founders say internal AI spend in June was about 20x January's before flattening out. Their Dashbench benchmark measures models on coding tasks, and they want to route cheaper work to open-weight models. Tang predicts more Dashers in ten years, not fewer, because the business grows 25% a year.
