## Thesis

Recorded at Valar Atomics' reactor site in Utah, founder Isaiah Taylor tells Sarah Guo that nuclear never had its "Ford moment" because the industry became a modelling-and-simulation business. Valar's bet is to build simple, inherently safe reactors and iterate on real hardware, measured by "tick rate", the time between successive reactors splitting atoms.

## Key points

- **Why the US stopped.** Taylor blames Three Mile Island, where nobody died, for public fear, and says the US meanwhile lost its civil-infrastructure skills while getting better at advanced manufacturing. So nuclear must become manufactured, not constructed.
- **The DOE pathway.** The Department of Energy began as ERDA, a testing agency split off from the Atomic Energy Commission. Executive order EO 14301, calling for three advanced reactors to go critical by July 4th, let Valar test under DOE authority instead of the NRC.
- **Safety by consequence, not odds.** Valar's helium-cooled, graphite-moderated design with TRISO fuel removes decay heat passively. Taylor plans to scram the Ward 250 reactor and switch off every safety system, as already done with electrical heaters in Hawthorne. Its safety case assumes everything in the plant has failed.
- **Tick rate.** From incorporation to first split atom took 2 years and 4 months; the second reactor came about 7 months later. Ward 250, which just made power, is the first advanced reactor built by a startup to do so.
- **Verticalise what blocks scale.** Valar designed its own rebar-free concrete after a three-week "rock hunt" and stacked its precast bioshield in about 42 hours instead of three months. Quoted $5 million and two and a half years for a reactor protection system, a team of six built one in six weeks for about $400,000.
- **Financing and demand.** Taylor says US venture capital is best placed to underwrite execution risk; project finance becomes possible after about five reactors. Valar plans its own gigasite and expects data-centre load to follow. It powered an NVIDIA Blackwell chip directly from the reactor, and Taylor argues cheaper energy creates its own demand.
