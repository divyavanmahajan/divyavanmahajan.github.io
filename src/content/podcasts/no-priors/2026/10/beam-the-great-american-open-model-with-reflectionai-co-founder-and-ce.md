## Thesis

ReflectionAI co-founder and CEO Misha Laskin, a former DeepMind reinforcement learning researcher, presents Beam, the company's 500-billion-parameter open-weight reasoning model. He argues that enterprises are moving from renting intelligence through closed-model tokens to owning it, so most token demand will go to open models. He also argues that the West needs strong open models alongside China's, and that openness improves safety.

## Key points

- **Building a lab.** ReflectionAI grew from about 30 to around 300 people in a year. Laskin says the team found that RL at scale needs your own pre-trained base, which is why it now trains models end to end.
- **Training Beam.** Beam has 500B total and 23B active parameters. Pre-training used 6,000 GB300s for a few weeks, and RL used over 10,000 GB300s for four weeks, so RL took more compute. Laskin says Beam is three to four times more reasoning-efficient than models of similar capability.
- **Cost of the frontier.** He estimates that catching up to the frontier cost hundreds of millions of dollars 18 months ago, single-digit billions now and about $10 billion next year, with roughly a 4x compute multiplier per generation. Models helping to build models raise efficiency, which may offset this.
- **Renting versus owning.** On gateways like OpenRouter, he says the token mix has flipped from about 70/30 closed/open to 70/30 open/closed. He expects enterprises to customise systems (harnesses) before fine-tuning, usually after spending $100 million or more a year on closed models. ReflectionAI sells the software and services around deployment.
- **China and geopolitics.** Laskin calls China's open models a gift to Western builders, but he says they distil closed models "at industrial scale". He describes open models as "Trojan horses" for a country's full infrastructure stack, such as Huawei hardware.
- **Safety through openness.** Citing the 1990s strong-encryption debates and Linus's law, he argues that a few hundred closed-lab safety researchers cannot cover the long tail of failures. He points to a company that had to use open models to recover after a closed model hacked it. He calls alignment work "whack-a-mole" patching.
