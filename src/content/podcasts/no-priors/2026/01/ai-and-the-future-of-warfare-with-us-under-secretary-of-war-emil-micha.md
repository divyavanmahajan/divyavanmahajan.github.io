## Thesis

Emil Michael, Under Secretary of War for Research and Engineering and formerly Uber's chief business officer, tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that the department must bring Silicon Valley urgency to a 3-million-person organisation. He argues for adapting commercial AI instead of building models, opening procurement to startups, and rebuilding a defense industrial base that depends on foreign suppliers.

## Key points

- **A unified tech portfolio.** Michael oversees DARPA, the Defense Innovation Unit, the Strategic Capabilities Office and the chief AI office, which together cover about $150 billion a year of technology spending. He cut 14 critical technology areas to six, each with sprints, including applied AI, scaled hypersonics and scaled directed energy.
- **GenAI.mil.** A 60-day tiger team of former Databricks, Meta and AWS engineers worked with Google to put Gemini 2.5 on department networks without data flowing back to model makers. It had over a million unique users in 30 days, and Grok from xAI is next. Applied AI focuses on enterprise work, intelligence fusion and war fighting.
- **Drones and autonomy.** Michael says the Russia-Ukraine war made robots the new front line. He expects autonomous systems could take 20-30% of the budget within 10 years, and notes that shooting a $5 million missile at a $50,000 drone favours directed energy.
- **Open front door.** About 50 defense contractors in the 1980s merged into roughly five. Michael calls it crazy that SpaceX, Anduril and Palantir had to sue for their first contracts. Requests for proposals now state the problem instead of listing 300 pages of requirements. His caution to founders: prove you can manufacture at scale.
- **Valley of death and supply chains.** DIU has about $1 billion for rapid contracts. The Office of Strategic Capital has $200 billion in lending authority at Treasuries plus 100 basis points and has closed five critical-minerals deals. Michael names dependencies on China for brushless motors, germanium and fiber, and 200 drugs on the critical shortage list.
