## Thesis

Rippling co-founder and CEO Parker Conrad tells Sarah Guo that the conventional advice to focus narrowly is wrong: the most durable software companies, from Oracle and SAP to Salesforce and Microsoft, are platforms whose shared capabilities make every application better. He also argues that AI will not make companies employment-conserving, because cheaper software raises customer expectations and pushes the competitive frontier outward.

## Key points

- **Failure is overrated.** Conrad says Zenefits failed "for dumb reasons", that people learn more from success than failure, and that his advice to would-be founders is usually "don't do it". The lessons he kept were strict regulatory compliance and an aversion to leaning on ops.
- **Platform math.** Permissions, reports, analytics, workflow automations and approvals recur across business software. Conrad says every dollar of R&D on Rippling's underlying platform "pays off 35x", which is why application teams should not be allowed to disconnect from it.
- **Ownership and "A and B".** He looks for leaders who close the gap between plan and market reality, and he rejects "CEO in the box" A-or-B choices by asking why both cannot coexist. Guo recalls former employees saying he got more out of them than they knew they could give.
- **Organising engineering.** Over 80% of an engineering org of over a thousand people works on existing products; new products run with five to seven engineers each, such as a variable compensation product.
- **AI and headcount.** Conrad has not seen large efficiencies from coding assistants and expects demand for software and AI support to rise. He predicts verticalised versions such as "Rippling for ophthalmology clinics", and argues AI agents must inherit the permissions of the person they work for.
- **Staying private.** Private-market liquidity, Databricks versus Snowflake, and a lack of high-growth public comps keep Rippling private for now, a choice he revisits every year.
