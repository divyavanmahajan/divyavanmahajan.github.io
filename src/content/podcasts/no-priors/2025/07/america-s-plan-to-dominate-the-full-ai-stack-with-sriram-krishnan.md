## Thesis

Sriram Krishnan, senior White House policy adviser on AI and a lead author of the AI Action Plan, argues that the US holds only a small lead over China and that whoever pulls ahead will gain compounding economic, military and cultural advantages. His plan rests on three pillars: build infrastructure, speed innovation by cutting red tape and supporting open source, and get allies to standardise on the American stack.

## Key points

- **DeepSeek as the starting gun.** Krishnan says DeepSeek, released the weekend before he started, showed the US lead was very small. He thinks its cost claims were inflated by the press but credits its technical work, and still rates DeepSeek and Qwen ("Quinn" in the captions) as the best open source models.
- **Measuring the race.** He proposes tracking what share of the world's inferenced tokens run on American hardware and models, treating the US as "America Inc." with a stack from Nvidia and AMD GPUs through models to applications.
- **Culture and "no woke AI".** After Gil raises models as cultural exports, Krishnan describes an executive order requiring federally procured models to be truth-seeking and free of undisclosed ideological bias, drawing on his time at Twitter seeing how algorithms shape culture.
- **Build and deregulate.** The plan targets easier permitting for data centers on federal land, federal rather than state-level AI rules (citing California's SB 1047), and open source. Krishnan says US power usage grew only 1 to 2% for a long period, leaving a grid and regulatory "spaghetti mess" unprepared for demand.
- **Exporting the stack.** He says the Biden diffusion rule, a 200-page document, made it hard to ship GPUs even to allies; the new approach, including partnerships in the Gulf, aims to get US GPUs running US models abroad, with robotics a focus in the next 18 to 24 months.
- **Open source and safety.** Gil argues that restricting open source centralises power and resembles regulatory capture by closed-model companies. Krishnan cites Linus's law: more scrutiny from thousands of researchers finds issues better than a small lab safety team.
