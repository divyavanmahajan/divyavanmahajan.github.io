## Thesis

Ryan Noon, co-founder of Material Security, tells Elad Gil that LLMs are a step function in cyber security: whoever adopts them first gains the edge, the same way iron weapons beat bronze. He argues defenders should start with off-the-shelf models rather than fine-tuning to fake a moat, and that the security market's structure still leaves room for startups.

## Key points

- **Origin of Material.** Noon started the company around 2016-2017 after the election-year hacks of Gmail accounts, including the Podesta emails. The product finds sensitive content in Google Workspace and Office 365 mailboxes, redacts it and gates access behind an extra check, then added anti-phishing and account takeover protection on a "defense in depth" principle.
- **Early LLM adoption.** Material used LLMs to filter noisy signals (such as raw email headers) before a human analyst reviews them. Noon says even GPT-3.5 left plenty of headroom and that much fine-tuning in the market is "shovel selling".
- **The threat.** Noon sees text and voice impersonation at scale as the main risk: one attacker "with a for loop" can now do the work of a room full of scammers, and even spell-checking phishing mails changes returns.
- **Security as cleanup crew.** He frames cyber security as the "waste heat" of innovation: utility drives adoption, not safety. Open societies are more exposed than authoritarian states that can lock systems down.
- **Market structure.** Citing Ian Grigg's "The Market for Silver Bullets", Noon argues buyers cannot judge product effectiveness, so incumbents such as Cisco and Palo Alto Networks act as distribution machines that buy startups (Cisco bought Splunk). LLMs could absorb much of the log-parsing spend.
- **Founder advice.** Pick co-founders carefully (a solo founder beats a hasty marriage), choose a big market because market size sets the game's difficulty, ignore Gartner categories, and include a technologist who learns a market beyond developer tools.
