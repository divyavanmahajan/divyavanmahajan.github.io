## Thesis

Kawal Gandhi, lead for generative AI in the Office of the CTO at Google Cloud, tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that enterprises adopt AI in stages: efficiency first, then productivity, then creativity, with trust in the models growing at each step. In his view, success depends less on the technology than on an organisation's maturity, ambition and handling of data security.

## Key points

- **Dogfooding first.** Gandhi says Google tested generative features internally in Workspace (summarising, personalising and suggesting text in documents and email) before launching them externally as Duet AI. Slides image generation and email drafting saw strong uptake.
- **Model Garden and choice.** Google Cloud offers its own foundation models, domain-specific models such as Med-PaLM and Sec-PaLM, and open-source models like Llama and Stable Diffusion. Gandhi stresses that customer data, adapter models and weights stay in the customer's tenant.
- **Prototype excitement versus production.** Gil cites Ankur Goyal of Braintrust on companies jumping straight to fine-tuning. Gandhi says an API prototype creates excitement, but deploying, monitoring and chaining models is where serious thinking starts, and whether to train your own model depends on the organisation's maturity.
- **Where adoption shows up.** Gandhi sees early demand in sales and marketing content, customer care and internal employee experiences such as HR benefits. Engineering adoption surprised him most. He says more than 10,000 people have been certified on GCP generative AI.
- **Silicon and inference.** On TPUs versus GPUs, Gandhi wants hardware abstracted away and treated as reasonably fungible. He says Google is now focused on scaling inference, citing Character AI as an example, and that the NVIDIA GPU shortage tends to give way to questions about data access and deployment.
- **Multimodal and data.** Gandhi calls multimodal early, with gaming leading, and says Google is exploring shared datasets and synthetic data for regulated industries.
