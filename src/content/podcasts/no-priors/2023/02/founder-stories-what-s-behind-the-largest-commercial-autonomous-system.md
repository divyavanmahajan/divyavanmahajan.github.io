## Thesis

Keller Rinaudo Cliffton, co-founder and CEO of Zipline, tells Sarah Guo that Zipline survived by being the most practical company in drone delivery, not the best funded. In his account, putting a simple product in front of paying customers early revealed that the aircraft is only a small part of the problem. He also argues that hardware and infrastructure companies are hard to build but highly defensible.

## Key points

- **Origins.** Rinaudo Cliffton moved from consumer robots (a $150,000 Kickstarter) to logistics, inspired by Kiva's warehouse robots. Rwanda's health minister told the team in 2016 to focus on blood only, starting with a contract for 21 hospitals.
- **Near-death first year.** With about 20 people, Zipline served one hospital for nine months instead of two weeks. He says the drone is about 15% of the complexity; data logging, pre-flight checks, GPS lock and maintenance (43 fastener types cut to two) were the real problems, while redundant flight computers proved unnecessary.
- **Tesla path, not moonshot.** He compares Zipline to Tesla's ship-simple-first strategy and contrasts it with competitors that he says spent billions without serving customers.
- **Acoustic detect-and-avoid.** Since drones have no right of way and transponder compliance is low, Zipline built a microphone array with neural nets that, he says, identifies an aircraft's make and model and its position within a "two mile hockey puck". It is cheap, light and covers 360 degrees.
- **Scale and business.** Zipline serves about 3,000 facilities from over 20 distribution centres, has flown 50 million autonomous miles and signed a $61 million national deal with Rwanda. He says almost all distribution centres are gross-margin profitable and expects flight volume to rise 400% in 2023.
- **Advice.** He told early staff the odds of success were about 1% and argues that ambitious, unglamorous problems face less competition.
