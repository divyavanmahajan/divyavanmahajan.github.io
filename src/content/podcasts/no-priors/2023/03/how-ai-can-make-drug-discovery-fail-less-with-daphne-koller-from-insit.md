## Thesis

Daphne Koller, founder and CEO of Insitro and co-founder of Coursera, argues that the main cost driver in drug development is failure: 95% of drug programs fail, mostly because they go after the wrong target, indication or patient population. Insitro focuses on that early discovery step. It combines machine learning on human data with data it generates in its own lab, so it can rely less on animal models that don't translate to humans.

## Key points

- **Career path.** Koller ran a split ML/biology lab at Stanford and resigned an endowed chair to stay at Coursera. In 2016 she saw that machine learning had barely touched the life sciences. After 18 months at Calico she left to build a platform company.
- **Where to apply ML.** Koller divides drug development into target discovery, molecule design and clinical trials. She chose target discovery because most programs fail there, and fixing it pushes down the roughly $2.5 billion cost per successful drug.
- **Two data sources.** Koller describes two data sources: human genetics as an "experiment of nature", and genome-edited stem-cell models as an in-vitro A/B test. Insitro started in neuroscience, metabolism and oncology, where there is high unmet need and good human data such as brain MRIs.
- **Partnering and biomarkers.** Koller says a discovery engine lets Insitro partner on or license out programs, or reuse existing drugs, which can save two to five years. She cites research that drugs with a biomarker are about twice as likely to succeed, with Herceptin as her example of selecting the right patients.
- **Limits on speed.** Gil asks whether ML can compress timelines the way COVID vaccine development did. Koller says regulators raise fair questions about measuring biomarkers reliably, and that slow diseases like Alzheimer's cannot be rushed.
- **Bridging cultures.** Koller says engineers look for patterns while scientists look for outliers. Her favourite of Insitro's norms is that people engage with each other openly, constructively and with respect. She warns that tech newcomers who assume they will solve everything put biologists off.
