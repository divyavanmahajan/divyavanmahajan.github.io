## Thesis

Brett Adcock, founder and CEO of Figure AI, tells Sarah Guo that one general-purpose humanoid robot beats thousands of special-purpose machines, because the physical world was designed around the human body. He sees labour, about half of GDP, as the biggest market there is, and he plans to start in industrial work cells, build a data engine from a fleet of robots, and only then move into the home.

## Key points

- **Background and funding.** Adcock grew up on a farm in Illinois, then sold the software company Vettery in 2017 and founded the eVTOL maker Archer Aviation. Figure is 21 months old and has just raised $675 million from Microsoft, OpenAI, Nvidia, Intel and Jeff Bezos.
- **Why humanoid.** Adcock compares the human form to a keyboard and mouse for the physical world: a single hardware platform whose cost is spread over millions of tasks. In his view, a separate robot for each use case would need its own company, team and capital, which does not scale.
- **Why now.** He points to better battery energy density and motor torque density, to bipedal locomotion controllers (Figure had its robot walking less than a year after incorporating), to modern AI, and to speech as the default way to talk to a robot.
- **Team and vertical integration.** He spent the first year recruiting, making about 300 calls over six months to engineers he contacted by cold email. Figure prefers to buy parts, but with no mature supply chain it now builds nearly everything apart from the battery cells, GPU and CPU.
- **Partners.** BMW is Figure's first commercial customer. The first site is Spartanburg, South Carolina, which makes about 1,400 cars a day, and five use cases have already been chosen. OpenAI supplies the vision-language model for high-level reasoning, while Figure's own neural nets drive low-level control.
- **Acceptance and AGI.** Adcock says trust has to be earned through a safety record in factories before robots enter homes. He says a robot must be cheap for this to work, and he calls it dystopian for humans to end up as the actuators for an AGI.
