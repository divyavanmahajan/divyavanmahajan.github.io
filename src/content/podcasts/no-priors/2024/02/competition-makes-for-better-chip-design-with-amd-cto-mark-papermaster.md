## Thesis

Mark Papermaster, AMD's CTO, tells Sarah Guo and Elad Gil that the AI chip market lacked competition and that this hurt everyone. He argues AMD can now compete through holistic design, combining CPUs, GPUs and dedicated engines as chiplets, plus open software, and that AI inference will spread from hyperscale clouds to the edge and to PCs.

## Key points

- **Long road to AI.** Papermaster joined AMD in 2011 after IBM and Apple. AMD first rebuilt its CPUs with Zen in 2017, built on the ATI acquisition for GPUs, won supercomputer bids, and launched the MI300 on 6 December 2023, with public commitments from Meta and Microsoft.
- **MI300 positioning.** He says the MI300 is competitive on training, leads in inference with over 2X on FP16 measures, and carries more memory for better efficiency per rack.
- **Open software.** AMD uses LLVM and the open-source ROCm stack, is a founding member of the PyTorch Foundation, and works with Hugging Face so models are tested on Instinct GPUs. Guo, an investor in Lamini, notes customers want heterogeneous compute.
- **Inference moves outward.** Papermaster expects smaller, fine-tuned models to spread from hyperscalers to tier-2 data centres, factory floors and AI-enabled PCs, which AMD has shipped through 2023 and updated at CES.
- **Supply and power.** He says AMD handled pandemic shortages with TSMC; for AI the bottleneck is also advanced packaging of chiplets and high-bandwidth memory. Power will be the lasting constraint. He supports geographic diversity, citing TSMC fabs in Arizona and Samsung in Texas.
- **Moore's Law slowing.** New nodes still help but cost more, so gains come from matching engines to workloads, chiplets on the best node, packaging and software. On new devices like Vision Pro and Rabbit, he says success comes from creating a category people love, not incremental gains.
