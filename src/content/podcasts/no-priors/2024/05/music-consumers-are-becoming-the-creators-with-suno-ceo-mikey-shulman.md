## Thesis

Mikey Shulman, Suno's co-founder and CEO, says Suno is a consumer product meant for everyone, not a tool for producers. He argues that making music with AI is enjoyable in itself and that the line between creating and consuming music will eventually blur. He expects people to spend more time and money on music without losing their connection to human artists.

## Key points

- **From transcription to music.** Shulman, who has a physics PhD and has played piano since age four, did his first audio work transcribing earnings calls after his startup was acquired by S&P Global in 2018. The team released Bark as open source and chose music over speech, because speech "just needs to be right".
- **Transformers plus tokenization.** Shulman says Suno's models are "just transformers". Most of the team's innovation goes into tokenizing continuous audio sampled at about 50,000 samples per second. The model is given no music theory and learns everything through next-token prediction.
- **Aesthetics over benchmarks.** With no standard tests for audio, Shulman says "aesthetics matter": the team evaluates by listening and running A/B tests. He calls human emotion, which varies by culture and age, the hard target.
- **Business model.** Suno charges early (a free tier of 10 songs a day plus subscriptions), but Shulman suspects SaaS-style pricing in generative AI is a holdover from the previous era. A host compares this to the 1990s web settling on ads over micropayments.
- **Emergent behavior.** Shulman says users co-write lyrics, trade verses and put their names in song titles to show up on the trending page. The hosts and Shulman also create a "sitar trap" song live.
- **Future of music.** Shulman compares Suno to the DAW, which let people make music in their dorm rooms. He predicts faster cultural change, new song structures and an "untick tockify" shift toward longer listening.
