## Thesis

Rune Kvist, an early Anthropic go-to-market hire and co-founder of the AI Underwriting Company (AIUC), argues that the main constraint on AI adoption is now risk, not capability. Waymo is his example: it drives better than humans, yet you still can't take one to the airport. As with electricity, cars and nuclear power, he says the market will build "confidence infrastructure" from two parts: standards that define the tests, and insurers who pay when things go wrong. AIUC announced a $40M Series A from Ribbit Capital and FirstMark.

## Key points

- **AIUC-1.** The standard covers agent security, safety and reliability. It maps onto existing frameworks, specifies the evidence required, needs quarterly third-party testing (thousands of simulated attacks) and is updated every quarter with a consortium of Fortune 1000 risk leaders. Auditors such as KPMG and Schellman check the controls.
- **Customers and gaps.** Cursor, Harvey, Lovable and ElevenLabs are customers. Kvist says most companies already have guardrails in place but have never stress-tested whether they work. Certification takes three to ten weeks.
- **Real insurance.** ElevenLabs bought what Kvist calls a first-of-its-kind AI agent policy, backed by Lloyd's of London, with AIUC-1 as the underwriting framework. He says standards must come before insurance, and that cyber insurance struggled because insurance and technical expertise were kept apart.
- **Liability builds on standards.** He cites the Air Canada chatbot ruling and argues that widely adopted standards define the duty of care in negligence cases. Copyright cover is scarce because of a lemons problem: those who most want it are the most likely to have infringed.
- **Models and robotics next.** Kvist argues the labs cannot audit themselves. He sees a need for a neutral third party between labs and government, compared with Moody's, because CAISI's budget is small next to the task. Insurers keep the watchdog honest because they pay the bill.
- **Evals have limits.** Eval awareness makes test results less reliable, so Kvist expects more monitoring in production. Mechanistic interpretability could earn optional credit.
