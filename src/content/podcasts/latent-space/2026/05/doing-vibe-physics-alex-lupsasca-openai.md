## Thesis

Alex Lupsasca, a Vanderbilt professor and OpenAI fellow, tells the AI for Science hosts that AI has become superhuman at certain physics calculations. His evidence is two recent papers showing that "single minus" gluon and graviton tree amplitudes are nonzero, where GPT models produced the key formulas and proofs. He argues the remaining gaps are choosing the right questions, taking bigger creative leaps, and verifying results.

## Key points

- **His conversion.** Lupsasca says o3 was the first model useful for his research, doing in 11 minutes a calculation that would have taken him days. GPT-5 Pro then reproduced his paper on black hole symmetries ("Why Is There No Love in Black Holes?") in under 30 minutes after one warm-up hint.
- **The gluon paper.** Written with Alfredo Guevara, David Skinner, Andrew Strominger and Kevin Weil, it builds on the humans' insight that textbook arguments have a loophole when particles are collinear. GPT-5.2 Pro simplified the five- and six-point results and conjectured a general formula whose terms grow linearly rather than factorially; an internal model rederived and proved it from scratch in 12 hours.
- **The graviton follow-up.** Given the gluon paper and two paragraphs of prompting, public GPT-5.2 Pro did the graviton calculation over a 110-page chat, applying the directed matrix tree theorem. He says the answer arrived in days and most of the three weeks went on verification.
- **How research changes.** He spends far less time confused and can send ten chats down different routes as scouts. He worries about training graduate students when models can solve their starter problems.
- **Taste and slop.** Lupsasca compares the model to a highly skilled graduate student that cannot yet pick the right question. He warns that AI-generated papers are flooding arXiv and wants the bar raised instead.
- **Bottlenecks.** He names writing papers, verification and creativity, and suggests models may need to sample further from the tails of the distribution.
