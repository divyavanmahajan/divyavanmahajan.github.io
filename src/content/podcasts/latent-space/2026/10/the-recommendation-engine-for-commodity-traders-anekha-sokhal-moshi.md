## Thesis

Anekha Sokhal, CEO of Moshi and a former energy derivatives trader, argues that traders do not need another data terminal but a system that pushes what matters to their portfolio, including second- and third-order effects they would not spot themselves. She frames Moshi as a recommendation engine, and argues that getting the interface right is as important as the model. swyx, himself a former currency options trader, interviews her at AI Engineer New York.

## Key points

- **Background.** Sokhal studied maths, operational research, statistics and economics at Warwick, traded emerging markets and then energy derivatives at JP Morgan, and came to the US on a Fulbright data scholarship, the only one awarded in the UK. She says she published at ICLR with Stability AI and worked in collaboration with NASA.
- **Volume and types of data.** She says Moshi learns 300 new things in the four seconds it takes her to say a sentence. It combines futures time series and technical indicators, supply and demand fundamentals, news, posts on X and podcast audio with each user's private sources, then tags them into an ontology and an "n-dimensional graph".
- **Bloomberg, Claude and Netflix.** Rather than a "new Bloomberg", Sokhal describes Moshi as a cross between Bloomberg, Claude and Netflix, because its in-house recommendation system surfaces indirect links, such as an energy equity event that affects oil.
- **Trust and disagreement.** Traders follow trusted voices, such as LNG facility operators posting on X, so Moshi attaches confidence scores and highlights where sources such as Reuters, Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal disagree.
- **Owning models.** Moshi runs its own vision language model for messy PDFs so client data is not sent to third parties, which Sokhal says suits a team where most engineers have worked with NASA in some capacity.
- **Conference demand.** After her talk she extended her New York stay for meetings and heard from AI engineers told by management "you are the team". A recent job post drew 2,500 applicants in four days.
