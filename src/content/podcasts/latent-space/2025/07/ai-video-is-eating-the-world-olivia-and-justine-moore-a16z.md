## Thesis

Olivia and Justine Moore, both partners at Andreessen Horowitz, argue that AI video has crossed into the mainstream: non-technical people now create and remix viral content on TikTok and Instagram, often building whole character universes. Generation is still costly and model tooling is awkward, so the money and the craft sit with creators who understand both the tools and the platforms.

## Key points

- **Where trends start has flipped.** The guests say early AI video spread from Reddit forums to Twitter, but after Veo 3 and Minimax 2 most viral content now originates on TikTok and Instagram, where the Italian Brainrot characters are far bigger than on X.
- **Workarounds for model limits.** Veo 3 cannot do image-to-video with audio, so creators lean on characters the model already knows (Stormtroopers, Yetis) or easily recognised designs like Kim the Gorilla, who reached about 300,000 followers, and chain clips past the eight-second limit.
- **Making it pay is hard.** Olivia ran her own TikTok account of ASMR videos and found Veo 3 expensive, sometimes needing eight generations for one usable clip. The guests list platform payouts, ads and, for skilled prompters such as Nick St. Pierre, courses and consulting.
- **Model layer vs interface layer.** Asked by the hosts how money flows, the guests note Veo 3 is hard to reach through Google's Flow and a $125 a month plan, so many creators use Krea or Replicate instead; even Adobe Firefly now hosts other companies' models.
- **Advice for Latent Space.** The guests suggest brain-rot style educational clips and clipping tools like Overlap, which a16z uses to cut 30 to 140 second clips automatically. The hosts worry repurposed content underperforms native content and that the brand must resist clickbait.
