## Thesis

Jeffrey Wasserstrom, a historian of modern China, tells Lex Fridman that Xi Jinping shares Mao's personality cult but not his love of chaos, and borrows from Confucius, Mao and even Chiang Kai-shek to support single-party rule and nationalism. He argues that Xi has narrowed the space for difference, as Hong Kong shows. Even so, he says history has no fixed direction, so movements that fail can still matter later.

## Key points

- **Mao versus Xi.** Wasserstrom says both leaders have personality cults, but Mao loved disorder (his favourite novel featured the Monkey King) while Xi values stability and praises Confucian hierarchy. He adds that Xi is in some ways living out Chiang Kai-shek's dream.
- **Tiananmen and the Tank Man.** Wasserstrom describes 1989 as closer to Prague Spring than to 1989 in Eastern Europe: protesters wanted the party to live up to its own ideals and opposed corruption and nepotism. In his view the Tank Man image is banned because it makes the People's Liberation Army look like an invading force.
- **Fear, friction, flooding.** Citing Margaret Roberts, he explains censorship as threats, added effort (VPNs) and saturation with official stories. Orwell's 1984 has been on sale since 1985, but the China passages are cut from mainland editions of Huxley's Brave New World Revisited.
- **The post-1989 bargain.** Wasserstrom describes an unwritten deal: more consumer and cultural choice in exchange for no political choice. He says the space for change widened until the 2008 Olympics and has narrowed since.
- **Trade war and Taiwan.** Fridman sets out the tariffs (145% US, 125% Chinese). Wasserstrom argues US-China relations hinge on third parties (the Sino-Soviet split, 9/11, Europe, India). He thinks a move on Taiwan is more likely if Xi feels desperate than if he feels confident.
- **Hong Kong.** He calls the 2019 protests, with 1 to 2 million marchers in a city of 7.5 million, perhaps the largest ever as a share of population. He says Hong Kong's spirit survives in diaspora communities and in exile bookstores such as JF Books in Washington.
