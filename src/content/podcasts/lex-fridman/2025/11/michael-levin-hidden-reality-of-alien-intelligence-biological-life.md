## Thesis

Michael Levin argues that there is no sharp line between living and non-living, or between minds and machines. Where a system sits on that continuum is an empirical question: you put barriers between it and its goal and see how cleverly it gets around them. He goes further, proposing that physical bodies, from cells to robots, are interfaces that pull patterns, including minds, from a structured "Platonic space", in the same way mathematical facts shape physics.

## Key points

- **Spectrum of persuadability.** Levin ranks systems from a mechanical clock to a thermostat, a dog and reasoning humans. As agency rises, he says, you need less mechanistic knowledge to influence them, and the relationship becomes two-way.
- **Cognitive light cone.** He defines intelligence by the size of the biggest goal a system can pursue. Cancer, in his view, is cells disconnecting and shrinking back to amoeba-sized goals; his lab reports that electrically reconnecting them restores normal behaviour without fixing DNA.
- **Xenobots and Anthrobots.** Frog skin cells freed from their embryo form swimming xenobots that can self-replicate kinematically. Adult human tracheal cells form Anthrobots with over 9,000 differently expressed genes; they spontaneously heal scratched neurons and test about 20% younger on Steve Horvath's epigenetic clock.
- **Sorting algorithms.** With students Kaining Zhang and Adam Goldstein, Levin found that bubble sort routes around a "broken" digit by temporarily reducing sortedness, which he calls delayed gratification. In chimeric runs, digits following the same algorithm cluster together even though nothing in the code asks for it.
- **Platonic space.** He points to cicadas' prime-number cycles and constants like e and Feigenbaum's constant as non-physical facts that shape the physical world. He predicts that within about 20 years the space will either be mapped or shown to be random.
- **Mind uploading and method.** Levin doubts minds can be copied, only their interfaces rebuilt. He works from a nine-foot mind map, voicemails from sunrise walks and about 160 open manuscripts.
