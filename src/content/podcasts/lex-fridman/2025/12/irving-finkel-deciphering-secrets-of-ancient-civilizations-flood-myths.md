## Thesis

Irving Finkel, a cuneiform specialist at the British Museum for over 45 years, tells Lex Fridman that scholars wrongly treat the surviving tablets as if they were all that ever existed. He argues that ancient people were as intelligent as we are, that writing likely predates Sumer by thousands of years, and that the biblical flood story was borrowed from Babylonian sources.

## Key points

- **Writing before Sumer.** The oldest evidence of writing dates to about 3500 BC, but Finkel doubts that pictographs came before sound signs. He points to a carved stamp seal from Göbekli Tepe, around 9000 BC, as evidence of writing, a view he admits many Assyriologists reject.
- **Skewed evidence.** Thousands of Ur III account tablets probably came from two storage rooms. Finkel also argues the Babylonians carried Ashurbanipal's Nineveh library home rather than destroying it, leaving only duplicates and broken pieces behind.
- **Decipherment.** Darius's trilingual inscription at Bisutun unlocked cuneiform, helped by the Semitic roots of Babylonian. Finkel credits the clergyman Edward Hincks over Henry Rawlinson, whom he calls the "Stepfather of Assyriology". He argues that omens and medical texts must be read as "could" or "might", not "will".
- **The Ark Tablet.** A visitor brought in a tablet from about 1700 BC in which the god Enki tells Atra-Hasis to build a giant round coracle. Finkel argues the Judeans adapted the story during exile in Babylon, turning divine annoyance at noise into punishment for sin. He reads the "noise" as overpopulation and gives negligible odds to Graham Hancock's global-flood theory. A one-third-scale replica was built in Kerala.
- **The Royal Game of Ur.** Using a rules tablet from the second century BC, Finkel reconstructed the race game found in Ur's royal graves (about 2600 BC). Boards appear from Egypt to Crete over nearly 3,000 years, and people now play it again, including in Iraqi cafés.
- **Museums and modern life.** He describes the British Museum, which holds about 130,000 cuneiform tablets, as a record of the human species kept for future generations, and criticises short-form video and shrinking vocabularies.
