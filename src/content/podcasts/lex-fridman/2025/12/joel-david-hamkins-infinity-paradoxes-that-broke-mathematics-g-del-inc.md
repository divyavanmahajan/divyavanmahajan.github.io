## Thesis

Joel David Hamkins, a mathematician and philosopher at Notre Dame and the top-ranked user on MathOverflow, walks Lex Fridman from Cantor's sizes of infinity to Gödel's incompleteness theorems. He argues that one diagonal argument runs through Cantor, Russell, the halting problem and Gödel. He also argues that independence from ZFC should be celebrated, because it shows there are many set-theoretic universes rather than one.

## Key points

- **Countable and uncountable.** Hamkins uses Hilbert's Hotel, bus and train to show that countably many countable sets are still countable, then gives Cantor's diagonal proof that the reals are a strictly larger infinity.
- **One argument, many theorems.** Cantor's power set theorem, Russell's paradox (which Hamkins teaches as Russell's theorem) and the halting problem share the same diagonal logic. Russell's letter reached Frege as his book was going to print. Hamkins gives a short proof of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem from the undecidability of the halting problem.
- **Truth versus proof.** Following Tarski's disquotational account, truth is semantic and proof is syntactic. Hamkins says this distinction was blurred before Gödel and calls it the most beautiful idea in philosophy.
- **Continuum hypothesis and the multiverse.** Gödel (1938) and Cohen, who invented forcing in 1963, showed CH is independent of ZFC. Hamkins argues CH can be switched on and off by forcing, like a light switch. This puts him against the single-universe view of his former supervisor Hugh Woodin.
- **Computation and games.** Answering a question from Alexey Myasnikov, Hamkins and colleagues proved that the halting problem has a black hole: almost all Turing machines fall off the tape, so almost every instance can be decided. With Corey Evans and Norman Perlmutter he built infinite-chess positions with game values up to omega to the fourth.
- **AI skepticism.** Hamkins says current LLMs have given him basically zero help for his mathematics. He compares their polished but wrong output to his own LaTeX-typeset undergraduate homework at Caltech.
