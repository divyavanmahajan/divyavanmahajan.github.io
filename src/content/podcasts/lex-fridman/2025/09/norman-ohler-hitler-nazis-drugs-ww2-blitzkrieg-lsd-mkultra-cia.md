## Thesis

Norman Ohler, author of "Blitzed" and "Tripped", argues that historians have largely ignored drug use, even though it helps explain both the Wehrmacht's early speed and Hitler's later decline as a leader. He accepts his mentor Hans Mommsen's warning not to argue in a "monocausal" way, but says the archive documents show the army ran on methamphetamine while Hitler ran on opioids. Lex Fridman presses him on the criticism from historian Richard Evans and on how much weight drugs should carry.

## Key points

- **Pervitin and the Blitzkrieg.** Ohler says the Berlin firm Temmler patented methamphetamine as Pervitin, sold it without prescription, and that army physiologist Professor Ranke wrote a "stimulant decree" for the May 1940 Ardennes attack, with Temmler supplying 35 million doses so tanks could reach Sedan in three days.
- **Dunkirk.** He argues the halt order before Dunkirk came partly from Göring, a long-time morphine addict, persuading Hitler to let the Luftwaffe finish the job, which let the British army escape.
- **Morell's patient A.** Personal physician Theodor Morell gave Hitler vitamin and glucose injections from 1936, then the opioid Dolantin in August 1941 and Eukodal (oxycodone) from July 1943, before a meeting with Mussolini. Ohler says Hitler took meth only twice. After the July 1944 bomb plot, army ear doctor Giesing added cocaine. Ohler calls the bombing of Merck's Eukodal factory in December 1944 the start of Hitler's withdrawal in the bunker.
- **Responsibility.** Ohler rejects Evans's charge that the book excuses Nazi crimes. Citing his father, a former judge, he says planned crimes are not mitigated by intoxication, and that the genocidal policies had nothing to do with drugs.
- **The Bohemians.** He describes Harro Schulze-Boysen and Libertas, whose resistance network of more than 100 people recruited at parties and put up over a thousand anti-Nazi stickers across Berlin in May 1942.
- **LSD to MKUltra.** In the Sandoz archive (now Novartis), Ohler found Nazi chemist Richard Kuhn receiving half a gram of ergotamine from Sandoz CEO Arthur Stoll in October 1943. He says the US took over the truth-drug research, and the CIA's Sidney Gottlieb brought $240,000 to buy the world's LSD supply.
