## Thesis

Julia Shaw tells Lex Fridman that calling people evil ends the conversation: dark traits such as psychopathy, narcissism, Machiavellianism and sadism sit on a continuum, and everyone can do terrible things in the right circumstances. Understanding offenders, she argues, is how society prevents harm. She applies the same lens to memory, which she calls inherently malleable, and to the corporate and organised criminals who damage the environment.

## Key points

- **Murder is mostly mundane.** Shaw cites studies in which about 70% of men and more than 50% of women have fantasised about killing someone. Real murders are usually fights that get out of control over trivial things such as $4 or a stolen bike, and recidivism is low, so she says long sentences for killers are misaligned with prevention compared with high-risk crimes like fraud and sexual violence.
- **Serial killers and loneliness.** Discussing the Robert Pickton case, Shaw links serial offending to isolation, which removes the social "reality monitoring" that keeps people tethered.
- **Implanting crime memories.** In her PhD study, three suggestive interviews led 70% of participants to remember committing a crime as a teenager that never happened; ethics had been told to expect a 13% rate. A replication is planned at Maastricht. Her advice for witnesses: write things down immediately and before talking to others.
- **AI as a false memory machine.** Shaw warns that chatbots tell people what they want to hear and that leading prompts distort both human memory and model output. Her company Spot, co-founded in 2017 with Evernote's Phil Libin, uses a chatbot to run the scripted cognitive interview for workplace reports.
- **Sexuality and relationships.** Shaw, who is bisexual and polyamorous, describes Kinsey finding about half of men in the middle of his scale, calls persistent jealousy a red flag, and argues that monogamy sets most people up to fail.
- **Green crime.** Her book examines cases such as Volkswagen's Dieselgate, where defeat devices hid emissions up to 40 times the legal rate for about 10 years, and explains them through conformity, rationalisation and social pressure.
