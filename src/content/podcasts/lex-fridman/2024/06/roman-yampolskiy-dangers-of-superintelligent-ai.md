## Thesis

Roman Yampolskiy, author of AI: Unexplainable, Unpredictable, Uncontrollable, argues that controlling a general superintelligence is like building a perpetual safety machine: it must work on the first try, with zero bugs, forever. Because we get no second chance, he puts the probability of a bad outcome near certainty and says the only winning move is not to build it. Lex Fridman plays devil's advocate, arguing progress will be incremental enough to learn from and that current systems lack real agency.

## Key points

- **Three kinds of risk.** Yampolskiy separates X-risk (everyone dies), S-risk (mass suffering, for example by malevolent actors with functional immortality) and I-risk, the "ikigai" loss of meaning when machines can do every job.
- **Timelines.** He cites prediction markets pointing to 2026 for AGI and says that by averaging over common tasks, current models already beat an average human, though not elite experts.
- **Against open source for agents.** Responding to Yann LeCun, he says models are now grown from data and compute rather than designed, and that open-sourcing agents amounts to "giving open source weapons to psychopaths". Fridman counters that open research lets people study failures while stakes are low.
- **Limits of verification.** Formal proofs, peer review and verifiers all rely on other fallible verifiers, and self-modifying systems break static verification. Even 99.9% safety fails for a system making a billion decisions a second over a hundred years.
- **Capabilities outpace safety.** Ten times more compute yields better models, but ten times more money for safety groups does not yield ten times more safety; explainability work can even speed up self-improvement.
- **What he supports.** Narrow AI for problems such as protein folding, a pause conditioned on safety capabilities rather than time, and any regulation that moves money "from compute towards lawyers".

## Quotes
