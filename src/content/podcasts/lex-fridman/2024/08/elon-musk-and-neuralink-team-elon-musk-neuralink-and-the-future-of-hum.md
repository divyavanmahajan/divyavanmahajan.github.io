## Thesis

Elon Musk argues that human output bandwidth is the bottleneck between people and AI, and that Neuralink can eventually raise it by orders of magnitude. The Neuralink team, DJ Seo, Matthew MacDougall and Bliss Chapman, describe the nearer goal: restoring digital independence to people with paralysis. Noland Arbaugh, the first implant recipient, explains what that independence means in practice.

## Key points

- **Bandwidth and alignment.** Musk says the average human communicates at under one bit per second over a day. Faster output could help align "collective human will" with AI. He also wants AI trained for "rigorous adherence to the truth".
- **The N1 implant.** Seo describes 64 threads with 16 electrodes each (1,024 channels), a quarter-sized device, on-chip spike detection and Bluetooth output. He targets 3,000 to 6,000 channels by the end of the year and Blindsight, a visual-cortex product, next.
- **Surgery and safety.** MacDougall calls his part of the surgery simple: a one-inch hole over the "hand knob", then the R1 robot inserts threads while avoiding blood vessels. He says deep brain stimulation's roughly one-in-a-hundred bleed rate is not acceptable for Neuralink.
- **Thread retraction.** Seo and Chapman say threads pulled out about four weeks after surgery. An over-the-air firmware update switched to "spike band power", and performance recovered to a record 8.5 BPS on Webgrid, against a previous human record of about 4.6.
- **UX is the decoder.** Chapman says the main open problem is labelling what the user intends at millisecond resolution. He cites 22 ms brain-to-cursor latency, against about 75 ms for the natural brain-to-hand path, and features like Quick Scroll and magnetic targets.
- **Independence.** Arbaugh, paralysed in a 2016 diving accident, describes moving from attempted to imagined movement and playing Webgrid at 2 AM without a caregiver. He wants Optimus control and sign-language finger spelling next.
