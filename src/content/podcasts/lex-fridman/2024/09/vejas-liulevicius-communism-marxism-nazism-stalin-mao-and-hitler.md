## Thesis

Vejas Liulevicius, a historian of Germany and Eastern Europe, argues that Marxism's power came from combining a supposedly scientific law of history with room for heroic revolutionaries, and that this confidence of being on the "right side of history" let Lenin, Stalin and Mao justify almost any means. He describes communism as a hidden secular religion, and with Hannah Arendt treats Nazism and Stalinism as ideological enemies that shared a totalitarian ambition for total control.

## Key points

- **Marx's contradictions.** Liulevicius says Marx took Hegel's purposeful history and grounded it in material class conflict; Engels called him "the Darwin of history". Marx's predicted impoverishment of workers was already failing in his lifetime as unions and the middle class grew.
- **Revolution in the wrong country.** Germany, whose Social Democrats were its largest party by 1912, was meant to lead; instead the Bolsheviks seized power in the wreckage of the Russian Empire after World War I. Lenin cast himself as a revolutionary machine, and Stalin rose as a supposed moderate by controlling personnel.
- **Man-made famine.** Collectivisation punished successful "kulak" farmers, and falsified statistics left five-year plans on "a foundation of sand". Liulevicius calls the resulting famine deliberate and describes a "negative selection" that the Great Terror of 1936-38 extended to intellectuals and officials.
- **Response to Darryl Cooper.** He rejects the claims that Churchill was the war's chief villain and that the death camps were accidental, citing Mein Kampf, the Einsatzgruppen killings in Poland and the Generalplan Ost plans for Ukraine.
- **Mao and after.** Mao tried to outdo Stalin; Fridman cites about 40 million deaths in the Great Leap Forward. In 1981 the party judged Mao "70% correct", and China opened its economy while keeping political control.
- **Russia and Ukraine.** Without a Nuremberg-style reckoning, he argues, Putin draws selectively on Tsarist and Stalinist memory; Liulevicius is pessimistic about peace because accumulated suffering rules out compromise. His advice to young people: read.
