## Thesis

Ian Hutchinson, a nuclear engineer and plasma physicist at MIT, tells Lex Fridman that fusion research deserves more funding and that fission is already a clean, underrated energy source. He insists, though, that the big problems of energy, population and pollution are human and social, not technological. The second half turns to his Christian faith and his case against scientism, the belief that science is the only real knowledge.

## Key points

- **How a tokamak works.** Hutchinson explains that fusion needs tens of millions of degrees, so the plasma has to be held by magnetic fields in a doughnut-shaped (toroidal) chamber. Experiments so far have produced a few tens of megawatts for a few seconds. ITER in the south of France aims for about 500 megawatts for hundreds of seconds, but will not start until about 2025, which he calls a personal disappointment.
- **Fission is underrated.** He lists four drawbacks of fission (fuel supply, waste, after-heat safety, proliferation) and says fusion does better on all of them. Still, he calls most complaints about fission ill-informed, notes it supplies about 20% of US electricity, and argues Fukushima is overstated because the reactors killed essentially no one, against a tsunami that killed 15,000 to 20,000.
- **No technological fix.** Hutchinson says sustainable, fair energy use would mean cutting Western emissions by a factor of 100, which he doubts is possible. He is as worried about a population of about eight billion as about COVID-19.
- **Scientism and faith.** He argues history cannot be studied scientifically because it is not reproducible. He became a Christian at Cambridge, influenced by two friends, and finds the historical evidence for the resurrection persuasive. He defines faith as belief, trust and loyalty, not believing without evidence.
- **Suffering, mind and AI.** On suffering, Hutchinson says compassion matters most and a consistent, law-governed world is needed for life to exist. He suspects, like Roger Penrose, that quantum mechanics matters in the brain, so minds cannot be uploaded, and he calls transhumanism a nightmare. Fridman, who works in AI, pushes on whether religion has anything to say about machine intelligence; Hutchinson calls it interesting but not yet pressing.
