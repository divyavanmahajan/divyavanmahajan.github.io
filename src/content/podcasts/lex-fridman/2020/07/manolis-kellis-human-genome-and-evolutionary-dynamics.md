## Thesis

Manolis Kellis, head of the MIT Computational Biology Group, tells Lex Fridman that the genome is a digital code wrapped in a lot of analog machinery, and that its defining design trait is resilience rather than clean modularity. He argues that evolution's mix of blind mutation and ruthless selection holds lessons for engineers and for AI, while human culture adds a second, "horizontal" channel of inheritance through ideas.

## Key points

- **Vertical and horizontal inheritance.** Kellis contrasts genetic inheritance from parents with the transfer of ideas across a generation, which the internet has sped up. He warns that the same democratisation can erode respect for expertise, so education should teach how to find and check knowledge, not just facts.
- **Evolutionary signatures.** His PhD work aligned the genomes of related species to tell protein-coding genes, RNA genes and regulatory motifs apart by how they change over time, scaling from yeast and flies to 29 and now 200 mammals.
- **Applying the method to SARS-CoV-2.** Comparing the virus with 44 related strains, Kellis says his group concluded that ORF10 is not a protein and found a hidden gene inside ORF3a. He reads the D614G mutation as a new adaptation to human transmission and argues the lineage shows no sign of lab engineering, since its nearest relatives have never infected humans.
- **Content-based indexing.** Unlike a computer's "go to" addressing, Kellis says genes are switched on by short regulatory motifs, which makes the genome very fault tolerant: flipping 20% of letters on a hard drive would break it, but not a genome.
- **Messiness as a feature.** He cites the whole-genome duplication in baker's yeast, after which about 90% of the duplicate genes were lost, as an example of how complexity builds up and is then trimmed. He links this to deep learning techniques that help models escape local optima.
- **Brain-computer interfaces.** On Neuralink, Kellis expects people to learn to drive machine outputs before machines can write information into the brain, which he calls far off.
