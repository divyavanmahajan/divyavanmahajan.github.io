## Thesis

Jitendra Malik, a Berkeley professor and long-time computer vision researcher, tells Lex Fridman that vision feels easy only because most of it happens unconsciously. He argues that today's supervised, feed-forward systems need far more data than humans, and that the field should learn the way children do: multimodal, incremental, physical and exploratory, with perception always tied to action.

## Key points

- **The fallacy of the first step.** Malik says getting 50% of a vision problem may take a minute, 90% a day, 99% five years, and 99.99% perhaps not a lifetime. For this reason he is a pessimist on fully autonomous driving in the near future, citing a Tesla he owns that misjudged a skateboarder.
- **Teenagers are visual geniuses.** A 16-year-old in driver's education already understands 3D, occlusion and intuitive physics, mostly acquired by age two, so they only need to learn control. Machines, he argues, learn tabula rasa.
- **Video lags images.** Compute and storage limits pushed the field toward single images and edge detection. Malik estimates video recognition is about 10 years behind object recognition, and says long-form video understanding will need schemas, goals and intentionality.
- **The three R's.** He frames vision as recognition (labels), reconstruction (inverse graphics) and reorganisation (finding entities), and notes that multi-head neural networks now share representations across these tasks.
- **Active learning and simulation.** Malik backs robotics and simulators such as Facebook AI Research's Habitat, and links a child's experiments to randomised controlled trials and Alison Gopnik's "The Scientist in the Crib".
- **AGI and today's risks.** He thinks human-level AI is possible in principle but not within about 20 years, calls fears of AGI in 10-20 years unwarranted, and says present harms such as bias and the Uber pedestrian death in Arizona deserve attention now. Fridman adds recommender systems as an existing concern.
