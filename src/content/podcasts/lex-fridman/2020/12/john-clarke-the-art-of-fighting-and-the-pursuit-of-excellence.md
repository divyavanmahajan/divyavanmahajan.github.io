## Thesis

John Clarke, head of Broadway Jiu Jitsu in South Boston and Lex Fridman's own coach, argues that real achievement means doing all the work and removing every excuse before you compete, and that greatness always costs something. Fridman pushes back, asking for more patience with beginners who brag about small dreams and for more optimism about people and social media.

## Key points

- **No excuses.** Clarke says he does not respect someone just for signing up for a jiu-jitsu tournament. He respects the competitor who diets, trains properly and then loses in the first round. Fridman counters that beginners need room to protect a "little flame" of a dream, and cites Jony Ive keeping early ideas away from Steve Jobs.
- **Violence and philosophy.** Clarke links the philosophical side of martial arts to warriors who had to take stock every day because they might not come home. He admires Mike Tyson's "brutal honesty".
- **Sacrifice and relationships.** Explaining the Hunter S. Thompson quote at his gym, Clarke says success leaves broken relationships behind. On Whiplash, he argues the drummer's girlfriend was simply "the wrong girl": a good partner supports your goals, like two stars in a binary system.
- **Greatest fighters.** Clarke names Anderson Silva, Lyoto Machida and Murilo Bustamante, and has trouble leaving out George St-Pierre. He discounts title defences because promoters choose favourable match-ups. He describes coaching Kyle Bochniak against Zabit Magomedsharipov: avoid chain wrestling and press forward. Fridman calls it the greatest MMA fight he has seen.
- **Khabib and Conor.** Clarke says Khabib's striking is good enough to get him to his grappling, where he is the best ever at 155. In Khabib's place, he would not give Conor McGregor a rematch, because McGregor keeps adapting. His karate-style distance management sets up his left hand.
- **Social media and podcasting.** Clarke sees social media as "a battleground of fragile egos" and believes most people prefer looking successful to doing the work. He started his own podcast, Please, Allow Me, during lockdown.
