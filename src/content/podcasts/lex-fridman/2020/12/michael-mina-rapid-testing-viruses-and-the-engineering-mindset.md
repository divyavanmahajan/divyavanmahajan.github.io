## Thesis

Michael Mina, an immunologist and epidemiologist at Harvard, tells Lex Fridman that the pandemic is an engineering problem, not a medical one. He argues that cheap at-home rapid tests could push transmission into decline within weeks, and that the main obstacle is a regulatory system that only knows how to evaluate medical devices. Fridman agrees and takes a test on air.

## Key points

- **Imperfect is good enough.** Mina says tests do not need to catch every case. If 100 infected people go on to infect 90 instead of 130, growth turns into decay. He estimates the US needs 20 to 40 million tests a day.
- **Cost and scale.** According to Mina, a paper-strip test costs under a dollar to make. He points out that the US government bought 150 million Abbott BinaxNOW tests at $5 each, while Fridman pays around $160-170 a week for a PCR test. The real bottleneck, Mina says, is the nitrocellulose membrane.
- **Medical versus public health.** Mina argues that PCR keeps people positive for weeks after they stop being infectious. Antigen tests miss many of those late positives, which is why the FDA rates them only about 50% sensitive. He says the FDA judges tests only as medical devices, CMS says point-of-care tests fall outside its remit, and so nobody owns the public-health use case.
- **A weather system for viruses.** Mina describes a "global immunological observatory" built from anonymous plasma samples, collected through Haemonetics and Octapharma, and profiled for antibodies. He argues it could have spotted the virus spreading in New York City months before the first PCR tests did.
- **Engineered threats.** Both men call engineered viruses a top risk. Mina mentions Marc Lipsitch's warnings about gain-of-function research, and Fridman asks whether tools like AlphaFold 2 could be misused.
- **Seeing solutions.** Mina recalls being told it was impossible to turn the Broad Institute's robots to COVID testing; it became the highest-throughput lab in the country. He says working in a Sri Lankan refugee camp after the 2004 tsunami, having been a monk there, pushed him into public health.
