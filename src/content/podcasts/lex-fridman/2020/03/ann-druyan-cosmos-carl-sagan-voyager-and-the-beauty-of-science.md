## Thesis

Ann Druyan, co-writer of Cosmos and creative director of the Voyager Interstellar Message Project, tells Lex Fridman that science is the clearest way of knowing reality as it is, which she compares to loving someone without illusion. She argues that science must be accessible to everyone, not a priesthood, so that a civilization in its technological adolescence uses its power wisely, especially against climate change.

## Key points

- **Skepticism and wonder.** Fridman asks whether scientific skepticism shuts down bold ideas. Druyan says Carl Sagan, her 20-year tutor in science, never traded one for the other, and that science's error-correcting mechanism, like the Bill of Rights, is what makes it deliver.
- **Voyager.** Druyan calls Voyager perhaps the greatest scientific achievement of the 20th century: built in the 1970s, running on about 11 watts, designed for a dozen years and still in contact after 42. Its thrusters fired again after decades unused.
- **The Golden Record.** It carries about 118 images, 27 pieces of music and a sound essay; Druyan says it helped invent the idea of world music. She recorded an hour of her brainwaves for it, days after she and Sagan decided by phone on June 1, 1977 to marry. A congressman objected to a nude image, which she reads as humanity's discomfort with itself.
- **Cosmos.** Season 2 aired in 181 countries within two weeks. Druyan credits Seth MacFarlane with getting the series to Fox with creative control, and highlights outsider scientists such as Michael Faraday, who never took out a patent.
- **Existential threats.** Druyan ranks climate change above AI as a concern, notes the original 1980 Cosmos warned about it, and argues that politics needs the 100- and 1,000-year timescales science uses.
- **Life and mortality.** Druyan suspects life arises more often than we think and sees consciousness as a product of physics and chemistry. At 70, she says she would not choose immortality for herself, only for Sagan.
