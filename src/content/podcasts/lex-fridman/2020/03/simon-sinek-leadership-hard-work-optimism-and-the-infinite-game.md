## Thesis

Simon Sinek, author of Start With Why, Leaders Eat Last and The Infinite Game, tells Lex Fridman that trying to "win" in games with no finish line, such as business, politics or a life, erodes trust, cooperation and innovation. He argues that leaders are responsible for giving people a just cause; pressure in service of that cause unites a team, while pressure to hit arbitrary numbers only produces stress.

## Key points

- **Finite vs infinite games.** Sinek credits the philosopher James Carse with the idea: finite games have fixed rules and known players, while infinite games aim to keep playing. He cites the Washington Post obituary of Jack Welch, which said he pleased Wall Street and distressed employees, as the legacy of a finite player.
- **Optimism and idealism.** Sinek calls himself an idealist: world peace will never come, but a vision should be practically impossible and still worth working towards. Fridman compares himself to the "fool" in Dostoevsky's The Idiot.
- **Mortality and ego.** Fridman raises Ernest Becker's The Denial of Death. Sinek says the desire to contribute, not fear of death, drives him, and that he fought publishers to keep his name small on his books.
- **Hard work vs sleep.** Fridman defends obsessive hard work, especially in early ventures. Sinek calls that a short-term strategy, citing studies that well-rested executives get more done, and blames leaders who lay people off over "arbitrary projections" for staff who leave at five o'clock.
- **Tough leaders.** On Whiplash, Elon Musk, Steve Jobs and Jeff Bezos, Sinek says being hard on people works only with belief and care. He adds that companies should be honest about demanding cultures so people can choose them.
- **Lasting organisations.** Sinek's "school bus test" asks whether a company survives without its founder. He says Microsoft drifted under Steve Ballmer's numbers-driven pressure and recovered under Satya Nadella.
