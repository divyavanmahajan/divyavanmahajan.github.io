## Thesis

Oxford philosopher Nick Bostrom, director of the Future of Humanity Institute, separates the simulation hypothesis (we live in a computer simulation) from the simulation argument: almost all civilisations die before technological maturity, or mature civilisations lose interest in running ancestor simulations, or we are almost certainly simulated. He declines to put numbers on the three. In the second part he describes superintelligence as a general-purpose technology with a large upside, and says public debate mixes up near-term and long-term AI.

## Key points

- **Technological maturity.** Bostrom cites Eric Drexler's work on molecular manufacturing, including a sugar-cube-sized computer that could match a million human brains, to argue that a mature civilisation could run many simulations using a tiny fraction of its resources.
- **Consciousness as computation.** The argument assumes that a computation copying the human brain down to the neuron would be conscious. Bostrom notes a simulation need not render every detail, just as games only render what the player sees, and dreams show how cheaply a convincing reality can be produced.
- **Indifference versus doomsday.** He explains the "bland principle of indifference": if ten times more simulated observers share your experiences, you are ten times more likely to be one of them. He contrasts this with the Carter-Leslie doomsday argument, which needs a stronger and more contested self-sampling assumption.
- **Experience machine.** Discussing Robert Nozick's thought experiment, Bostrom suggests that people's reluctance to plug in may partly be a status quo effect. Fridman says he would be willing to take the leap.
- **Superintelligence upside.** Bostrom says that since his 2014 book, alignment research has grown, and the upside now gets too little attention. AI would not on its own solve political problems that need coordination. He puts more weight on an intelligence explosion than he thinks the average AI researcher does.
- **Utopia.** With huge abundance, he argues, society should first try to score well on many value systems at once, perhaps 98% of the best on each, before accepting trade-offs.
