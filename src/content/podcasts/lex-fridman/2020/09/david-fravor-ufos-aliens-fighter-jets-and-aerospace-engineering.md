## Thesis

David Fravor, a Navy pilot for 18 years and commander of strike fighter squadron VFA-41, tells Lex Fridman that the "Tic Tac" he and three other aircrew watched in 2004 was a physical object that reacted to them and outperformed anything he knows of. He says he is not a "UFO guy" and calls the event five minutes of his life, but wants the topic studied without stigma as a possible lead on new propulsion. Fridman calls himself more sceptical of the released videos than of Fravor's eyewitness account.

## Key points

- **The encounter.** Fravor says the USS Princeton had tracked objects for about two weeks. He saw white water shaped like a 737 under the surface and a white, wingless object about the size of a Hornet (around 40 feet) moving abruptly above it. As he cut across to meet it at about half a mile, it accelerated away in under half a second; the Princeton then reported it at their CAP point roughly 60 miles away.
- **The video and the rumours.** Chad Underwood later filmed it with the targeting pod, and Fravor says the radar showed jamming indications. He calls claims of a 10-minute original false (it ran about 1:30) and says no one in suits took the tapes; intel officers were playing a prank.
- **Answering debunkers.** Fravor rejects Mick West's idea that the object only appears to dart off screen because tracking was lost, saying the pod's gimbal rates could not keep up. He also argues a secret military test is unlikely, since tests are run in controlled areas, not among unknowing pilots.
- **Flying and AI.** Fravor describes the "80 percent solution" in air combat and the jet's sensor fusion as a "third brain". He argues AI works within hard limits, while a human can act in grey areas, for example departing controlled flight on purpose.
- **Private money and space.** Fravor praises SpaceX for doing Boeing's contracted crew-capsule work faster and for half the money, and suggests figures such as Bezos and Musk could fund university propulsion research outside government constraints.
