## Thesis

Stephen Wolfram tells Lex Fridman that the universe may be generated by a simple rule repeatedly rewriting a hypergraph of discrete "atoms of space". Most of what happens is computationally irreducible, but Wolfram argues that the pockets of reducibility found in such rules match the two pillars of 20th-century physics, general relativity and quantum mechanics. In his model these turn out to be facets of one theory.

## Key points

- **Computational irreducibility.** Wolfram says that knowing a system's rules does not let you jump ahead of it. Science works in "pockets of reducibility", which is why planets are predictable and battles are not.
- **Space, time and causal invariance.** In the Wolfram Physics Project, space is a hypergraph and time is the repeated application of rewriting rules. Causal invariance, meaning the order of updates does not change the causal graph, is what lets special relativity emerge. Curvature of the hypergraph gives Einstein's equations, and energy is the flux of causal edges.
- **Quantum mechanics as branchial space.** Wolfram says the possible update sequences form a multiway graph. Slicing it gives "branchial space", where distance corresponds to entanglement. He claims the Feynman path integral describes geodesics bending in branchial space in the same way that Einstein's equations describe them in physical space.
- **Quantum computing scepticism.** Based on early simulations that have only factored 2 times 3, Wolfram suspects that the cost of combining parallel branches into a classical answer cancels the exponential speed-up. He adds that practical quantum hardware research is still worthwhile.
- **Rule space and observers.** Running all possible rules at once yields a "rulial" multiway graph that is also causally invariant. Wolfram argues that the rule we attribute to the universe depends on our reference frame, so aliens could describe physics in ways incompatible with ours. He links this to meta-mathematics: Euclid's hardest theorem, that there are five Platonic solids, sits 33 steps from his axioms.
- **Practical spillovers.** Wolfram says causal invariance is essentially the idea behind eventual consistency in distributed databases, and predicts that parallel programming may one day be done "in a certain reference frame".
