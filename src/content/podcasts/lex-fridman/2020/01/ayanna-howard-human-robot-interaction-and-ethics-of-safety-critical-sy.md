## Thesis

Ayanna Howard, a Georgia Tech roboticist and director of the Human-Automation Systems lab, tells Lex Fridman that the robots people want are not perfectly accurate rule-followers but ones that adapt to humans, as Rosie from The Jetsons did. She argues that every developer of safety-critical algorithms holds real responsibility for outcomes, and that the hardest problem in human-robot interaction is adaptation and trust, not perception or manipulation.

## Key points

- **Autonomous vehicles.** Howard, a Tesla owner who stays "hyper alert" on Autopilot and Smart Summon, expects near-term success in fixed, slow-moving settings such as closed campuses or new neighbourhoods designed for self-driving cars, because mixing with unpredictable human drivers is the hard part.
- **Ethics as a developer's job.** Howard compares coding to medicine: a great gift with great responsibility. She says developers should check ethical outcomes themselves, as programmers once tested their own code before dedicated testers existed. Fridman describes receiving messages saying he has blood on his hands for working on semi-autonomous vehicles.
- **Bias.** Howard separates bias from prejudice, citing teenage insurance premiums versus premiums tied to socio-economic areas, and exoskeletons designed around the body types in a lab. She proposes bug-bounty-style rewards for anyone who can "find an ethics hole" and argues companies should open up rather than fix bias in-house.
- **Over-trust.** Howard defines trust by behaviour, not survey answers: people say they would not follow a robot in a burning building, then do. She cites a radiology study where offering several candidate areas, not one, kept trust calibrated and improved accuracy.
- **Education and jobs.** Howard is more hopeful about workforce retraining than early education, and worries that people without strong education will struggle to adapt to new jobs.
- **Robotics companies and the future.** Howard says most failed robotics firms lacked product-market fit, though Anki puzzles her; iRobot survived on government contracts until the Roomba. She expects a second batch of co-robot companies, and does not fear existential AI risk, comparing AI to children who inherit their parents' values.
