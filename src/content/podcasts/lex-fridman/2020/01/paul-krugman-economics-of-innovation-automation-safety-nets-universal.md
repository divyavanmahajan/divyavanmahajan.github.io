## Thesis

Paul Krugman, Nobel laureate, CUNY professor and New York Times columnist, tells Lex Fridman that today's technological change is not faster or bigger than earlier shocks, and that the productivity data do not show robots taking the jobs. In his view, stagnant wages and a harsh US safety net are mostly the result of political choices, and the better fix is to strengthen existing targeted programmes rather than adopt universal basic income.

## Key points

- **Markets have limits.** Krugman calls competition great where it works, citing phone carriers, but points to Kenneth Arrow's 1963 paper to argue it fails in health care because buyers cannot be well-informed. He suggests a roughly three-quarters invisible hand, one-quarter visible hand balance.
- **Automation and productivity.** Krugman says productivity grew about 2% a year after World War 2, picked up from the mid-90s to the mid-2000s, and is unimpressive now. He compares current change to containerisation, which ended New York's longshoreman jobs.
- **Why people blame robots.** He argues the technologies now advancing are visible to "the chattering class", that the post-2008 unemployment was a failure of demand, and that union decline and policy weakened workers' bargaining power. He notes two-thirds of Danish workers are unionised despite the same technology.
- **Safety nets and UBI.** Krugman says the US is the only advanced country without a universal health-care guarantee and gives little support to children. He argues UBI is either too low to live on or enormously expensive, and favours longer unemployment insurance, food stamps, the Earned Income Tax Credit and family support.
- **Innovation and infrastructure.** He estimates 70 to 80 percent of per capita income growth comes from advancing knowledge, often prosaic inventions such as the cardboard box or EZ Pass, and says spending on things like a new Hudson River rail tunnel stalls because it gets tangled in disputes over the welfare state.
- **Trade and public debate.** Krugman says US-China trade made both countries richer but hurt North Carolina furniture workers. Despite heavy hate mail, he advises writing what you think is right.
