## Thesis

Dmitry Korkin, a professor of bioinformatics at Worcester Polytechnic Institute, tells Lex Fridman that computational methods let scientists produce useful predictions about a new virus within weeks, which experimental labs then validate. His group used the published genome and decades of shared structural data to build a 3D map of the coronavirus's proteins and released it openly. He sees computing's role as helping experimental scientists streamline discovery.

## Key points

- **A classroom project became a head start.** Korkin says his autumn 2019 bioinformatics course studied the Eastern equine encephalitis virus, a neglected disease too small to attract drug companies. When the Wuhan genome came out weeks later, his group reused the same workflow.
- **Modelling from templates.** Korkin explains that the group found gene boundaries using SARS data, then used template-based modelling against the Protein Data Bank. He says this is far more accurate than building structures from scratch, though it does not cover all 29 proteins.
- **Mutations cluster, drug sites mostly unchanged.** Korkin reports that some proteins are almost identical to SARS while others are heavily mutated. The sites where known small molecules bind look intact, which he calls promising for reusing existing compounds.
- **Open data and preprints.** Fridman and Korkin both note the openness of the field. Korkin argues that knowledge, not the journal, is becoming the core value, and says bioRxiv was so overloaded that his group first posted the preprint on its own website.
- **Folding and AI.** Korkin cites DeepMind's AlphaFold as among the top teams in the CASP competition and argues that decades of structural data now make protein structure prediction a learnable problem.
- **Simulating outbreaks.** His group's agent-based model of infections on a cruise ship treats the pathogen as its own agent. Korkin stresses that the long asymptomatic period changes containment, and argues that masks protect others as well as the wearer.
