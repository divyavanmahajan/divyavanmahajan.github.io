## Thesis

Scott Aaronson, a quantum computer scientist at UT Austin, tells Lex Fridman that the evidence so far suggests the world satisfies the Church-Turing thesis, so there is no known need to step outside computation to explain physics. He is sceptical of theories of consciousness that ignore obvious test cases, and he walks through complexity theory, from universality to P versus NP, as the field that measures how hard computable problems really are.

## Key points

- **Simulation and theories of everything.** Aaronson says a perfect simulation would be indistinguishable from reality, so the question only matters if the universe has exploitable bugs. He says Eric Weinstein and Stephen Wolfram have ideas that might lead to a theory of everything, not such a theory.
- **Against integrated information theory.** He argues that Giulio Tononi's phi rewards graph expansion, so a regular grid of XOR gates running an error-correcting code could score as more conscious than a human. Tononi accepted that conclusion; Aaronson says a theory must first get known cases right.
- **Penrose and Gödel.** Aaronson counts five or six separate speculations in Roger Penrose's view, including uncomputable quantum gravity, objective collapse and an argument from Gödel's incompleteness theorem, which he and most computer scientists consider unsound.
- **GPT-3.** Where Eugene Goostman was no advance over ELIZA, Aaronson calls GPT-3 a major advance that can write a B-minus student essay, though it struggles with arithmetic and spatial reasoning. Nobody knows where scaling hits its ceiling, he says.
- **P versus NP.** Aaronson created the Complexity Zoo in 2002, which now lists about 500 classes. He puts two or three percent odds on P equalling NP; an efficient algorithm would break most internet encryption and could find short proofs for all seven Clay prize problems.
- **Pandemic and discourse.** Aaronson calls the US COVID response a historic failure of institutions such as the CDC and favours human challenge trials. Both men criticise cancel culture; Aaronson argues the silent supporters of open discourse need to speak up.
