## Thesis

Robert Langer, MIT professor and one of the most cited engineers in history, tells Lex Fridman that big advances in medicine come from pursuing broad, platform-style ideas, surviving early rejection, and then putting in years of costly development. He presents patents, capable business leadership and happy, motivated researchers as what turns lab discoveries into products.

## Key points

- **Rejection and long timelines.** Langer recalls that Nature and Science each first rejected the two early papers from his postdoc work with Judah Folkman. He says the line of research they started in 1976 led, 28 years and billions of dollars later, to Genentech's Avastin being approved by the FDA in 2004.
- **Drug delivery challenges.** Langer describes the field's open problems: targeting specific cells, getting drugs across barriers such as the blood-brain barrier, and "smart" systems, including microchips that release a drug on a signal. He sees AI helping to find patterns in screening data.
- **Tissue engineering.** Langer says engineered skin is already FDA-approved for burn victims and diabetic ulcers, and that blood vessels are in phase three trials. He expects modest rather than radical gains in longevity.
- **Patents and cost.** Langer argues that without patents nobody would fund the more than two billion dollars (a Tufts estimate) it takes to bring a new drug to market. He says clinical trials are usually the biggest expense, while admitting patents can limit access once a drug is out.
- **Startup formula.** Fridman notes that Langer has helped launch about 40 companies. Langer looks for a platform technology, a paper in a top journal, animal data, broad patent claims and a founder from the lab who wants to keep working on it. He calls the business people the most important factor, and lists market size, animal models, clear trial endpoints, competition, reimbursement and manufacturing as criteria.
- **Running a lab.** Langer says he mainly wants researchers to feel they are making progress on important work. He names basic-research funding as academia's top concern and is proudest of his former students.
