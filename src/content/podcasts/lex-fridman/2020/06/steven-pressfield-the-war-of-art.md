## Thesis

Steven Pressfield, author of The War of Art and Gates of Fire, tells Lex Fridman that he is less interested in war itself than in war as a metaphor for the fight inside each person. He frames that fight as ego against the greater self: Resistance is the ego's fearful voice, while creative work means opening up to the muse. The way to win, he argues, is mundane daily discipline over decades rather than any shortcut.

## Key points

- **War as metaphor.** Pressfield believes humans still carry a tribal us-versus-them instinct and hopes it can be sublimated into sport. He reads the Spartans at Thermopylae as willing self-sacrifice and the Six-Day War, which he wrote about from the Israeli side in The Lion's Gate, as a return from exile. He says lasting peace needs mutual respect and mutual fear.
- **Ego versus self.** Drawing on Jung and Kabbalah, Pressfield says the ego believes death, time and separateness are real and is ruled by fear, while the self is ruled by love. Resistance, in his view, is the ego trying to keep control; in stories the villain plays that role.
- **The muse.** He describes ideas as arriving from another plane, like the angels in City of Angels, and cites the bodies of work of Bruce Springsteen and Philip Roth as evidence of a destiny running through an artist's career.
- **No hack.** Learning to let go and tune in took him about thirty years. He credits his editor Shawn Coyne with forcing a full rewrite of a supernatural thriller that broke genre conventions.
- **Health and routine.** Pressfield, 75 at the time, says four hours of writing a day is his maximum. He gets up very early, goes to the gym first, writes for about three hours, starts immediately, stops when he knows what comes next and then closes the office. Fridman argues that obsession sometimes overrides health; Pressfield sides with the athlete's model of looking after the body.
- **Born or trained.** He puts it at 90% training and 10% genetics, and compares a writer's courage to a mother protecting a child.
