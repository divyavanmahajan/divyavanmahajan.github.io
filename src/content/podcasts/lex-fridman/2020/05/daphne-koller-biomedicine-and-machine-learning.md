## Thesis

Daphne Koller, Stanford professor, Coursera co-founder and CEO of insitro, tells Lex Fridman that machine learning has so far played little role in medicine because the datasets it needs did not exist. Her company reverses the usual order: instead of applying machine learning to data gathered for other purposes, it combines lab techniques to produce data designed for predictive models. She also reflects on what Coursera taught her about learning, and argues that near-term AI risks come from complex, poorly understood systems and misuse rather than superintelligence.

## Key points

- **Data built for models.** Koller says new tools now make data at scale, and insitro designs experiments to feed machine learning. Data is the means; better human health is the goal.
- **Better models than mice.** She argues most drugs fail because results in animal models do not carry over to humans. insitro's "disease in a dish" approach uses patient-derived cells, now an almost industrialised service, to compare sick and healthy cells. She estimates only five to ten thousand such cell lines exist worldwide.
- **Measurement turns biology digital.** Single-cell measurement and advanced microscopy produce quantitative data, which Koller uses to find disease subtypes and test whether a drug moves cells back towards a healthy state. She is careful not to promise cures for specific diseases.
- **What Coursera learned.** After the 2011 Stanford courses drew about 100,000 students each, Koller and Andrew Ng started Coursera in 2012. Lessons: 5-7 minute videos beat 15 minutes, short courses beat 15-week ones, quick quizzes and fast feedback keep people engaged, and study groups improve completion. She expects online learning to grow mainly in continuing education rather than replace classrooms.
- **Uncertainty and risk.** Koller warns that neural networks become more confident as inputs drift from the training data, which is dangerous in diagnosis or driving. She sees AGI as far off and puts the nearer risks in complex systems with unpredictable ripple effects, such as financial crashes, and in misuse of powerful tools, including gene editing.
