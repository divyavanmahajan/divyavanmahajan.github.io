## Thesis

Sertac Karaman, an MIT professor and co-founder of Optimus Ride, tells Lex Fridman that the hard part of robotics is putting machines at high density into places built for humans. He argues that autonomy will spread through geofenced, market-specific deployments where a few people supervise many vehicles, and that progress depends on iterative learning in the real world rather than on confident timelines.

## Key points

- **Flying versus driving.** Karaman says consumer drone autonomy is easier today, but large-scale flying for transport and logistics is harder than driving. He sees an underused "agile airspace" above stone-throwing height and below large aircraft, for trips such as Boston to Manhattan in about one and a half hours.
- **Simulation and human behaviour.** Physics is easy to simulate and cameras are getting close, he says, but human behaviour remains very hard. Predicting what other road users will do, and the game-theoretic question of how aggressive a robot should be, are still open; he links this to a trade-off between transport efficiency and livability.
- **Waymo and Tesla.** Karaman sees Waymo treating autonomy partly as research into a new "AI engine", while Tesla sells a product people pay for and use. He says the key for both is an informed public.
- **Optimus Ride's model.** Rather than teleoperation, he describes "AV controllers" who help vehicles with efficiency, aiming for ten people operating 50 vehicles. Optimus Ride targets transportation-deprived areas of roughly two by two miles, such as the Brooklyn Navy Yard, using small four- to six-seat vehicles instead of shuttles.
- **Lidar and cameras.** He believes camera-only autonomy will work eventually, but expects early deployments to use lidar, which is easier to build with and may become cheaper than the extra compute cameras need.
- **Fast flight.** Through the Lockheed Martin AlphaPilot challenge and his own lab, Karaman works on drones that see at a kilohertz. He says such fast systems hit physical limits like copper bandwidth and clock speeds, and could one day steer cars out of accidents. He names Bellman's equation as the most beautiful idea in robotics.
