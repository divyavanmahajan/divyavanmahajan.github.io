## Thesis

Pamela McCorduck, author of Machines Who Think (1979), tells Lex Fridman that AI is the latest form of a very old impulse, found in myth and literature, to create intelligence outside the human cranium. She recalls the founders of the field as people who knew they were doing something important, argues that "AI winters" are a commercial rather than scientific phenomenon, and stays a self-described congenital optimist about where the field is going.

## Key points

- **Writing the first history.** McCorduck, a novelist and English major, decided to interview the founders while they were alive. John McCarthy thought it a nutty idea, the NSF told her she was "only a writer", and a private grant from Ed Fredkin at MIT funded the work.
- **Four founders, different motives.** She names Allen Newell, Herbert Simon, Marvin Minsky and John McCarthy as the founding fathers: Newell and Simon arrived at Dartmouth with a working program, the Logic Theorist, and wanted to simulate human cognition, while McCarthy took a mathematical approach and saw AI as a boon to humanity.
- **Hellenic versus Hebraic views.** McCorduck contrasts the Greek view of robots as helpers (Hephaestus's forge, the self-steering ships of the Odyssey) with a view rooted in the commandment against graven images, which she says shapes the sense that AI is blasphemous.
- **No real winters.** She calls the AI winter idea a crock: basic research in the 1980s laid important groundwork that simply could not be monetised. Fridman counters that over-promising, such as autonomous-vehicle timelines from Elon Musk and carmakers, can still cut funding; she points to the UK's Lighthill report as an example.
- **From symbols to algorithms.** McCorduck says she was surprised that algorithmic, data-driven methods became the main event while symbolic processing stalled, and warns that algorithms trained on human decisions inherit human biases.
- **Fear and the "male gaze".** She reads warnings about existential risk from Musk, Hawking and others as men threatened by something that might be smarter, and says what drew her to AI, subconsciously, was disproving that intelligence is male.
