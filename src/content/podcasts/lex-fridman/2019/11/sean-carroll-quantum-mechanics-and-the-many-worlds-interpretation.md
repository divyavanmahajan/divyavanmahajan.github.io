## Thesis

Sean Carroll, a theoretical physicist at Caltech and the Santa Fe Institute and author of Something Deeply Hidden, tells Lex Fridman that measurement and observers should have no special role in the laws of physics. He argues that Hugh Everett's many-worlds interpretation, where there is only a wave function obeying the Schrodinger equation, is the simplest form of the theory. In his view its difficulty lies in mapping that maths onto what we observe, not in the theory itself, and it is the best starting point for quantum gravity.

## Key points

- **Conservation of momentum.** Carroll calls it the most beautiful idea in physics because it replaced Aristotle's natures and purposes with impersonal patterns. He credits Avicenna, around the year 1000, as probably the first to state that a projectile in a vacuum would keep moving.
- **How many-worlds works.** Observers are quantum systems too, Carroll says. Measuring something means becoming entangled with it, and the branches of the wave function then stop interacting. He stresses that the universe splits into "thinner" branches rather than being copied, so energy is conserved.
- **The rival interpretations.** Carroll names hidden-variable theories, spontaneous collapse theories (with Roger Penrose's gravity-induced collapse as a variant) and epistemic interpretations as the alternatives. He says their formalism is more complicated, but they sit closer to what we experience.
- **A finite Hilbert space.** Because the universe's expansion is accelerating, there is a horizon around us. Carroll says this suggests a finite Hilbert space of roughly 10^(10^122) dimensions, which would put a limit on branching.
- **Emergent spacetime.** Carroll argues that quantising general relativity has failed and that black holes, Hawking radiation and holography point to non-local physics. He favours starting from a purely quantum description and recovering space as an approximation. He separates the emergent arrow of time from time itself.
- **Minds.** Carroll calls himself a physicalist: minds are largely classical and consciousness is emergent, like spacetime.
