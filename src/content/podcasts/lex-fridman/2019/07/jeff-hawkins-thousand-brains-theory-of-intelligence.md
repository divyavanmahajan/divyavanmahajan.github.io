## Thesis

Jeff Hawkins, founder of Numenta and the Redwood Center for Theoretical Neuroscience, tells Lex Fridman that understanding the neocortex is the fastest route to intelligent machines. He argues that every part of the cortex stores knowledge in reference frames, that thousands of columns each build complete models and vote to reach a shared answer, and that scaling today's deep learning will not produce intelligence on its own.

## Key points

- **One uniform circuit.** Hawkins says the neocortex takes up about 70-75% of the human brain and looks the same everywhere, like a dinner napkin about 2.5 mm thick. Following Vernon Mountcastle's 1978 argument, he holds that vision, language and mathematics all run on the same cortical algorithm.
- **Reference frames.** Thinking about how his finger predicts the rim of a coffee cup led Hawkins to propose that each patch of cortex tracks location relative to the object, reusing the grid-cell mechanism from older navigation circuits. He extends this to concepts, citing the method of loci and an fMRI study on birds.
- **Voting columns.** Each of roughly 150,000 square millimetres of cortex can learn whole objects; columns share candidate guesses over long-range connections and settle on one answer, which he offers as a fix for the "sensor fusion problem".
- **Real neurons predict.** Citing Numenta's 2016 paper on why neurons have thousands of synapses, Hawkins says most synapses sit far from the cell body and detect patterns of 10-20 co-active inputs that prime the cell to fire. Learning forms new synapses rather than tuning weights, so it is fast and does not overwrite earlier learning.
- **Sparsity in machine learning.** Hawkins says the brain keeps about 2% of neurons active, and that a Numenta team led by Subutai Ahmad found enforced sparsity makes convolutional networks harder to fool with adversarial examples. He rejects Richard Sutton's "bitter lesson" that scale alone is enough.
- **Timelines and risk.** If machine learning researchers take up these ideas, he expects an under-20-year horizon. He calls existential AI fears hollow, saying an engineered super virus worries him more, and urges builders to leave out emotions and self-replication.
