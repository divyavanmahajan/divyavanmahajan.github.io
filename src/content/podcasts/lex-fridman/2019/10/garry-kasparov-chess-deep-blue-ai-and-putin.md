## Thesis

Garry Kasparov tells Lex Fridman that treating chess as the peak of human intellect was a mistake: machines did not beat him by understanding the game better but by making fewer mistakes. In his view machines will always prevail in closed systems, and the real question is how humans add value in collaboration with them. The second half turns to the Soviet Union, Putin and Kasparov's life in opposition.

## Key points

- **Psychology of winning.** Kasparov says losing felt like physical pain because he believed every result came down to his own play, and that fear of mistakes guarantees mistakes. He highlights games 16 and 24 of his 1985 match against Karpov, when he became world champion at 22, and his 1999 win against Topalov.
- **Comparing eras.** He refuses to rank champions across generations because each one knows more than the last. He describes Magnus Carlsen as a combination of Fischer's dynamism and Karpov's efficiency, and argues his own 2851 rating in an era with less rating inflation compares well with Carlsen's 2882.
- **Deep Blue.** The 1997 loss was the first match he had ever lost; he says he played poorly but believes the match was not fairly organised and gave IBM unfair advantages. He notes he beat Deep Thought in 1989 and won the 1996 match.
- **Closed versus open systems.** Kasparov argues engines rated around 3400-3500 beat humans through a "steady hand", not intelligence. He calls AlphaZero, trained on about 60 million games, the first step towards machine-generated knowledge, but says machines cannot tell which questions are relevant and adapt slowly to exposed weaknesses.
- **Bias and safety.** He says biased machines mirror society, and that autonomous vehicles should be judged on making fewer mistakes than humans, not on perfection.
- **Russia and Putin.** Kasparov argues totalitarian, centrally planned systems cannot drive innovation and that communism was never condemned as fascism was. He says he lives in New York for safety, expects to return to Russia, and claims Russia interfered in the 2016 US election and would in 2020; Fridman raised the question without taking a position.
