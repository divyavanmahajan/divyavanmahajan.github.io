## Thesis

Melanie Mitchell, author of "Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans", tells Lex Fridman that analogy making underlies all concepts, and that how to form and use concepts flexibly is the field's most important open problem. She expects current supervised deep learning to hit fundamental limits and places human-level AI more than a hundred years away. Fridman is more optimistic about how far learning can go, but largely agrees that common sense will have to be built in somehow.

## Key points

- **Copycat and analogy.** Mitchell describes Copycat, which she began building in 1984 with Douglas Hofstadter: an agent-based program that solves letter-string analogies (if ABC changes to ABD, what does IJK change to?) using a shared "workspace" inspired by blackboard systems. She calls its "birth" one of her happiest days.
- **Limits of deep learning.** Mitchell cites DeepMind's Atari Breakout agent, which failed to transfer when the paddle was moved a few pixels, as evidence that it never learned concepts such as paddle, ball or tunnelling. She argues networks lack feedback and top-down expectations, and that some innate knowledge is needed.
- **Common sense is invisible.** She argues most human knowledge (intuitive physics, psychology and "metaphysics") is not written down anywhere, which is why projects like Doug Lenat's Cyc and ConceptNet fall short.
- **Autonomous driving.** Mitchell sees the long tail of edge cases as the core problem and expects self-driving to be limited to instrumented, mapped areas for a long time. Fridman describes Tesla's vision-only, active-learning approach and the conservative policies of Waymo and Cruise.
- **Superintelligence fears.** Mitchell explains her New York Times op-ed, arguing against Stuart Russell's and Nick Bostrom's examples (the climate-fixing AI that kills humans, the paperclip maximiser) because she does not think intelligence separates from values and emotions. Fridman reads Yoshua Bengio's reply that value alignment already matters for powerful companies.
- **Complexity and the Santa Fe Institute.** She describes SFI, founded in 1984 largely by Los Alamos scientists, with about ten resident and around a hundred external faculty, and argues that reductionism, as in the human genome project, misses emergent behaviour.
