## Thesis

Sebastian Thrun, who led Stanford's DARPA Grand Challenge team and Google's self-driving car program and co-founded Udacity, tells Lex Fridman that the biggest shift in computing is machines learning their own rules from experience. He argues that progress comes from picking problems ordinary people care about, such as transportation, rather than methods, and that education must be opened up to everyone.

## Key points

- **Winning the DARPA challenge.** Thrun credits Stanford's 2005 win with Stanley to a focus on machine learning over hardware, a four-person core team, freezing the system a month early and a 160-page test booklet aimed at the weakest part of the system.
- **Funding outcomes, not effort.** He praises DARPA for paying for accomplishment instead of hours, which pulled in new people and reset the field; many of today's self-driving leaders took part in those challenges.
- **The long tail of driving.** Thrun says reaching 90% of driving situations is easy but each further fraction of a percent is hard. He is a daily Tesla Autopilot user, sees deep learning as having boosted all approaches, and argues cameras are sufficient because humans drive with eyes alone.
- **Machine learning as expertise transfer.** He describes a Stanford study using 129,000 biopsied skin images, where a network detected skin cancer on an iPhone about as accurately as board-certified dermatologists, while stressing it is narrow pattern recognition, not general intelligence.
- **Udacity and soft skills.** Thrun cites over 20,000 self-driving nanodegree students, 100,000 scholarships for Americans, and an Egyptian cohort of 1,100 high-school students with a 95% graduation rate. He wants to teach empathy and teamwork next.
- **Kitty Hawk's Heaviside.** He says the eVTOL is about 38 decibels, has eight motors so losing one is not critical, and flies 100 miles with 30% reserve, which he claims could cut 300 hours of yearly commuting to 30.
