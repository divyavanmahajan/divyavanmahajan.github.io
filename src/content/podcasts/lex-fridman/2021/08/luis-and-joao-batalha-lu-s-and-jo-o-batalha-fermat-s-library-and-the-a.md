## Thesis

Luís and João Batalha, brothers from Portugal who co-founded Fermat's Library, tell Lex Fridman that research papers are among the densest texts people read and are not written for clarity, so shared annotations can make them easier to understand over time. They argue the journal system, driven by impact factors and paywalls, holds science back. Fridman, who opens by calling for all papers to be free to read, pushes for crowdsourced review at the scale of Amazon reviews.

## Key points

- **Origins of Fermat's Library.** João says it grew out of a journal club he, Luís and two friends ran across computer science and physics. It now offers a weekly annotated paper, the free Margins tool, and Librarian, a browser extension that adds comments and reference extraction to arXiv.
- **Broken publishing economics.** Luís describes governments paying for research, unpaid peer review and then buying the papers back. He says impact factors turn papers into "accounting units", push top journals such as Nature towards media-friendly claims and leave a long tail of journals scoring below two.
- **Preprints matter.** Luís notes some math papers take close to three years to appear in a journal, and says journal pressure ended an NIH biology preprint experiment in the 1960s; only about 2% of biology papers now appear first as preprints.
- **How to read and take notes.** One brother cites Feynman reading a conclusion and trying to derive it himself. Fridman prefers a breadth-first pass through conclusions and related-work sections. João says right after you understand something, you are the best teacher for your future self.
- **Favourite papers.** The brothers discuss Tom Apostol's paper on the 6th-century BC tunnel on Samos, Fermi's one-page Trinity estimate (one brother reapplied the method to a viral video from the Beirut explosion), the Bitcoin paper and Freeman Dyson's one-page Dyson sphere paper.
- **Startups and side projects.** João says startups lack a linear reward for effort. He says they run Fermat's Library as a side project with a 20-year horizon because some things, like arXiv, cannot be grown in three to five years.
