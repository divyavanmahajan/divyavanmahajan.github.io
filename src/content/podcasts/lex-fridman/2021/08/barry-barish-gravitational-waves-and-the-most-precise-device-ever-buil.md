## Thesis

Barry Barish, the Caltech physicist who shared the Nobel Prize for LIGO, walks Lex Fridman through the history of gravitational waves from Einstein's intuition to the 2015 detection of two colliding black holes. He argues that the hard part was largely engineering, and that because the remaining noise is technical rather than physical, better instruments will turn gravitational waves into a new kind of astronomy and eventually cosmology.

## Key points

- **A contested prediction.** Barish recounts how Einstein proposed gravitational waves in 1916 with a factor-of-two error, later doubted them in a 1936 paper he withdrew from Physical Review after a referee's critique, and how a 1958 conference, including a Feynman thought experiment, settled that the waves carry energy.
- **The measurement.** LIGO uses two interferometers with 4 km vacuum arms to detect a change of one part in 10^21, about 10^-18 metres or a thousandth of a proton's size. Barish calls it the world's biggest high-vacuum system, with leaks a constant worry.
- **Isolation as engineering.** Four layers of passive shock absorbers plus active cancellation, with seismometers and actuators working like noise-cancelling headphones, cut ground motion by one part in 10^12. Barish keeps insisting it is "just" mechanical engineering; Fridman, a roboticist, is less convinced.
- **Building and leading.** After NSF funding in 1994 and a first instrument by 1999, the team iterated for almost 10 years without a detection, then got roughly $100 million more for an upgrade. The 2015 signal matched Einstein's equations in both detectors; about a thousand collaborators kept quiet while the team ruled out instrument faults and even hacking.
- **What comes next.** Barish wants a next-generation detector 10 times more sensitive, covering a thousand times the volume, and describes the space-based LISA mission planned for the 2030s. He sees black holes as a possible bridge between quantum mechanics and general relativity, and doubts string theory's predictive power.
- **Beyond physics.** Barish says the Nobel Prize makes politicians listen to him, compares a Mars base to the South Pole station he visited, and says Moby Dick at 15 "cured" him of becoming a novelist.
