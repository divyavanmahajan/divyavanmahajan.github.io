## Thesis

Matt Walker, a Berkeley sleep scientist and author of Why We Sleep, argues that the question is no longer why we sleep but whether any physiological or mental system escapes damage without it; so far, he says, none does. Lex Fridman, who describes an irregular schedule of all-nighters and naps, pushes back that people chasing a dream should not be judged for sacrificing sleep. Walker agrees that it is a personal choice, as long as it is an informed one.

## Key points

- **Coffee versus caffeine.** Walker says coffee's health benefits come mostly from antioxidants in the bean (decaf shares them), not from caffeine. Evening caffeine can cut deep sleep by 10 to 30 percent. With a half-life of five to six hours, he suggests the average adult stop by about 2 pm. The CYP1A2 gene explains why people clear caffeine at different speeds.
- **Sleep and memory.** Walker describes four roles: preparing the brain to learn, saving new memories afterwards, linking them into new associations, and forgetting. He calls REM sleep "group therapy for memories" and cites Mendeleev, Paul McCartney and Keith Richards as people whose ideas came from dreams.
- **Drowsy driving.** Fridman, drawing on his MIT driver research, and Walker discuss detecting microsleeps through eyelid closure, pupil size and steering behaviour, ideally tuned to each driver's well-rested baseline.
- **Insomnia.** Walker recommends cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). His own approach: make sense of the stress behind it, keep the same schedule after a bad night (no naps, no lie-in), meditate, and wind down before bed.
- **Fasting and REM sleep.** Ramadan studies show less melatonin, less total and REM sleep, and more of the wake-promoting chemical orexin. Walker says fasting makes people feel more alert because the brain reads it as starvation, not because they need less sleep.
- **Dreams and mood.** Walker calls dreaming "emotional first aid" and says what you dream about matters, citing maze-learning and divorce-depression studies. He says sleep loss hits positive emotions hardest and that no psychiatric condition has been found in which sleep is normal.
