## Thesis

Michael Mina, on his second appearance, tells Lex Fridman that rapid antigen tests are the most practical tool for controlling COVID-19 because they quickly answer whether someone is infectious. He argues the US lacks them at scale because the FDA judges them as medical devices against PCR, and that a presidential designation of "public health tools" with CDC evaluation would fix this. Fridman agrees and frames testing as one of the few non-politicised solutions.

## Key points

- **Infectiousness, not molecules.** Mina says PCR can detect a single molecule and stays positive for 30 to 70 days, while people are infectious for roughly three to seven. Rapid tests are about 97% sensitive for infectious people and near 100% for likely super spreaders, he argues.
- **Wrong regulatory yardstick.** Mina says the FDA's in vitro diagnostics division has no public health pathway, so companies skew trials to match PCR. He contrasts this with unregulated pooled PCR testing of schoolchildren (citing Ginkgo) and with the UK, which evaluated rapid tests outside its medical regulators within weeks.
- **Cost and dynamic use.** Mina estimates manufacturing at around 50 cents per test, with tests selling for 80 cents in Germany, where 60 to 70 companies are authorised. He proposes household test supplies triggered by wastewater signals, with the goal of getting R below one rather than perfect compliance.
- **Verification and privacy.** Mina describes proctored at-home testing via eMed, accepted by the CDC for US re-entry, and supports letting individuals opt out of public health reporting so distrustful people still test.
- **Biden action plan.** Mina calls the plan's 280 million tests and $2 billion an encouraging step but less than one test per person per year. He objects to "vaccinate or test" framing because, in his view, vaccines mainly protect against severe illness rather than transmission, and he warns it could politicise testing.
- **Beyond COVID.** Mina recounts life as a Buddhist monk until the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, and voices concern about American institutions' response to pandemics and climate change; Fridman takes a more optimistic view.
