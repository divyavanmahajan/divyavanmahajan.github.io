## Thesis

Jimmy Pedro, a four-time Olympian, 1999 world champion and coach of Kayla Harrison and Travis Stevens, tells Lex Fridman that the US lacks the deep talent pools of Japan and Europe, so its champions have to be built: a clear goal written down, a gripping-heavy American system, constant competition abroad and mental preparation through visualization. He adds that champions need to hate losing more than they like winning.

## Key points

- **An American system.** Pedro describes Japan's free-flowing, technical randori, Europe's physical grip fighting and Russia's sequential drilling. The US system perfects each athlete's main throw and builds combinations off the same grip, then adds gripping, groundwork and conditioning. Short rotations ("shark bait") make up for having few good training partners.
- **Visualization.** Pedro says athletes should live out the whole competition day in their heads, from weigh-in to the podium, until the real moment feels familiar. He credits his father with the physical preparation and himself with building confidence.
- **Defining moments.** Pedro calls his bronze medal at Atlanta 1996 the moment that defined his career. The crowd chanted "USA" while he trailed a Brazilian, and he won with uchi mata. He says Stevens cried for 45 minutes after losing to Ole Bischof in 2012, then took silver in Rio after beating the world number one. Harrison lost both of her matches at the Starrett Cup in January 2010 and was world champion ten months later.
- **Life after gold.** Pedro says Harrison's post-Olympic depression came as she lost her purpose and income at once: about $72,000 a year from USA Judo, her stipends and her sponsorship all stopped when she retired.
- **Olympic footage.** Fridman objects that the IOC keeps match video private. Pedro blames TV rights, and says he was asked for about $30,000, later negotiated to $15,000, to use a few minutes of his own Olympic footage.
- **Running a dojo.** After consultants' advice, Pedro split classes by level and in 2006 set a goal of a 200-plus member judo-only club with Olympic champions, reached by 2012. He recommends judo for children and jiu-jitsu for most adult beginners.
