## Thesis

Kelsi Sheren, who deployed to Afghanistan with the Canadian artillery at 19, tells Lex Fridman how one IED blast during an operation with British troops broke her emotionally and led to a decade of severe PTSD. She argues that militaries prepare soldiers physically but not mentally, then fall back on medication and discharge. Her own recovery came through long-term treatment, her husband's support, purposeful work and, eventually, a psychedelic retreat.

## Key points

- **Fast-tracked into war.** Sheren says she joined in November 2007, finished training by September 2008 and was posted to a French-speaking unit, where she had to relearn every weapon system in French. She describes artillery work on the M777 155mm howitzer as a coordinated "symphony" of a five-to-seven-person crew.
- **The IED.** Lent to British units as a female searcher, she watched a young soldier killed by an IED hidden in a grape hut. She describes collecting his remains under fire and says the moment flipped a "light switch" in her, followed by intense hatred of the enemy.
- **Medicated, not supported.** Sheren says she was sent back to the guns heavily medicated without her sergeant being told, and argues that admitting struggle ends a career. She calls for meditation, yoga, peer support and protection for soldiers who report symptoms.
- **Recovery.** She describes years of daily suicidal thoughts, cannabis prescribed by her doctor Greg Passey to taper off pharmaceuticals, art therapy that grew into her company Brass and Unity, and an ayahuasca retreat with the charity Heroic Hearts in January 2021.
- **Afghanistan withdrawal.** Sheren criticises the 2021 pullout, especially the Canadian government's handling, and describes helping evacuate a nine-person family. Fridman stays more sympathetic to political leaders, suggesting they are surrounded by "yes people".
- **Giving back.** She says Brass and Unity sends profits to groups such as Honour House and the All Secure Foundation, and cites over 22 veteran suicides a day.
