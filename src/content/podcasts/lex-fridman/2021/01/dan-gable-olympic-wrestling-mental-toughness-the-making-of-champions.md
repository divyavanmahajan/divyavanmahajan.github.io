## Thesis

Dan Gable, the 1972 Olympic champion and longtime Iowa coach, tells Lex Fridman in his home that winning at the highest level means joining relentless toughness and conditioning with the technical science he credits to Russian wrestling. He argues that losses and tragedies are never fully overcome, but can be turned into purpose, and that luck is mostly made through preparation. The conversation was recorded two years before Fridman published it on the podcast.

## Key points

- **Toughness plus science.** Gable says Russians lead in technique and strategy but do not push conditioning, while Americans do the opposite. After 181 straight wins, his college loss to Larry Owings pushed him to study the Russians, and he points to Dave Schultz as the American who showed the value of the artistic side.
- **Coaching by example.** Gable says a coach must be first in and last out, outwork the athletes and read each one individually, often learning the most in relaxed settings like the sauna after practice. He believes this has helped him save a couple of athletes' lives.
- **The 1972 Olympics.** Gable outscored opponents 57-0 in the 68 kg freestyle event. He says his confidence came from three and a half years of winning in practice and lying awake until he had solved any takedown he gave up.
- **Fear and anger.** Gable says eliminating fear of fatigue gave him an edge, and that anger beyond control causes mistakes. He cites Royce Alger and the Brands brothers as wrestling to the edge but stopping at the whistle.
- **Loss and family.** Gable speaks about his sister's murder in 1964, when he was 15, and the guilt of having heard a warning sign. He says it shaped how he lives and holds his family together. He also describes weeping when the IOC voted wrestling out of the Olympics in 2013, and blames the sport for having no voice inside the IOC.
- **Making luck.** Discussing Hemingway's The Old Man and the Sea, Gable says luck comes mostly from preparation. He cites writer John Irving and Norman Borlaug as former wrestlers who credited the sport for their discipline.
