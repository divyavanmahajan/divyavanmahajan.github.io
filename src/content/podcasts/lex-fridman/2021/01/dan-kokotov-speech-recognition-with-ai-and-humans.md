## Thesis

Dan Kokotov, VP of Engineering at Rev.ai, argues that the hardest part of automatic speech recognition (ASR) is data, and that Rev's human transcription business gives it a natural advantage: customers pay it to produce the labelled data its models learn from. Lex Fridman, a Rev customer, adds a theme of his own: good products come from teams who love what they build, not from teams chasing a metric.

## Key points

- **Simplifying the freelance marketplace.** Kokotov says Rev's CEO was an early Upwork employee and founded Rev to remove the effort of picking freelancers. Rev began with translation of standard documents such as birth certificates, then added transcription. As a two-sided marketplace, it must balance customer demand against available work for its transcribers.
- **Humans correct the machine.** Transcribers start from an ASR draft in a purpose-built editor. Kokotov estimates that even on good audio a skilled transcriber needs two to three times the audio's length. He dislikes the word "gig" and describes the workers as parents, students and others who want flexible hours.
- **Accuracy numbers.** Kokotov reports about a 14% word error rate on Rev's internal test suite. He puts realistic human accuracy at 2-3%, which is the long-term goal, and says Rev beats Google, Amazon and Microsoft in its own tests. Human transcription costs $1.25 a minute; the self-serve Temi product costs about 25 cents.
- **Untapped signal in edits.** Kokotov says Rev trains on final transcripts and audio but has not yet used the edits transcribers make. Fridman compares this to polygon-correction data in driving annotation.
- **Searchable audio.** Kokotov pictures indexed, shareable archives of company meetings. Fridman wants Spotify or the wider ecosystem to make podcasts searchable; Kokotov mentions a Buzzsprout proposal for a standard transcript extension to podcast RSS. Fridman criticises exclusive deals because they cut podcasts off from open RSS.
- **Management.** Kokotov describes missing the clear feedback of coding after he moved into management. Rev gives new managers "First, Break All the Rules" and its idea of managing by exception. He also recommends Ken Kocienda's "Creative Selection".
