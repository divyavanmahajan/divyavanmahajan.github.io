## Thesis

Risto Miikkulainen, a computer scientist at UT Austin and associate vice president of evolutionary AI at Cognizant, tells Lex Fridman that evolutionary algorithms can be creative: they produce solutions that are new, useful and surprising because they do not share human assumptions. He sees intelligence as rooted in social cooperation, and argues that evolution and neural networks work best together.

## Key points

- **Basil does not need to sleep.** In a collaboration with the MIT Media Lab's hydroponic "food computer", Miikkulainen says evolution pushed every light recipe to the six-hours-of-darkness limit the team had set. With the limit removed, basil grew bigger and tastier under 24-hour light, which surprised the biologists too.
- **Exploiting loopholes.** In a student tic-tac-toe tournament, an evolved network won by playing moves millions of squares away, so rival programs ran out of memory and crashed.
- **How it works.** Variation plus selection on an encoded genotype. Miikkulainen notes biology relies mostly on crossover, while computation leans on statistically guided mutation, and says the field is impatient compared with nature. It also has yet to model major transitions such as single cells becoming multicellular organisms.
- **Evolution versus RL and deep learning.** Deep learning suits labelled data. Reinforcement learning suits costly trials, while population methods can afford failures. Evolved walkers fall flat many times before discovering fast, controlled running.
- **Neuroevolution today.** Evolution can tune deep learning architectures, activation functions and loss functions. He reports a 15 percent improvement on a winning image-captioning design, and says multitask learning works even across unrelated tasks.
- **Social simulations.** Simulated hyenas learned to team up to steal kills from lions, and a predator-prey arms race with zebras emerged. He says a society can tolerate some cheaters, and that diversity is essential.
