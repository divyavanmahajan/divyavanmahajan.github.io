## Thesis

Yannis Pappas, comedian and former co-host of History Hyenas, tells Lex Fridman that a good life rests on accepting reality, above all that nothing is permanent. He reads history the same way: power reveals rather than creates a person's darkness, charm is the con artist's main tool, and history is written by the victors. Fridman pushes back on mortality, arguing with Ernest Becker that fear of death, not acceptance, is the more honest response.

## Key points

- **Power and mortality.** Pappas says power reveals people rather than corrupting them, and credits his late father, who lived to 91, with the advice to survive emotionally by not taking ephemeral things too seriously. Fridman cites Becker's The Denial of Death and the Stoic habit of meditating on death daily.
- **Dogs and hyenas.** Pappas argues humans could not have moved from hunter-gatherer to agrarian life without dogs. He prefers hyenas to lions for their cunning, matriarchal packs and bone-crushing bite.
- **Battle of Crete.** Pappas's mother lived through the 1941 airborne invasion of Crete and four years of Nazi occupation. He says Greece's defeat of Italy and Crete's ten-day resistance delayed Hitler's invasion of Russia; Fridman adds that historians also stress the psychological impact.
- **History Hyenas.** Pappas calls the show's chemistry with co-host Chris unplanned and "musical", and says ending it after about three years was right. He contrasts it with Dan Carlin's accuracy and argues podcasts push listeners to learn more.
- **Con men and charisma.** Pappas calls Bernie Madoff the greatest thief, using exclusivity, availability and steady returns to hide a pyramid scheme. Fridman suggests Jeffrey Epstein worked similarly, flattering outsider scientists at places like MIT and Harvard.
- **New York and advice.** Pappas, who says he leans left, argues New York is a "money town" needing a tougher, business-minded mayor; Fridman praises Andrew Yang's data-driven style, including his figure of over $400,000 a year per prisoner in New York. Pappas recounts his first panicked stand-up set and calls love an action, not a feeling.
