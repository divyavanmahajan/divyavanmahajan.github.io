## Thesis

Peter Woit, a theoretical physicist and mathematician at Columbia and author of the blog Not Even Wrong, tells Lex Fridman that the deepest ideas in physics and mathematics use the same structures, and that unification should follow them in four dimensions rather than adding extra ones. He argues that string theory failed, that its practitioners have not admitted this publicly, and that overselling it damages trust in science.

## Key points

- **Maths and physics converge.** Woit points to the Langlands programme, Edward Frenkel's book Love and Math and a recent paper by Peter Scholze and Laurent Fargues as signs that unification in mathematics uses the same objects as unification in physics.
- **Why string theory failed.** Woit explains that strings need ten dimensions, so six must be shrunk into Calabi-Yau spaces. There turned out to be so many ways to do this that the theory predicts nothing testable. He credits it with useful mathematics such as two-dimensional conformal field theory.
- **Witten and the Nobel.** Woit calls Ed Witten a remarkable figure whose best, Fields Medal-winning work has little to do with string theory. He says no one has improved on the 1973 standard model, whose Higgs sector needs 20 to 30 parameters.
- **Other theories of everything.** Woit says he has almost no sympathy for Stephen Wolfram's cellular automata approach. He says Eric Weinstein's and Garrett Lisi's E8 models cannot explain how their large symmetries break down to what we observe.
- **Twistors.** Woit is now working on Roger Penrose's twistor theory, which only works in four dimensions and treats light rays rather than points as basic. In it, spinors, the two-complex-number objects that describe electrons, come out naturally. Treating imaginary time as fundamental gives him structures that resemble the standard model.
- **Outlook and responsibility.** Woit expects 20 more years of LHC data at the same energy and no Nobel for string theory. He says selling failed ideas as successes misleads top graduate students and erodes trust in science.
