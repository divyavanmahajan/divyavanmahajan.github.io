## Thesis

Ben Askren, a two-time NCAA champion who went 19-0 in MMA before losing to Jorge Masvidal, says champions must balance hating to lose with seeking out the hardest opponents. He credits his own rise to obsessive, creative problem-solving rather than athleticism, and now applies that thinking as a coach at his Wisconsin wrestling academy.

## Key points

- **Losses in context.** Askren says the Masvidal flying knee was "one move", that an injured hip limited him, and that Dana White had refused him a UFC offer in 2013. He took the Jake Paul boxing match for money and fun, and the Jordan Burroughs exhibition to raise money for Beat the Streets.
- **How he got good.** He was not good until about 13, quit baseball and football to wrestle more, and went 40-10 as a redshirt freshman before reaching the NCAA final the next year. Struggling in the room pushed him toward creative scrambling; by his senior year, figures wrestled in his head and produced new moves, which John Smith told him had happened to him too.
- **Drilling versus sparring.** Askren and Fridman disagree: Askren thinks drilling past basic proficiency adds little and favours graded sparring (for example 70 percent effort against 40), while Fridman argues for heavy drilling on a cooperative partner. Askren also criticises jiu-jitsu scoring for not rewarding near-submissions.
- **Robots and AI.** Fridman describes reinforcement learning and AlphaZero's piece sacrifices; Askren argues wrestling is far harder than chess because forces, angles and timing are continuous and decided in an instant.
- **Wrestlers in MMA.** Askren attributes wrestling's MMA success to discipline (showing up on time, working hard), 1,500 to 2,000 matches of experience by age 20, and openness to learning jiu-jitsu, which he says rarely runs the other way.
- **Bitcoin and advice.** He backs Bitcoin for remittances and the unbanked, citing Western Union fees he saw in Singapore, and tells young people to find a wise coach and reuse whatever recipe made them succeed once.
