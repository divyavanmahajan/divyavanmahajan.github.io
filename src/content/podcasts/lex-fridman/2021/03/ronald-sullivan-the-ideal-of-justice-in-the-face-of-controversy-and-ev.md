## Thesis

Ronald Sullivan, a Harvard law professor who has represented Harvey Weinstein and Aaron Hernandez, tells Lex Fridman that everyone, however unpopular or evil they are seen to be, deserves a competent defence, because the treatment of such clients sets the rights of all citizens. He argues that Harvard was wrong not to renew his faculty dean post after he joined Weinstein's defence team, and that universities should challenge students through civil debate.

## Key points

- **Why take unpopular cases.** Sullivan points to Ernesto Miranda, a man he describes as unlikeable, whose case produced the Miranda warnings. He says his wrongful-conviction work has freed over 6,000 people.
- **The Harvard dispute.** Sullivan calls the decision by deans Claudine Gay and Rakesh Khurana "craven" and says he refused to resign. He believes a student survey Harvard commissioned but never released would have favoured him, and blames a "consumerization" of education.
- **Safe spaces and cancel culture.** Sullivan says the presumption should be that hard topics belong at a university, with a line drawn at uncivil discourse, not at controversial ideas. Fridman argues universities lack ways to humble students with ideas.
- **Evil and process.** Sullivan believes evil exists but is shaped by circumstance, describing a "constellation of bad choices" many people face. He would defend anyone as a public defender, though in private practice he would likely pass on a hypothetical Hitler case unless no one else would take it.
- **Race and juries.** Sullivan cites Black Americans as 12 percent of the population but 40 percent of drug charges. On the Derek Chauvin trial, then in jury selection, he explains that "unbiased" means a juror can set opinions aside, not one who knows nothing about the case.
- **O.J. Simpson and Hernandez.** Sullivan says Johnny Cochran showed that trials are about storytelling. He believes Hernandez did not commit the double murder he was acquitted of, and says the suicide shocked him.
