## Thesis

Natalya Bailey, an MIT-trained propulsion engineer and co-founder and CTO of Accion Systems, walks Lex Fridman through how chemical and electric rocket engines work and why electric propulsion is the efficient choice once a spacecraft is in orbit. She argues that the industry is being driven hard on cost, and that bringing silicon chip manufacturing to thrusters is how a startup competes. She also sees the meaning of human life in generating and preserving knowledge.

## Key points

- **Two kinds of propulsion.** Bailey says all rockets rely on conservation of momentum. Chemical engines give large instantaneous thrust for launch but are fuel-inefficient; electric engines push charged particles out with electric or magnetic fields and are far more fuel-efficient, but are less well understood.
- **Colloid thrusters on a chip.** Accion's engine draws ions from ionic liquids that form cones about 20 nanometres across at the tip. Its one-square-centimetre thruster chips carry about 500 emitters, a number Bailey says the company aims to quadruple.
- **What satellites need thrust for.** Bailey lists station-keeping against gravity perturbations, debris avoidance (about three manoeuvres a year for some small satellites, mostly planned by people on the ground), changing orbit and end-of-life disposal. She sees tens of thousands of satellites ahead, mostly driven by communications.
- **Cost pressure.** Bailey says ion engines historically cost 20-25 million dollars, while customers now want them for around 10,000. Fridman worries that cost-cutting leaves no room to innovate; Bailey says unconventional, mass-producible manufacturing is the answer and sees SpaceX as a future customer, not a competitor.
- **Speculative propulsion.** She calls propellant-free propulsion the ultimate goal but notes the EM drive was debunked when a reversed test device produced thrust in the same direction. She says better power sources, possibly nuclear, would unlock stronger electric engines.
- **Founder advice.** Bailey stresses an inspiring vision, deep first-principles understanding, a genuine network and early attention to culture. She says the name Accion came from "accio", the summoning charm in Harry Potter.
