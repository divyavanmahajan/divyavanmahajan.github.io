## Thesis

Zev Weinstein, Eric Weinstein's teenage son, tells Lex Fridman that philosophy, which he defines broadly as any deep thinking including technological innovation, becomes dangerous when growth stalls, because people then fight over existing resources and those in power resist new ideas. He argues that radical, first-principles thinking is nonetheless the only escape from stagnation, and that his generation has an obligation to think in public and change its mind openly.

## Key points

- **Stagnation and danger.** Weinstein cites Socrates' execution and the Communist Manifesto in early 1900s Russia as examples of ideas becoming riskier in desperate times. He calls modern scientific stagnation "undeniable", but unlike his father he is not sure that the end of the growth period is inevitable.
- **Labels and language.** Weinstein argues that labels corrupt abstractions, and that the word "radical" has shifted from meaning "the root of a problem" to meaning "extreme". Fridman describes being labelled a Republican or Trump supporter for saying he loves America.
- **Philosophers.** Weinstein admires Thomas Aquinas for bringing elements of scientific reasoning into a dark period without offending the powers of his day. He disagrees with Nietzsche's immoralism and proposes that good is a proxy for a civilisation's stability, so good outlasts evil unless everything is destroyed.
- **Mediums and free will.** Weinstein suggests people prefer podcasts to books because speech evolved while writing was invented. On free will, he argues that decisions are legitimate even if a complete computer could predict them.
- **Thinkers and doers.** He names Einstein, Newton and Feynman as making life meaningful, and Kary Mullis as making it possible, and calls Elon Musk "radically individualistic".
- **His father and music.** Weinstein credits his father with helping him accept being an idiosyncratic thinker, recalling being given a Tom Lehrer CD at about five. He learned music theory before touching an instrument, which taught him that almost anything can be self-taught.
