## Thesis

Zach Bitter, a former 100-mile world record holder and running coach, tells Lex Fridman that ultramarathon success rests on a large aerobic foundation, careful pacing and a clear reason for running that holds when the urge to quit arrives. He presents his low-carbohydrate diet as something that works for him, not a universal rule. Fridman presses him on whether "never quit" should be a life philosophy; Bitter says it helps in races but can backfire in training.

## Key points

- **Quitting and the big picture.** Bitter says that since 2019 he frames a bad moment mid-race as part of months of preparation, which makes dropping out harder to justify. He advises first-time 100-milers to finish even if that means what ultrarunners call "death marching" to the end.
- **The 11-hour barrier.** Bitter ran 100 miles on a track in 11:19 (about 6:47 per mile), a record since broken by Aleksandr Sorokin in roughly 11:15. He expects someone to break 11 hours within a few years.
- **Aerobic base first.** Bitter builds about 80% of training around aerobic threshold work, endorses Phil Maffetone's "180 minus your age" heart-rate formula as a good starting point, and then adds short intervals and race-pace work.
- **Diet as individual.** Bitter has eaten low-carb for about 10 years and says his best season, 2019, came when his meat intake was highest. In races he takes 15-40 grams of carbohydrate per hour while burning mostly fat, but he says some clients do better with more carbs.
- **Treadmill and shoes.** His March 2020 treadmill 100-miler felt frustrating because the belt removed small pace adjustments. He says Nike's foam and carbon-plate shoes give a 2-8% advantage, raising fairness questions, as with Kara Goucher's fourth place at the Olympic trials.
- **Transcontinental run.** Bitter plans to run from San Francisco to New York from 1 September for Justin Wren's Fight for the Forgotten. The record, held by Pete Kostelnick, is 42 days 6 hours at about 72 miles a day.
