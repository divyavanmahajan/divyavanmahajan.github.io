## Thesis

Donald Knuth, author of The Art of Computer Programming and creator of TeX, tells Lex Fridman that the lasting value in computing comes from understanding: programs written so people can read them, optimisation guided by measurement, and theory that improves everyday programming. He is wary of tools whose output "seems to work" without anyone knowing why, while Fridman takes the more optimistic view of automation.

## Key points

- **First programs.** Knuth recalls programming the IBM 650 in decimal machine language in 1957: a factoring program, then a tic-tac-toe program whose learning "brain" converged to a safe draw after about 600 games. He says the 650 manual was so badly written that he figured he could write a better one.
- **Literate programming.** Knuth calls it the most significant result of the TeX project and points to the Academy Award-winning book Physically Based Rendering by Matt Pharr as a full literate program.
- **Premature optimisation.** He explains the famous line: programmers guess wrongly where time goes. In one study a Fortran compiler spent more than 80% of its time reading comment cards, a fix worth a 2x speed-up once measured.
- **AI code tools.** When Fridman describes OpenAI Codex and GitHub Copilot, Knuth worries that society is losing control and understanding of what machines do, especially where decisions affect people's lives. Fridman counters that automation frees humans for the parts that need genius.
- **Hardest problem and KMP.** Knuth names "the birth of the giant component" in random graphs as the hardest problem he worked on; spotting that 23023 factors as 7 x 11 x 13 x 23 unlocked it. He also tells how Knuth-Morris-Pratt came from three independent discoveries, his via Steve Cook's stack automaton theorem.
- **TeX, Conway and life advice.** He released TeX freely because proprietary typesetting languages held the industry back and his income was already "over the threshold". He recounts writing Surreal Numbers in a week from John Conway's napkin theory, and advises young people to pursue what they feel called to do rather than what is trendy.
