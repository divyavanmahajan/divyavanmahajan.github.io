## Thesis

Jo Boaler, professor of mathematics education at Stanford and co-founder of youcubed, tells Lex Fridman that schools teach maths as one method and one answer, when it is better taught as a creative subject seen in many ways: visually, with numbers, words and physical models. She argues that struggle is good for learning, but only when students believe they can succeed, and that teachers' and parents' messages shape that belief. Fridman adds his own experience of the Soviet system, which valued excellence and assumed everyone could reach it through hard work.

## Key points

- **Multi-dimensional maths.** Boaler cites neuroscience showing five brain pathways involved in maths, with high achievers having more connections between them. She points to Fields Medallist Maryam Mirzakhani, whose work was entirely visual, and to "groupitizing" (seeing groups of dots) as a predictor of maths success.
- **Messages matter.** A study found students whose essay feedback added "because I believe in you" did better a year later. Another found parents' maths anxiety predicted children's achievement, but only when they helped with homework. Boaler says many children give up on maths around fifth grade.
- **Big ideas over standards.** As one of five writers of a new California maths framework, Boaler argues for teaching a few big ideas with rich activities instead of dozens of fragmented standards, and for rubrics rather than constant grading.
- **Collaboration.** In a four-week Stanford calculus course in 2019, undergraduates who first resisted group work learned to collaborate, and collaborators scored better on the final problem. Fridman links this to his conversation with Daniel Kahneman.
- **youcubed and online learning.** A free MOOC taken by 30,000 teachers led to youcubed, which Boaler says has had over 52 million visitors. Her short "How to Learn Maths" class for children left them 68% more engaged in randomised trials.
- **Change is slow but real.** Boaler notes data science can now replace Algebra 2 in some US high schools, with the UC system accepting it and about 12 states allowing it.
