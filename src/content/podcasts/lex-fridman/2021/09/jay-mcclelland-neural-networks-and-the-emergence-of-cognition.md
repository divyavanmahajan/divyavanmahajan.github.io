## Thesis

Jay McClelland, co-author of the Parallel Distributed Processing books with David Rumelhart, tells Lex Fridman that neural networks link biology to the mysteries of thought. He describes himself as a radical emergentist connectionist: higher-level cognition such as concepts or mathematical reasoning is real, but it emerges from connection weights between simple units rather than being stored as symbols. He also argues that much of what experts treat as innate, including formal reasoning and the natural numbers, is learned through cultural immersion.

## Key points

- **From experts to units.** McClelland recalls Rumelhart's interactive model of reading, where "experts" for letters, words and meanings update each other. After reading Geoffrey Hinton's dissertation and James Anderson's linear-algebra models, the two replaced those experts with neuron-like units in the interactive activation model of letter perception.
- **Origin of backprop.** McClelland says Hinton told the PDP group, which also drew in Francis Crick and Paul Smolensky, to stop looking to biology for learning rules and instead adjust weights to solve a defined problem. Rumelhart then generalised the Widrow-Hoff delta rule to hidden layers, which became backpropagation and beat Hinton's Boltzmann machine approach.
- **Semantic dementia.** McClelland describes patients who lose distinctions between concepts, calling every middle-sized animal a dog, which he explains with degrading distributed representations. Rumelhart himself later suffered such a condition; McClelland recalls him still able to bowl and point to his order on a menu without naming it.
- **Mathematical cognition.** McClelland sees mathematics as tools for exploring idealised worlds, and wants models where intuition drives discovery and logic supplies proof. He finds hope in large text models and AlphaZero-style systems.
- **Expert blind spot.** He suggests formally trained academics, Noam Chomsky included, have intuitions shaped by their training, citing Lila Gleitman's finding that linguists read phrases differently from lay people.
- **Career and psychiatry.** McClelland traces his path from the 1968 Columbia protests to cognitive psychology. He says a biology-only direction at the US National Institute of Mental Health around 2000 proved, in his view, a dead end.
