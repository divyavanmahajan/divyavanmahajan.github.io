## Thesis

Jeffrey Shainline, a physicist at NIST (speaking for himself, not NIST), tells Lex Fridman that electrons are good at computation and light is good at communication, and that a brain-like computer needs both. He argues that superconducting circuits at 4 kelvin make this combination practical in a way room-temperature silicon does not. He then extends Lee Smolin's cosmological natural selection to propose that the universe may be tuned to allow technology.

## Key points

- **Why silicon won.** Shainline credits silicon's native oxide gate insulator and its 1.1 electron-volt band gap (versus about 0.75 for germanium) for its dominance, and argues that Moore's law scaling down to seven-nanometre features was made possible by physics.
- **Superconductors are not a silicon replacement.** Josephson junctions can switch in tens of picoseconds and run in the hundreds of gigahertz, but he says loop sizes cannot shrink like transistors and liquid-helium cooling rules them out of phones. He does not expect them to replace semiconductors for digital logic.
- **Light for fan-out.** A neuron makes roughly 10,000 connections; driving electrons down that many wires costs capacitance and power, whereas photons in waveguides do not interfere and branch without that penalty. Because superconducting detectors register a single photon, he says even poor light sources, including silicon at 4 kelvin, could be good enough.
- **Loop neurons.** His group's design stores synaptic weights and signals as current in superconducting loops, built from one leaky-integrator building block. A simplified model gives a speed-up of a thousand to ten thousand times in simulation, targeting millions of neurons. Reaching human-brain scale (about 10 billion neurons) would mean stacking layers and whole wafers linked by fibre.
- **Science before products.** Shainline frames the work as a study of the physical limits of cognition rather than a machine learning business; colleagues such as Mike Schneider are exploring fast superconducting classifiers. He sees possible data-centre uses, while Fridman raises Tesla's Dojo.
- **Technology and cosmology.** Shainline estimates that turning one kilometre-wide asteroid into 10-kilogram black holes would yield at least a trillion offspring universes, beating star formation by about three orders of magnitude. He leans towards intelligent civilisations being rare.
