## Thesis

Vincent Racaniello, a Columbia virologist and host of This Week in Virology, tells Lex Fridman that science already knows how to respond to new viruses with vaccines, antivirals, masks and rapid tests, but that funding dried up after SARS and the COVID-19 response repeated old mistakes. Fridman's main concern is trust: he argues that leaders who spoke with unearned certainty damaged public confidence in science.

## Key points

- **Biology as a data problem.** Racaniello says about 90% of sequences from environmental samples match nothing in the GenBank database. He expects structure prediction such as DeepMind's AlphaFold to help classify this "dark matter", and says experimental virologists need to work with machine learning people.
- **Vaccine platforms.** Racaniello traces the shift from egg-grown inactivated vaccines (flu shots reach about 60% efficacy at best) to viral vectors and mRNA. He admits he expected mRNA to be too fragile and was wrong. He credits the lipid formulations Moderna and Pfizer spent years refining, and predicts viral vectors will treat most cancers within 10 to 15 years.
- **Preparedness gaps.** He says SARS vaccines and antivirals stalled because industry and NIH saw no market or need. Molnupiravir was developed five years before the pandemic but never tested in people. He wants two or three antivirals used together to prevent resistance, and says early trials failed because they treated patients who were already in hospital.
- **Testing.** Both back Michael Mina's idea of daily antigen tests costing under a dollar. Racaniello calls the CDC's first test a fiasco and says 100 companies should have been making tests.
- **Trust and communication.** Fridman criticizes Anthony Fauci's communication as inauthentic and overconfident. Racaniello says the trial data are public, but faults the CDC and WHO for never admitting their early advice against masks was a mistake. He argues for presenting risks and benefits calmly and letting people decide.
- **Repurposed drugs.** Racaniello, who discloses that his wife worked on ivermectin at Merck, says ivermectin should be tested properly in a large randomized trial. He says hydroxychloroquine looked promising only because the lab studies used kidney cells.
