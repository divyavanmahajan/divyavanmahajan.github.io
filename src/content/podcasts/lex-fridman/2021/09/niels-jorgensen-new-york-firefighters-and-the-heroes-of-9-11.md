## Thesis

Niels Jorgensen, a New York firefighter for over 21 years who worked at Ground Zero, tells Lex Fridman what he saw on September 11, 2001 and in the years after, including the rare leukemia that ended his career. He argues that the heroes of that day were defined by putting their team and strangers first, and that responders were later let down by politicians and institutions. Fridman closes by urging listeners to remember not only the attack but the unity of September 12.

## Key points

- **The day itself.** Jorgensen was off duty, driving an oil truck, when the planes hit. Following his father's and wife's advice to answer the recall at his firehouse, he reached the site after Ladder 114 had gone in; his childhood best friend John Shard and many colleagues were killed.
- **Stephen Siller.** He tells the story of the off-duty firefighter who ran through the closed Brooklyn Battery Tunnel in full gear and was never seen again. The Tunnel to Towers foundation in his name has, Jorgensen says, raised $250 million to house Gold Star families and wounded responders and veterans.
- **Illness and indifference.** Jorgensen describes useless paper masks and toxic dust, a 2011 diagnosis with roughly 48 hours to live, and fire department doctors who assumed he was drinking. He says he was the seventh rescuer in six months diagnosed with the same rare leukemia, and was later sued for $125,000 in hospital bills.
- **Fighting for coverage.** He credits John Feal, Ray Pfeifer, Luis Alvarez and Jon Stewart with shaming Congress into covering 9/11 illnesses, and says autoimmune diseases are still excluded.
- **Contested topics.** Jorgensen says he believes the towers and Building 7 fell from prolonged fire, not a plot. On Afghanistan, he favours targeted strikes and investment at home over long deployments; Fridman raises the war's cost and civilian deaths.
- **9/12 and kindness.** Through stories from his 20 for 20 podcast, such as engineer Mark Hanna, he says the common thread is selflessness and faith.
