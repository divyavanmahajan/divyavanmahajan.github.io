## Thesis

David Sinclair, a Harvard genetics professor and author of Lifespan, tells Lex Fridman that aging can be treated as an engineering problem: in his view it comes down to cells losing information to noise over time. He argues that cheap sensors, home tests and AI-driven analysis will give individuals better health data than their doctors have, and that economics will force hospitals and insurers to adopt them. Fridman largely agrees and presses on data sharing and privacy.

## Key points

- **Aging as information loss.** Sinclair compares the genome to the data on a DVD and the epigenome to the reader; aging is like scratches on the disc. His lab reported in Nature (December 2020) a way to partially reset cell age in mice, and he says human studies could start within two years.
- **Wearables and the hospital business case.** Sinclair shows a stick-on "bio button" sensor and says sending cardiac patients home with such devices saves hospitals at least $2,000 per patient. With the US spending about 17% of GDP on healthcare, he expects CFOs and insurers to push adoption, and predicts lawsuits once not monitoring becomes negligent.
- **Consumer health data.** Sinclair, who consults for InsideTracker, describes more than a decade of his own blood-test data and a doctor who could not order an HbA1c test without symptoms. He says his lab is working to bring home tests down to about a dollar, licensed to a consumer company.
- **AI in the lab.** Sinclair says his lab uses machine learning for biological clocks, protein folding and diagnostics, and he struggles to hire enough bioinformaticians as data volumes reach terabytes per week.
- **Privacy and control.** Fridman says he would share his data widely if he could always delete it; Sinclair calls healthcare data security his top concern and suggests users could choose to show insurers data in return for discounts.
- **Lifestyle and lifespan.** Sinclair describes his own one-meal-a-day habit and sees no fixed upper limit on human lifespan. Fridman questions whether death gives life meaning; Sinclair says his own joy does not depend on mortality.
