## Thesis

Anna Frebel, an astrophysicist at MIT, tells Lex Fridman that she does not need to look deep into space to study the early universe. Small, long-lived stars keep the chemical composition of the gas they formed from, so the most metal-poor stars in the Milky Way's outskirts work as a record of the first billion years. Her job, she says, is to read that chemistry and work out the events that produced it.

## Key points

- **The first stars.** Frebel says the Big Bang left only hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. The first stars, around 100 solar masses, exploded within a few million years, and their carbon and oxygen let gas cool enough to form small stars that still shine today.
- **Second-generation stars.** HE 1327-2326 was the second known star enriched by a single first star. It has very little iron but about 1,000 times more carbon. Frebel says Japanese theorists explained this with a "fallback" supernova in which a black hole swallows the inner iron.
- **Uranium dating and the r-process.** For the red giant HE 1523-0901 she measured thorium and uranium and estimated an age of 13.2 billion years. She is open that this was an average of ratios that gave anything from 10 to 15 billion years.
- **Reticulum II.** In 2016 she and her student Alex Ji found the first r-process dwarf galaxy, observing nine stars in poor weather. She links it to neutron star mergers, which LIGO and Virgo confirmed as a source of heavy elements in 2017.
- **Doing the work.** Frebel observes with the 6.5-meter Magellan Telescopes in Chile, now mostly remotely. A recent paper with three undergraduate women from a new MIT course found low-strontium, low-barium stars on retrograde orbits, a sign they were accreted early.
- **Women in astronomy.** She credits the Harvard Computers, Annie Jump Cannon, Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin and Lise Meitner, and performs a one-woman play as Meitner before giving a talk on the r-process.
