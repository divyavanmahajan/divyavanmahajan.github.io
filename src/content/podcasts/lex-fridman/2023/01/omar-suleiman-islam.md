## Thesis

Omar Suleiman, founder of the Yaqeen Institute and a professor of Islamic studies at Southern Methodist University, tells Lex Fridman that Islam offers a coherent account of God, suffering and purpose, in which submission to an all-wise God brings peace rather than loss of freedom. He argues that linking Islam with terrorism is a "lazy association" and that most conflicts framed as religious are political at root. Fridman opens by saying he intends to speak with people on all sides of the Israel and Palestine question.

## Key points

- **God and suffering.** Suleiman describes God as one, merciful and directly accessible without clergy. On suffering, he compares humans to a child who cannot see why a parent says no, and points to the hereafter as the place of "divine recourse".
- **His mother.** He recalls his mother, who had cancer and several strokes yet was known for her smile and for drawing outsiders into gatherings; he says his work is an extension of her.
- **Islamophobia.** Suleiman cites Christchurch, the Quebec mosque attack and armed protests outside Dallas-area mosques around 2015. He recounts a BBC documentary in which an armed protester changed his view after meeting him and a Syrian refugee family.
- **Practice.** He walks through the five daily prayers, prostration as the closest point to God, the Hajj, the six articles of faith, the five pillars and Ramadan, which he calls a "spiritual bootcamp".
- **Role models and Colleyville.** He discusses Malcolm X's change after Hajj, Muhammad Ali and Khabib Nurmagomedov, and his decision to go to the Colleyville synagogue hostage scene in 2022 to offer support.
- **Politics.** Suleiman says Muslims sit awkwardly between both US parties. He argues that groups like ISIS grew out of the political chaos in Iraq. On Israel and Palestine he states, as his own view and citing Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International reports, that Israeli policy meets the definition of apartheid, and he urges Fridman to speak to people on the ground rather than only leaders. Fridman says leaders are also worth hearing, with empathy.
