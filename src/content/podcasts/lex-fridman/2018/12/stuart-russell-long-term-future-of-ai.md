## Thesis

Stuart Russell, UC Berkeley professor and co-author of Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, tells Lex Fridman that the standard model of AI, where a machine optimizes an objective handed to it, is the root of the control problem. He argues that machines should instead be uncertain about what humans want, which makes them deferential and willing to be corrected, and that superhuman AI is plausible enough that researchers must ask what happens if they succeed.

## Key points

- **Meta-reasoning and AlphaGo.** Russell describes his Berkeley work on deciding what to think about: a program should explore moves whose value is uncertain enough that thinking could change its decision. He says AlphaGo combines strong position evaluation with this kind of selective look-ahead, playing at professional level even with depth-one search.
- **Hype and self-driving cars.** Recalling the late-1980s expert-system winter and costly Lisp machines, Russell warns of a similar disappointment, naming self-driving cars as the likely flagship. He notes a car changed lanes on a freeway in 1987 and says driving needs about eight nines of reliability, far beyond a successful demo.
- **The King Midas problem.** Russell argues that statistics, control theory and operations research all assume a known objective, and that a machine certain of a wrong objective has no reason to listen. Uncertainty about the objective turns the problem into a game between human and machine.
- **Scalable harm without oversight.** He compares software to pharmaceuticals, which led to the FDA, and argues that click-through optimization changes users' preferences towards predictable extremes. He calls for bias standards and a rule that machines self-identify, citing a narrower California law.
- **Denial and nuclear history.** Russell cites Rutherford's 1933 "moonshine" speech and Leo Szilard's chain reaction idea the next day, and attributes AI researchers' dismissal of risk to motivated cognition.
- **Three failure modes.** He lists loss of control, misuse, and overuse, the "WALL-E problem" of humans losing autonomy, pointing to E.M. Forster's 1909 story The Machine Stops.
