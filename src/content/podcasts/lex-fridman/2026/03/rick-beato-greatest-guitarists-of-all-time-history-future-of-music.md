## Thesis

Music educator and YouTuber Rick Beato tells Lex Fridman that musical ability is learned like a language: early exposure to complex music, daily practice and the struggle of working things out by ear matter more than shortcuts. Both men see AI music tools as impressive but ultimately boring, and Beato argues the people who gain most from them are already skilled songwriters.

## Key points

- **Perfect pitch as language.** Beato believes every child is born with perfect pitch and loses it around nine months unless engaged. He credits playing Bach, jazz and modern classical music to his son Dylan, starting in the womb, for Dylan's ability. A video of eight-year-old Dylan naming polychords got about 80 million views on Facebook.
- **Learning guitar.** Beato recommends starting with songs rather than theory, so beginners stay motivated, and practising 10 minutes every day. He learned a Joe Pass record by ear as a teenager, a gift from his father.
- **Why the greats peaked young.** Beato links the Beatles' run (Help!, Rubber Soul and Revolver within 365 days) to constant studio work after bad PA systems ended their touring. He suggests improvisers and pop writers peak before 30, while composers like Beethoven wrote their key works late in life.
- **AI in music.** Beato has generated about 130 song ideas with Suno (lyrics written in Claude) and found three good ones. His kids could spot AI songs from artefacts in the vocal reverb, which he links to training on low bit-rate MP3s. Fridman argues people want authenticity: he wants the real B.B. King, not an AI one.
- **Industry practices.** Beato criticises Song of the Year nominees with 10 or 11 credited writers and songs built on interpolations or samples, like Doechii's track over a Gotye song. A lawyer has fought 4,000 Content ID claims across his 2,100 videos and won every one.
- **Careers and craft.** Beato started his channel in his mid-50s, after a jazz master's degree, college teaching, a publishing deal and producing bands. Fridman says years spent mastering a craft, not chasing fame, explain that success.
