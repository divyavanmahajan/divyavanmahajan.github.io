## Thesis

Jeff Kaplan, quest designer on World of Warcraft and later game director of Overwatch, argues that games succeed when small teams with a clear vision cut scope hard. In his account they fail through hubris, hiring before there is a vision, or revenue targets. He now runs his own studio, Kintsugiyama, which he set up to keep that control.

## Key points

- **From rejection to Blizzard.** Kaplan got over 170 rejection letters in one year as a writer, fought depression and alcohol, and played about 272 days of EverQuest in three years. His guild-mates, including Rob Pardo and founder Allen Adham, worked at Blizzard and hired him for 35K a year.
- **Quest-driven leveling.** After a playtest showed WoW needed far more quests, the team made quests the "path of least resistance" for experience. Kaplan says this moved players through the world and let them play alone.
- **Why Titan failed.** Kaplan blames leadership, himself included. The team hired about 70 environment artists in one year with no defined vision, and its editor let one artist work only 20 hours in a 40-hour week. Fridman cites a cost of $83 million.
- **Overwatch in six weeks.** The remaining team had six weeks to pitch a game that could ship in two years. Overwatch grew from Geoff Goodman's wish for 50 simple classes instead of six complex ones. Its first hero, Tracer, was built from Titan's "Jumper" class.
- **Leaving Blizzard.** Kaplan says Overwatch League commitments and executive pressure starved the planned PvE Overwatch 2. He quit after a CFO told him a thousand layoffs would be "on you" if revenue targets were missed.
- **What's next.** His 34-person studio is making The Legend of California, a Gold Rush island game whose map seeds shift points of interest and difficulty tiers. He calls AI in game development "mostly a hot mess": handy for chores, but often wrong and unethical when it uses creators' work without permission.
