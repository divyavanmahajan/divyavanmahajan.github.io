## Thesis

Andrew Scull, a historian of psychiatry, argues that the field has swung between brain-based and mind-based models and has repeatedly claimed cures it could not deliver, often at great cost to patients. Today's drugs and talk therapies help some people, he says, but they treat symptoms rather than cure illness, and the diagnostic system itself is now in doubt. Lex Fridman steers the conversation from the asylum era to the present and presses on where hope lies.

## Key points

- **A field in crisis.** Scull cites former NIMH director Thomas Insel, who said that after spending twenty billion dollars on genetics and neuroscience the lot of the mentally ill had not improved. The DSM, symptom-based since DSM-III in 1980, delivers reliability but not validity, and genetic overlap between schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and autism undermines its categories.
- **Asylums to eugenics.** Early asylums promised 70-80% cure rates but discharged 35-40%. Psychiatrists then blamed degenerate biology, which fed California's sterilisations and the Nazi T4 killings. The Rockefeller Foundation had funded Ernst Rüdin, who became a key architect of Hitler's sterilisation laws.
- **Desperate remedies.** Scull recounts Wagner-Jauregg's malaria therapy for syphilis (Nobel Prize, 1937), Henry Cotton's tooth and organ removal at Trenton with 45% abdominal-surgery deaths, insulin comas, and Walter Freeman's ice pick lobotomy. The claim of an 80% cure recurs again and again.
- **ECT and talk therapy.** Unmodified ECT was often used as discipline, but Scull accepts trial evidence that modern ECT helps some severely depressed patients, although nobody knows why. CBT grew out of wartime clinical psychology and can be measured, unlike psychoanalysis, but Cochrane rates its evidence as low to medium confidence.
- **Drugs by accident.** Chlorpromazine started out as an antihistamine. The CATIE study found that newer antipsychotics were no better than older ones, and 67-82% of patients dropped out. SSRIs beat placebo statistically but often not clinically, and they bring numbing and withdrawal problems.
- **What next.** Scull wants research money for psychosocial support, warns against hype about ketamine and psychedelics, and notes that the largest psychiatric inpatient centres in the US are now jails.
