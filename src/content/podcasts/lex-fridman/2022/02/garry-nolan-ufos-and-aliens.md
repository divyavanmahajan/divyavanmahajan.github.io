## Thesis

Garry Nolan, a Stanford School of Medicine professor, tells Lex Fridman that UFO anecdotes, alleged materials and pilot reports are data worth studying, not conclusions to believe. He says his job is to clear hyped cases off the table, analyse what is reproducible, and publish it. Both men argue that more funding, transparency and data collection would help science, and that the topic itself can inspire new technology.

## Key points

- **Recurring stories.** Nolan says what interests him is the uniformity of encounter reports, citing a Zimbabwe school case where 50 or 60 children described the same craft and a message that humans were not looking after the planet. He speculates that witnesses may be seeing something shown to them rather than a physical object.
- **Experiencers' brains.** Studying MRIs of people brought to him, Nolan says most injuries turned out to be Havana syndrome, but he also found denser connectivity in part of the basal ganglia that runs in families, including his own, which he links to intuition rather than contact.
- **Debunking cases.** His team's genome study of the Atacama skeleton found it was 100 percent human with unusual bone-growth mutations. Nolan rejects the word "debunking" and says the aim is simply to collect data.
- **Engineered isotopes.** Using secondary ion mass spectrometry on fragments from the Ubatuba, Brazil case, Nolan found magnesium isotope ratios far from normal in one chain of custody. He says this proves only an engineered process, not an alien origin.
- **Funding and government.** Nolan calls the June 2021 ODNI preliminary UAP assessment hopeful, says he spent roughly $50,000-70,000 of his own money on research, and estimates a proper materials analysis needs a few million. He believes amnesty for officials and disclosure could bring people together. He has joined Avi Loeb's Galileo Project.
- **Advice to young scientists.** Nolan calls shame a societal control device, and both he and Fridman say a senior warning that a topic will hurt your career is often a sign the topic is worth pursuing.
