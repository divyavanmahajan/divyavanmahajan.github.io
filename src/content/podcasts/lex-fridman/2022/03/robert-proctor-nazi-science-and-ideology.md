## Thesis

Robert Proctor, a historian of science at Stanford, tells Lex Fridman that science and ideology are woven together rather than separate. He argues that German science was a full collaborator in Nazi exclusion and genocide, and that the same mechanisms (narrow "widget building", money, institutional power) let the tobacco industry manufacture ignorance for decades. Fridman connects this to scientific leadership, censorship and military funding of AI research today.

## Key points

- **Nazi medicine.** Proctor says the regime was not anti-science but against "Jewish" and "communist" science. Physicians joined the Nazi party and the SS in higher proportions than any other profession; he links this to a "sanitary utopia" of purification, and notes the Nazis looked to US segregation and sterilisation laws.
- **The Nazi war on cancer.** The same regime ran an aggressive anti-cancer campaign: it recognised asbestos as a cause of mesothelioma, encouraged breast self-exams and banned X-raying pregnant women in the early 1930s.
- **Cigarettes as the deadliest object.** Fridman cites Proctor's writing that cigarettes kill about 6 million people a year; Proctor says US consumption peaked at 640 billion cigarettes in 1981 and is now about 240 billion. He describes industry tactics such as the "doubt is our product" memo and measuring how much doubt its films created, which he calls agnometrics.
- **Money in universities.** He recounts the Philip Morris External Research Program funding Stanford researchers and his campaign that led Philip Morris to withdraw it, and argues students are rarely asked what they would not do for money. Fridman raises similar worries about DARPA and car-company funding of his own field.
- **Censorship and trust.** Proctor supports platforms trying to curb misinformation, comparing them to publishers. Fridman counters that the pressure to censor currently carries a political lean and that distrust of institutions needs to be fixed at its cause.
- **Human origins and ignorance.** Proctor argues ignorance is infinite and also culturally produced, and that after WWII researchers projected full humanness too far back onto fossil hominids.
