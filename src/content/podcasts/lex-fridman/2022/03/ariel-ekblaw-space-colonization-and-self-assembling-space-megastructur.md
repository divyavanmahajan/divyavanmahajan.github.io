## Thesis

Ariel Ekblaw, director of the MIT Space Exploration Initiative, tells Lex Fridman that scaling humanity's presence in space needs structures bigger than any rocket fairing, and that self-assembling, reconfigurable modules can deliver them. Her own contrarian view is that large human communities will live in floating cities in orbit, not on the surface of Mars, which she calls a poor home for now.

## Key points

- **TESSERAE tiles.** Ekblaw's PhD work uses flat-packed tiles with electropermanent magnets that find each other in microgravity, detect bad bonds and self-correct, with no central coordinator. Three-inch tiles were tested on the ISS; 2.5-foot tiles are being built, aiming for a buckyball about 10 meters across.
- **Space architecture candidates.** She describes three designs: a secular "space cathedral" with long spires, a nautilus-shaped station made of docked truncated octahedrons, and a ring world around a planet or moon.
- **Swarm robots and the Moon.** Small "astro ants" with magnetic wheels would crawl over spacecraft looking for leaks and punctures. One will ride a rover on an MIT lunar mission to the south pole site planned for Artemis III.
- **Living in space.** Ekblaw names radiation and mental health as the two big problems for long missions, says a Mars round trip takes about three years, and expects artificial gravity to be needed beyond that. Her lab also studies fermented food for deep-space missions.
- **Mars and commercial space.** Ekblaw sees McMurdo-style Mars outposts as feasible in the 2030s. She says commercial launch lets her fundraise and buy flights directly, which led to spinning out the Aurelia Institute; she argues NASA backed this ecosystem so it can go further.
- **Governance.** She worries about conflict following humans into space despite the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, and raises open questions on lunar property rights and protecting the space commons.
