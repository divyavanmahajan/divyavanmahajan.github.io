## Thesis

Betül Kaçar, an astrobiologist at the University of Wisconsin, tells Lex Fridman that life is chemistry exploring solutions and keeping a memory of the ones that work. She sees the cell's translation machinery, which turns genetic information into working proteins, as the oldest computing device in biology. Fridman keeps returning to computing analogies, aliens and the ethics of seeding life elsewhere, and Kaçar answers with caution and optimism.

## Key points

- **Translation as a computer.** Kaçar describes translation as chemical, physical, informatic, computational and biological at once. Unlike today's computers, she says, it links information directly to function, and spare capacity in the genetic code (64 codon states for 20 amino acids) makes it tolerant of errors.
- **Ancient genes in modern cells.** Her lab put a roughly 700-million-year-old version of one translation protein into modern bacteria, making a hybrid organism. Kaçar says this is the reverse of "Jurassic Park", which puts modern genes into an ancient organism.
- **Evolution is lazy.** Over about 150 days of lab evolution, the cells fixed one module at a time and stopped short of the best result. The more distant the inserted gene, the bigger the hit and the more likely the fix landed at the damaged spot.
- **A handful of one-off innovations.** Kaçar lists events that seem to have happened once, including translation, cyanobacteria and oxygen-producing photosynthesis, and eukaryotes. She argues the planet was largely alien and microbial for billions of years.
- **Seeding chemistry, not life.** She proposes "proto-spermia": studying another planet's chemistry and perhaps adding more of what it already has, rather than terraforming. Fridman supports seeding life but worries about creating beings that can suffer. Kaçar doubts panspermia because it moves the question off Earth, where it cannot be tested.
- **Science and opportunity.** Kaçar expects the origin-of-life field to make major progress this decade. She stresses that brains are evenly distributed but opportunities are not, and advises young people to tolerate failure and drop questions that bore them.
