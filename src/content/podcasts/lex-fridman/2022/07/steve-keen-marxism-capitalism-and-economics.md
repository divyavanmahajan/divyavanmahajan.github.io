## Thesis

Steve Keen tells Lex Fridman that neoclassical economics uses the wrong tools: it assumes stable equilibrium, treats money as irrelevant and models the economy with difference equations rather than the systems-engineering methods used for other complex systems. He argues that bank lending creates money, that private debt drives booms and crises, and that economists badly underestimate climate change. Fridman plays devil's advocate throughout, especially on central planning and climate forecasts.

## Key points

- **Reading Marx.** Keen ranks Marx as the most brilliant mind in economics, but argues Marx contradicted himself: his own use-value/exchange-value logic, found in a Grundrisse footnote, implies machines can generate surplus too, which undermines the falling-rate-of-profit case for socialism.
- **Why socialism failed.** Drawing on Janos Kornai, Keen says socialist economies were resource-constrained and kept producing last year's goods, while capitalism is demand-constrained and forced to innovate. He cites a Soviet-made motorbike copied from a 1942 BMW. He views China's mix of central political control and market activity more favourably; Fridman raises censorship, surveillance and concentrated power.
- **Money and debt.** Keen says banks create money when they lend, so credit is part of aggregate demand. He says US credit peaked at 16% of GDP in 2006-07 and fell to -5% in 2008-09, and that US private debt, at 170% of GDP, should be held at 30-70%.
- **Modelling chaos.** His Minsky software adds Godley tables (double-entry bookkeeping) to system-dynamics modelling. Extending Richard Goodwin's model with debt produced cycles that calmed before a crisis, which Keen links to the pre-2008 "great moderation".
- **Climate economics.** Keen attacks William Nordhaus for treating 87% of US industry as shielded from climate, and Richard Tol for a 2016 estimate that losing the Gulf Stream would raise global GDP by 1.1%. He contrasts this with an OECD scenario in which land suited to wheat falls from 20% to 7%.
- **Advice.** Keen tells students to skip an economics degree, learn system dynamics and then apply it to economics.
