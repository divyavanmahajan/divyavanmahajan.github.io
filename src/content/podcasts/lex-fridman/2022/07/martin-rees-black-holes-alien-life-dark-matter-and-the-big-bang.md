## Thesis

Martin Rees, Cambridge cosmologist and co-founder of the Centre for the Study of Existential Risk, tells Lex Fridman that the universe is far larger and stranger than we can see, and that human brains may be too limited to grasp its deepest laws without AI. He argues that humans are not the culmination of evolution: electronic successors may emerge from adventurers on Mars, while this century is also the first in which one species can wreck the planet.

## Key points

- **Limits of the human brain.** Rees says a "theory of everything" would not help biologists, and that string theory's 10-dimensional geometry may need AI to work through. He cites Demis Hassabis and DeepMind's protein-folding work, and argues that Nobel Prizes distort how science is done.
- **Dark matter and the early universe.** Galaxies need about five times more mass than we see. Rees still expects particles, noting the Large Hadron Collider has explored only a small part of parameter space, with axions as a candidate.
- **Alien life.** Life found independently on Europa or Enceladus would show it is common; the 39 m Extremely Large Telescope may take spectra of exoplanets. Rees chairs Yuri Milner's search for intelligence, funded at $10 million a year, and suggests advanced aliens would be electronic and possibly not expansionist.
- **Robots over astronauts.** From his book The End of Astronauts, Rees argues robots make human spaceflight hard to justify for NASA, so risky Mars trips should be left to privately funded adventurers. Fridman pushes back that Musk aims at a backup plan, not escape, and that the inspiration is valuable; both praise SpaceX's reusable rockets.
- **Black holes.** Rees describes stellar-mass black holes found through X-rays, quasars powered by monster black holes, our galaxy's 4-million-solar-mass black hole and the Andromeda merger in about 4 billion years.
- **Technological risk.** Rees's worst fears are engineered pathogens and cyberattacks, and he expects nations to trade privacy for security. He disputes Steven Pinker's optimism, arguing the gap between how the world is and could be is now wider.
