## Thesis

Bishop Robert Barron, founder of Word on Fire, tells Lex Fridman that most arguments about God make a category error: following Thomas Aquinas, God is not a big being among others but the act of existence itself, in whom essence and existence coincide, present to creation the way an author is present to a novel. From there Barron builds the core Christian claims (incarnation, Trinity, the church as an organism) and argues that love, understood as willing the good of the other, is the metric of a good life. Fridman probes evil, the abuse scandal, science and contested moral questions.

## Key points

- **God as author, not character.** Barron compares God to Tolkien: responsible for every detail of the story but not inside it. Atheists who treat God like Russell's orbiting teapot, he says, attack the wrong idea.
- **Pride as the root sin.** Barron calls pride the deadliest of the seven deadly sins, using Augustine's image of being "caved in" on oneself and Dante's frozen Satan. Humility, he says, is a dog on the beach lost in chasing a ball.
- **Evil and suffering.** Barron calls the problem of evil the strongest argument against God, noting that its best versions come from believers: Job, Aquinas and Dostoevsky's Ivan. His answer, which he admits is emotionally unsatisfying, is that God permits evil to bring about a greater good, and that Christ entered suffering himself.
- **The abuse scandal.** Barron calls it awful, says cases spiked in the 1970s and 1980s and fell after the bishops' 2002 Dallas protocols, and, citing sociologist-priest Andrew Greeley, says research shows no link to celibacy.
- **Science and scientism.** Barron respects science but opposes reducing all knowledge to it, citing Eugene Wigner's essay on the effectiveness of mathematics as a clue to a created, intelligible world.
- **Contested questions.** Barron states the church's positions: sex belongs within marriage between a man and a woman, while the church reaches out to gay people with respect; he is pro-life and favours overturning Roe v. Wade. Fridman frames abortion as a hard question about when life begins.
