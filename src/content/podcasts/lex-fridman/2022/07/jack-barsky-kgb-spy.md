## Thesis

Jack Barsky, born Albrecht Dittrich in East Germany, tells Lex Fridman how a gifted chemistry student became a KGB "illegal" in the United States and then walked away. He describes the KGB as strong at compartmentalisation but poor at understanding American life, and says love, first for his 18-month-old daughter Chelsea, is what ended his career as a spy.

## Key points

- **Recruitment and testing.** Barsky says a Stasi collaborator posing as a Carl Zeiss Jena recruiter handed him to a KGB officer, "Herman", who tested him for about 18 months with reports, profiles of fellow students and pretext visits. He passed practice trips to West Berlin and was told his first task was to find his own housing.
- **Training.** In Berlin and then two years in Moscow he learned Morse code, manual encryption, one-time pads, microdots and surveillance detection routes. He learned 100 English words a day with an index-card system and did nightly phonetics drills.
- **Incompetent preparation.** Barsky says his handlers had never lived as illegals, which left him with no backup plan in Chicago, where he arrived via a Canadian passport under the name William Dyson. He argues the CIA understood Russia just as poorly.
- **Quitting.** In 1988 a red danger signal, a radio message and a man on a Queens subway platform told him to come home. After a failed dead drop on Staten Island, he says, he decided to stay and told Moscow he had contracted HIV/AIDS.
- **Discovery.** Notes smuggled out by archivist Vasili Mitrokhin led the FBI to him. Barsky says agents recorded him confessing to his wife, then stopped him at a bridge toll, and he chose to cooperate fully.
- **Putin, Bezmenov and Ukraine.** Barsky calls Yuri Bezmenov's subversion framework a fabrication and says Oleg Kalugin described Putin as an unimpressive agent, then softens that claim when Fridman questions the source. On Ukraine, Barsky faults US wishful thinking; Fridman adds NATO expansion and the military-industrial complex.
