## Thesis

Glenn Loury, professor of economics at Brown University and host of The Glenn Show, tells Lex Fridman that Martin Luther King's dream was about equal citizenship and opportunity, not equal outcomes. Loury argues that group disparities reflect culture and development as well as discrimination, and that calling people racist instead of arguing with them drives views underground. He describes himself as a market-oriented economist who has moved towards conservatism on race.

## Key points

- **Opportunity versus outcomes.** Loury says groups with different norms and traditions will not be equally represented everywhere, using the NBA as an example of a fair process with unequal results. He stresses that developmental opportunity, from prenatal care to peer groups, shapes skills long before the labour market.
- **Black patriotism.** Loury argues that black Americans are "first and foremost" American and should work within American politics. He recounts his uncle's respect for the self-reliance of Malcolm X and the Nation of Islam, but says violent rebellion makes no sense today and that the 2020 riots set black Americans back.
- **Racism as an epithet.** Using Dorian Abbot's disinvitation from MIT and attacks on Charles Murray, Loury compares the racism charge to accusing someone of witchcraft. He cites Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann's "spiral of silence" to warn that suppressed views can erupt suddenly.
- **Universities and research.** Loury calls activists who claim to know the "right side of history" the barbarians at the gate. Fridman blames growing administrations. Loury lays out the case against research on group IQ differences, then rejects funding bans based on predicted political effects.
- **Politics and affirmative action.** Loury says he hates affirmative action as a "band-aid" for underdeveloped capacity. He criticises Joe Biden for announcing he would nominate a black woman to the Supreme Court, while calling Ketanji Brown Jackson qualified. He says Thomas Sowell deserves a Nobel Prize and faults Barack Obama for reinforcing a racial narrative after Trayvon Martin. Fridman pushes back that Obama's example was itself powerful.
- **Mortality.** Fresh from his brother-in-law's funeral, Loury reflects on his late wife Linda's death, Lucretius and teaching as legacy.
