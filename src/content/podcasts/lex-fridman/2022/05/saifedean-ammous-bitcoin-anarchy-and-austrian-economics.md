## Thesis

Saifedean Ammous, author of The Bitcoin Standard and The Fiat Standard, argues that money is always whatever is hardest to produce, and that the move from gold to government credit money since World War I has raised time preference, financed total war and forced savers to become speculators. He presents Bitcoin, with its fixed 21 million supply and no central authority, as the fix. Lex Fridman repeatedly pushes back on Ammous's certainty, his treatment of Keynesian economics, and his dismissal of altcoins and proof of stake.

## Key points

- **Hard money.** Ammous uses the stock-to-flow ratio to explain why gold, with stockpiles growing about 1.5-2% a year, beat silver and copper. He says the idea of money as a collective hallucination is wrong: if people chose copper, miners would flood the market.
- **Austrian vs Keynesian.** Ammous calls Keynesian economics propaganda for inflation and points to 1970s stagflation as data its model cannot explain. Fridman calls this too certain, citing his Soviet upbringing as a warning about ideological certainty.
- **Birth of fiat.** Ammous says Bank of England papers released in 2017 show only a third of Britain's World War I bonds were sold to the public, with the rest bought on Bank of England credit. He argues fiat is created through lending, and puts global fiat supply growth at about 14% a year from 1960 to 2020.
- **Bitcoin's critics.** On mining centralisation, Ammous says nodes set the rules, citing the 2017 block size war. He defends Bitcoin's energy use as worth it and as using stranded power, and calls proof of stake a version of the Federal Reserve. Fridman argues for keeping an open mind about NFTs and dapps.
- **Politics and CBDCs.** Ammous calls himself an anarchist who is warming to monarchy. He warns that central bank digital currencies could bring Soviet-style Gosbank control, and expects people, not governments, to move to Bitcoin first.
- **Personal.** Ammous, who grew up in Ramallah, says Bitcoin protected him from hyperinflation in Lebanon. He tells young people to create value for others, learn to code and save for the long term.
