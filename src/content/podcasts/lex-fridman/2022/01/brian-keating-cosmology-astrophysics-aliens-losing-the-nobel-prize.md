## Thesis

Brian Keating, a UC San Diego experimental physicist and author of Losing the Nobel Prize, tells Lex Fridman the story of the BICEP experiments, which hunted for gravitational-wave traces of cosmic inflation in the polarization of the cosmic microwave background. He argues that the pursuit of the Nobel Prize fuels ego, zero-sum competition and premature announcements, and that theories should be tested against experiments rather than accepted on reputation. Fridman adds that academics rarely celebrate each other's work publicly, and contrasts this with Joe Rogan's habit of promoting others.

## Key points

- **Why polarization matters.** Keating explains that inflation should leave a polarization signal of one or two parts per billion of the CMB's temperature, a few nanokelvin. Measuring it needs a small refracting telescope at the dry South Pole, with detectors cooled to about 0.1 kelvin.
- **BICEP2 and loss.** BICEP2 went from 98 to 512 detectors in January 2010. That same month Keating's mentor Andrew Lange took his own life, and Keating was left out of the BICEP2 leadership, which he says felt like a betrayal.
- **The dust problem.** The team announced a detection at Harvard before peer review. Keating says magnetically aligned galactic dust grains produced the same pattern. He proposes that science funders set aside money for publicising a retraction, as well as for the announcement.
- **Nobel incentives.** Keating says that when he was invited to nominate physics laureates, the only rule given was that he could not nominate himself. He also notes that Barry Barish won partly because Ron Drever died before the award.
- **The Assayer project.** Keating wants theories of everything judged on testable predictions, not retrodictions, with Bayesian updates as new results arrive. He praises Eric Weinstein's openness to rival theories; Fridman counters that Weinstein still has to prove his physics through peer review.
- **Aliens.** Keating doubts technological life exists elsewhere. He argues that eight hurdles at one in a thousand each give odds of 10^24, against roughly 10^22 planets in the observable universe. Fridman cites Lee Cronin's opposite view.
