## Thesis

Michael Levin, a biologist at Tufts University, tells Lex Fridman that living systems are built from parts that each pursue goals, from molecular networks up to whole organisms. He argues this makes cells an "agential material": engineers and doctors get further by finding the right high-level signals than by trying to control every gene. Fridman draws parallels to AI and to building robots.

## Key points

- **Xenobots as a robotics platform.** Levin describes taking skin cells from frog embryos, with no genetic edits, and finding that within 48 hours they form small self-moving bodies that can collect loose cells into new copies of themselves. The work was done with Josh Bongard's group at the University of Vermont, which uses simulated evolution to design interventions.
- **Working with agential materials.** Levin contrasts a tower of Legos with a tower of trained dogs: you lose fine control, but the system can recover from disruption by itself. He says the useful engineering question is how much a material can be trusted to do when nobody is micromanaging it.
- **Bioelectricity as a control layer.** Levin explains that all cells use ion channels, which act like transistors, and gap junctions to form electrical networks that store a pattern memory. By editing that electrical pattern in flatworms, his lab produced two-headed worms with an unchanged genome.
- **Medicine beyond micromanagement.** Levin says most drugs work at the pathway level and lose effect once stopped. His long-term goal is an "anatomical compiler" that turns a drawn shape into signals for cells. He believes this would address birth defects, injury, cancer and ageing, and reduce rising healthcare costs.
- **Early commercial work.** Levin discloses a spin-off, Morphoceuticals, with David Kaplan. A wearable bioreactor applied for 24 hours after amputation led to about 18 months of leg regrowth in frogs, and trials in mice are under way.
- **Cancer as a smaller self.** Levin argues that cancer cells disconnect from the body's electrical network. Forcing them to stay connected reduced tumour formation in his experiments, and the lab has moved on to human glioblastoma cells.
