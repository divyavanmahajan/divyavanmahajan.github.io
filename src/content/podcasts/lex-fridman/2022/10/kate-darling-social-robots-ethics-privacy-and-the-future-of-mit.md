## Thesis

Kate Darling, a research scientist at the MIT Media Lab and author of The New Breed, tells Lex Fridman that comparing robots to humans leads us astray; our history with domesticated animals is a better guide to how robots will work alongside us. She is excited about social robots and large language models but worried that companies will use emotional attachment to collect data and market to people. The conversation ends with her account of the Jeffrey Epstein controversy at MIT and what she sees as institutional failures of leadership.

## Key points

- **Animals, not humans.** Darling says people domesticated oxen, ferrets, pigeons and even navy dolphins because their abilities differ from ours, and robots should be designed the same way. She calls the humanoid form a research curiosity and a distraction; Fridman is more open to Tesla's Optimus effort.
- **Design for reactions.** Using the Stop and Shop "Marty" robot, Darling describes a student's sentiment analysis showing negative mentions peaked when the store threw it a birthday party, and links this to Clifford Nass's theory of why people hated Clippy.
- **Jobs and safety.** She argues robots take over tasks rather than whole jobs, citing safer remote-controlled mining and Amazon's first warehouse robot safe to work near people. Fridman adds that human demining in Ukraine still outperforms robots.
- **Manipulation risk.** Darling cites her LuLaRobot paper: social robots combine personalisation, persuasion, automation, data collection and embodiment. In a study she advised, most children preferred robots marketing through casual conversation, believing the robot knew them better than the company. She also questions Sony's aibo subscription model, priced at about 300 a year after a 3,000 dollar purchase.
- **Sentience claims.** On the Google LaMDA engineer, she argues believing a system is sentient is not naive; even experts in her lab anthropomorphise robots.
- **MIT and leadership.** Darling says Joi Ito was wrong to take Epstein's money but took responsibility, while others protected the institution. She also describes reporting a supervisor for unwanted physical advances in 2019 and finding the process favoured protecting MIT.
