## Thesis

Magatte Wade, a Senegalese entrepreneur who heads the Atlas Network's Center for African Prosperity, tells Lex Fridman that prosperity depends on economic freedom: rule of law, clear property rights and a simple business environment. In her view Africa's poverty comes from bad laws and senseless regulation inherited from colonial and post-independence socialist governments, not from its people. She argues that market reforms and "startup cities" can let a young continent leapfrog.

## Key points

- **Same people, different places.** Wade asks why migrants from poor countries thrive once they move, and rejects IQ, education and nutrition explanations. She points to East and West Germany and North and South Korea as evidence that institutions decide outcomes.
- **Doing business in Senegal.** She contrasts forming a US LLC in minutes with monthly in-person filings at three agencies, a minimum tax even with zero revenue, and nine months waiting for an electricity meter. She says her lip balm inputs face 40% and almost 70% tariffs, which she calls "senseless laws" that breed bribery.
- **Her own company.** Wade built a beverage company around bissap (hibiscus juice) to keep a cultural drink alive and employ women growers, growing from 400 to 9,000 suppliers. Her first husband died as she was about to sign her first term sheet, and she says those women convinced her to continue.
- **History and ideology.** She traces Africa's post-1960 socialist turn to the 1945 Pan-African Congress and Cold War politics, and argues that today's Black Lives Matter founders repeat that mistake by embracing Marxism. She criticises DEI programmes and TOMS-style giveaways, which she says put local shoemakers out of business.
- **Geopolitics and food.** On African abstentions over Ukraine, Wade sees resistance to Western pressure, but says the African Union chair asking Putin for grain came "too close to begging" for a continent with so much farmland.
- **Startup cities.** Because piecemeal reform is slower than population growth, she backs common-law business zones modelled on Dubai, Hong Kong, Singapore and China's special economic zones, with projects in Zambia and Nigeria.
