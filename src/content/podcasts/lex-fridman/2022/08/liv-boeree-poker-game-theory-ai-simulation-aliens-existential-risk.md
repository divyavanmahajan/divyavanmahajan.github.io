## Thesis

Liv Boeree, a former professional poker player trained in astrophysics, tells Lex Fridman that the lessons of poker (thinking in probabilities, calming the fight-or-flight response, avoiding zero-sum traps) apply far beyond the table. Her central argument is that "Moloch", a multi-polar trap where everyone sacrifices values for short-term advantage, sits behind problems from social media to arms races, and that its antidote is deliberately seeking positive-sum games. Fridman is more optimistic, arguing that virality and even runaway systems can produce good outcomes.

## Key points

- **Poker as math plus nerve.** Boeree says a better player might win only 52-53% over ten hands but 98-99% over 10,000. Top pros now train with solvers that run billions of simulated hands toward game-theory-optimal play, leaving gut feel to decide when to exploit opponents. She calls Phil Hellmuth, with 16 World Series bracelets, the outlier who keeps winning despite non-optimal play.
- **Moloch and social media.** Drawing on Scott Alexander's "Meditations on Moloch", Boeree uses Instagram beauty filters and engagement-driven feeds as examples of races to the bottom. She argues anger spreads best online, and suggests a subscription model would remove Twitter's incentive to maximise addictive refreshing.
- **Win-Win as the counter-meme.** Boeree describes a character she calls Win-Win, which enjoys bounded competition but values cooperation, as the opposite of Moloch.
- **Existential risks.** She cites 11 or 12 nuclear near-misses, including Stanislav Petrov, and says bio risk worries her most because it is avoidable. She criticises US pandemic-preparedness funding being cut from 60 billion to 2 billion dollars, and a proposed project to catalogue and publish dangerous pathogens.
- **AI race dynamics.** Boeree says competition among labs rewards speed over safety, and cites Bostrom's orthogonality point that intelligence and wisdom need not coincide.
- **Aliens and faith.** She summarises Future of Humanity Institute work suggesting roughly a 70% chance we are alone in the galaxy, and describes moving from strict atheism to "useful fictions" after a premonition before winning a 1,200-player tournament in 2010.
