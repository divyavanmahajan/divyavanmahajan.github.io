## Thesis

Chris Blattman, a University of Chicago professor and author of "Why We Fight", tells Lex Fridman that war is so costly for everyone that rivals usually "loathe in peace" and find a deal. Fighting happens when one of five forces overrides that incentive: unchecked leaders, uncertainty, commitment problems, intangible values, and misperceptions. He names unaccountable power as the common root. Fridman, who is half Ukrainian and half Russian, responds with a more emotional defence of people who stand up to invaders.

## Key points

- **Five buckets.** Blattman calls three of the causes strategic (leaders who do not bear the costs, uncertainty that rewards bluffing, and shifts in power that make striking now attractive) and two human (values such as liberty, and misjudgements). He compares war to pneumonia killing a frail patient: mistakes matter mainly when strategic forces have already narrowed the room for a deal.
- **Ukraine.** Blattman reads the war as Ukrainian refusal to give up sovereignty, comparable to the American Revolution, combined with a Russian elite that felt threatened by Ukrainian democracy, and uncertainty about Western unity and Russian military strength. Fridman praises Ukrainian, Russian and British resistance, quoting Churchill.
- **Nuclear risk.** Blattman, whose building houses the Doomsday Clock, says the risk is still very low but no longer near zero.
- **Medellín gangs.** Blattman describes about 17 mafia groups that keep street gangs in line through an informal board, "La Oficina". He compares its use of sanctions, mediation and enforcement to the UN Security Council. When homicides doubled, imprisoned leaders were transferred into the same holding area with a trusted mediator, and a week later homicides were back to normal.
- **Kony and Uganda.** He explains Joseph Kony's mass abduction of children in northern Uganda as a recruitment tactic for an unpopular movement. He argues the president tolerated the insurgency because it weakened a rival power base in the north.
- **Paths to peace.** On China and the US, Blattman points to economic, social and ideological interdependence and wider power-sharing as protection. He sees Xi Jinping's personalisation of power as a risk.
