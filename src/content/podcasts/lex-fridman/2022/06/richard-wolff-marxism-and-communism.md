## Thesis

Richard Wolff, a Marxist economist, tells Lex Fridman that Marx was mainly a critic of capitalism, and that his core idea is about exploitation: workers produce a surplus that someone else takes and decides how to use. In Wolff's view, communism means workers deciding together what to do with that surplus. Revolutions that only replaced private owners with state officials, he argues, kept the exploitation. Fridman, whose family history runs through the Soviet Union, pushes back on human nature, risk, competition and the record of government.

## Key points

- **Surplus and exploitation.** Wolff defines the surplus as total output minus what the producers consume. Slavery, feudalism and capitalism differ in who takes it. His alternative is worker co-ops that run the workplace democratically.
- **Soviet Marxism.** Wolff says Marx wrote almost nothing on the state or central planning. He traces "official" Soviet Marxism to Lenin and then Stalin, who declared that holding the state was itself socialism. He links Stalinist violence partly to isolation, including the Allied intervention by the US, Britain, France and Japan.
- **Fridman's objections.** Fridman argues that people form hierarchies naturally, that capitalists take risks, and that competition and new entrants keep firms accountable. Wolff replies that workers carry risk too, that competition leads to monopoly, and that big corporations are themselves central planners.
- **Who gets the gains.** Wolff says capitalists opposed most gains for workers, citing the federal minimum wage, unchanged at $7.25 since 2009, and CEO pay of about 300 times the average worker. He argues automation could have shortened the working day instead of cutting jobs.
- **Nazis and labels.** Wolff says the Nazis used the word "socialist" to reach German workers, but the first people they jailed were communists and socialists.
- **Today's left.** Wolff describes Bernie Sanders and AOC as a mild, FDR-style socialism after the long Cold War taboo. He says the right-wing label "cultural Marxism" has little to do with the real tradition of Lukács, Gramsci, Walter Benjamin and Stuart Hall.
