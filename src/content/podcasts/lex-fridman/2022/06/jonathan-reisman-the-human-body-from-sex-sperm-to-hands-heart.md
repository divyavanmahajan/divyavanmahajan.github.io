## Thesis

Jonathan Reisman, an emergency physician and author of The Unseen Body who has worked in Arctic Alaska, Kamchatka, Antarctica and Nepal, tours the body organ by organ with Lex Fridman. The recurring argument, made by both, is that medicine knows less than it thinks: data is thin, plausible treatments often fail controlled trials, and technology from wearables to artificial organs is slowly changing what doctors can do.

## Key points

- **Data and diagnostics.** Reisman says consumer devices such as smartwatches collect far more data than medicine yet uses, and hopes for drug choices guided by genetics. He notes Moderna built its COVID vaccine from the genome without holding the virus. Fridman argues that short appointments with no long-term data are "very 19th century" and that AI should flag issues for doctors.
- **Theranos.** Reisman calls the goal of testing on a drop of blood worthy and unrealised, pointing out that paediatric labs already get similar information from much smaller tubes.
- **Replacing organs.** Kidneys are the easiest transplant because living donors exist; dialysis has no liver equivalent; mechanical heart-assist devices have become common in about a decade. He mentions Dutch researchers claiming an artificial womb for premature babies within 10 years, and says scaffolding (extracellular matrix) is as important as stem cells for lab-grown organs.
- **Evidence over intuition.** Reisman cites sham-surgery trials in which knee arthroscopy and stents for stable chest pain did not clearly beat placebo. He also credits wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, and DARPA funding, with advances in trauma care such as tourniquets and whole-blood transfusion.
- **Remote and global medicine.** As a cruise-ship doctor in Antarctica he says a brain bleed far from a hospital is usually fatal, and that Australia once removed healthy gallbladders before winter postings. He praises Paul Farmer and Partners in Health, noting that cheap oral rehydration and amoxicillin could save many children.
