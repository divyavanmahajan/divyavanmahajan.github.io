## Thesis

Daniel Negreanu tells Lex Fridman that poker has moved from a game of card sense to one that can be studied with AI solvers, but that the best players still beat humans by spotting and exploiting their deviations from optimal play. He argues that long-term success depends less on any single result than on self-awareness, resilience through bad luck, and the humility to keep learning from younger players.

## Key points

- **GTO versus exploitative play.** Negreanu compares game theory optimal play to throwing rock, paper and scissors a third of the time each: unbeatable but not a winner. He prefers exploitative play, keeps phone notes on every opponent, and cites Michael Addamo's oversized bets, which solvers would call mistakes, as profitable because they target opponents' tendencies.
- **Ranges and bet sizing.** Players now think in ranges of possible hands rather than a single hand; the range should only shrink as a hand progresses, and a very large bet is "polarizing", signalling either the nuts or nothing.
- **Solvers changed who wins.** About five or six years earlier, Negreanu says, AI solvers turned poker into something that can be studied, bringing in data-driven players. He used a data scientist and a coach for six to eight hours a day, but warns against becoming "a slave to the sim".
- **Variance and resilience.** He describes losing about $1.1 million over the 2022 World Series of Poker, losing most all-ins where he was a 60-80% favourite for two weeks, and argues for letting yourself feel despair, sleeping, then resetting.
- **The GOAT debate.** Negreanu names Phil Ivey the greatest for dominating every format, credits Doyle Brunson's longevity, and says Phil Hellmuth has the best WSOP resume but is not the best player today.
- **Ethics, money and love.** He plays high stakes only against people who can afford it, defends Ivey in the edge-sorting case, advises young people to take risks in their 20s, and describes love with his wife Amanda as acceptance without judgment.
