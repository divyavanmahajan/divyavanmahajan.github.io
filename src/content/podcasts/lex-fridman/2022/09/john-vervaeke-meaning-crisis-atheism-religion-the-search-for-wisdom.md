## Thesis

John Vervaeke, a psychologist and cognitive scientist at the University of Toronto, tells Lex Fridman that meaning in life is a sense of connectedness to something real, neither purely subjective nor objective. The same processes that make us intelligent also make us prone to self-deception, and he argues the meaning crisis comes from losing the ecologies of practice that once cultivated wisdom. His answer is a "religion that's not a religion" built on cognitive science and older traditions. Fridman pushes back on several points, including the value of asking about the meaning of life.

## Key points

- **Relevance realization.** Vervaeke says general intelligence rests on zeroing in on what matters out of a combinatorially explosive world. Wisdom, in his model, is coordinating multiple rationalities to overcome self-deception, which he frames through Harry Frankfurt's essay on bullshit and the way advertising makes things salient regardless of truth.
- **Four kinds of knowing.** He distinguishes propositional, procedural, perspectival and participatory knowing, and argues religions transform people mostly at the non-propositional levels, which is the function lost when their creeds were rejected.
- **Non-theism.** Vervaeke calls himself a non-theist: he rejects the shared assumptions of modern theists and atheists that the ground of being is a Supreme Being and that sacredness is about correct beliefs.
- **Distributed cognition.** He cites the Wason selection task, where about 10% of individuals succeed but groups of four reach 80%, and argues dialogue can produce a shared flow state from which myth and ritual emerge.
- **Practices.** He recommends combining a meditative practice, a contemplative one such as metta or the stoic view from above, and a moving practice like Tai Chi that induces flow. He links flow to clear information, tight feedback and error that matters, and warns psychedelics need a "sapiential" framework.
- **Ideology and evil.** Vervaeke describes Marxism and Nazism as pseudo-religions driven by a Promethean spirit, and evil as a parasitic process in distributed cognition rather than a sum of individual immoral choices.
