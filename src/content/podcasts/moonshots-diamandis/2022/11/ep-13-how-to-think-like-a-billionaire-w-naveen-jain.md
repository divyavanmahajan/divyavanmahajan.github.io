## Thesis

Naveen Jain, founder of Infospace, Moon Express and Viome, argues that big problems are easier to solve than small ones. In his view, an audacious goal attracts the best talent, and capital follows the talent. He gives Peter Diamandis a three-question framework for moonshot companies. He then explains how he tried to pass the same drive on to his children by setting an example rather than giving instructions.

## Key points

- **Why this.** Jain tells founders to ask whether success would help a billion people live better. He says that is a capitalist test as well as a philanthropic one, because such products can create a hundred-billion-dollar company.
- **Why now.** A founder should be catching the exponential technologies of the next 3 to 5 years, not using yesterday's. Diamandis adds that the Siri founders built for technology that would only arrive 2 to 5 years later. He also argues for early revenue along the path. Jain says Viome passed on COVID testing because it did not serve its long-term goal.
- **Why me.** Jain asks what questions a founder is asking that others are not. His example is asking "why do we eat food" instead of "how do we grow food on Venus". He calls passion "for hobbies" and says winners need obsession. Both say a failing company should pivot rather than give up its purpose.
- **Outsiders disrupt.** Jain says most disruptors come from outside their industry, citing Elon Musk in space, Uber and Airbnb, because they carry no legacy revenue to protect. Diamandis uses Kodak as his example.
- **Raising founders.** Jain describes his children's ventures: Ankur's Bilt rent credit card, Priyanka's women's health company Evvy, and Neil's work on mortgages. He credits three rules: approval is conditional, children copy what parents do rather than what they say, and children should see a wide range of ideas before choosing a passion. Priyanka's time at Singularity University is his example of the last rule.
- **Mindset and wealth.** Jain lists "imagine", "what if" and "why" as his core mindsets. Both say they are frustrated that few billionaires take big bets. Jain thinks many have not found a purpose yet.
