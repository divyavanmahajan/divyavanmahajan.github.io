## Thesis

Balaji Srinivasan, former CTO of Coinbase and author of The Network State, argues that US regulators, and the FDA in particular, are holding back large amounts of value in medicine and longevity. In his view, reforming them from the inside is harder than building alternatives, much as it was easier to start Bitcoin than to reform the Fed. Peter Diamandis adds his own longevity and moonshot views. Both agree that the moral argument has to be won before the technical and economic ones.

## Key points

- **Regulators as classifiers.** Srinivasan proposes judging the FDA as a binary classifier with type one and type two error rates. He points to drug lag, ACT UP's 1991 protests and Banting and Best's fast path to insulin in the 1920s as costs that usually go unseen.
- **Right to try.** Diamandis floats an "accredited patient" status, modelled on accredited investors, so terminally ill patients can try experimental treatments. Srinivasan backs right-to-try laws.
- **Longevity from a face.** Srinivasan proposes an AI app that estimates age and life expectancy from a photo, trained on photos of people who have died. He would then use it to measure interventions. Both treat it as a possible prize. Diamandis cites a study putting the gain from each extra healthy year at $38 trillion of global GDP.
- **India and telemedicine.** Srinivasan expects India's doctors, generic-drug industry and software talent to push AI and telemedicine diagnostics into the US.
- **Media and the frontier.** Srinivasan wants a "VC model of content" that writes for the best readers rather than the most, plus an investigative-journalism prize on regulatory abuse. He argues that regulatory harmonisation lets US regulators set rules for much of the world.
- **Network states.** Srinivasan sets a goal of a diplomatically recognised network state by about 2032-2033. He compares this to the 12 years it took to go from Bitcoin to El Salvador's adoption.
