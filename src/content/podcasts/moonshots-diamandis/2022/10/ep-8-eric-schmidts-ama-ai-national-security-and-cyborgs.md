## Thesis

In an ask-me-anything session hosted by Peter Diamandis, former Google CEO Eric Schmidt answers founders' questions on leadership, emerging markets, defense and AI. His recurring argument is that technology scales so fast that time is the scarcest resource. In his view, institutions such as governments, defense systems and regulators are not keeping pace, so philanthropists and a new generation of technical talent have to fill the gap.

## Key points

- **Speed at Google.** Schmidt says Larry Page and Sergey Brin supplied the vision and he implemented it at scale. His main lesson is that decisions must be made within days. He cites YouTube overtaking Google Video, which led Google to buy YouTube, and Facebook pulling ahead in social.
- **Hiring divas, not knaves.** Drawing on Bill Campbell, Schmidt separates difficult but principled "divas", whom executives should protect, from self-optimizing "knaves", whom Campbell said to fire quickly.
- **Bioeconomy and Web3.** Schmidt favours non-drug synthetic biology, which he says could create millions of manufacturing jobs in rural states. He argues Web3 has to solve scaling first, pointing to Ethereum gas prices that rise with demand.
- **Emerging markets.** For Africa, Schmidt says the first money will be made in communications and phone-based services. He suggests putting cell towers in dense informal settlements and using translation models to cover local languages. For India, he suggests copying the Chinese companies that copied US models.
- **National security and AI.** After five years advising the US Secretary of Defense, Schmidt says faster, better-targeted weapons have outpaced defenses, and he would build purely defensive systems. He expects AI to be regulated differently by each country. He calls himself relatively pro-China in policy debates, but expects the US and China to stay on an oppositional path.
- **"These ads suck."** Schmidt recalls Page posting printed ads with that note. Over a weekend, engineers outside the ads team built the first ad-targeting system, which Schmidt calls a $150 billion business today.
