## Thesis

Guillaume "Gill" Verdon, founder of Extropic and of the effective accelerationism (e/acc) movement, argues that progress cannot be stopped and that variance and decentralisation, not central control, keep civilisation adaptive. His company aims to raise "intelligence per watt" with a third branch of computing: thermodynamic, probabilistic hardware that uses electrical noise instead of fighting it. Peter Diamandis shares the optimism and frames it as an abundance mindset.

## Key points

- **The moonshot.** Verdon wants a brain-scale processor that is more energy efficient than the brain, which he puts at roughly 14 to 20 watts. The wider goal is to climb the Kardashev scale and scale intelligence through the universe.
- **e/acc and variance.** Verdon says the manifesto began as late-night online conversations. He argues that centralised AI and a "monoculture" are fragile, that markets act as an aligning force on models, and that compute caps would be net negative.
- **No overnight singularity.** Citing Stephen Wolfram's computational irreducibility, Verdon expects steady exponential progress rather than a runaway intelligence explosion. He sees stopping that progress as the bigger risk.
- **Why thermodynamic computing.** Verdon argues that maintaining determinism costs energy (he uses Maxwell's demon to explain why). AI algorithms are natively probabilistic, so running them on stochastic hardware fits better. He claims sampling on picosecond time scales and possible speed-ups for protein and molecular dynamics.
- **Path to market.** Extropic's first prototype was superconducting and needed cooling. Verdon says the next chips will be room-temperature silicon, starting with small edge devices, with the goal of reducing dependence on TSMC in Taiwan. He gives a 15 to 20 year horizon for the full mission.
- **Advice to founders.** Verdon expects AI to commoditise work that has abundant existing data. He urges entrepreneurs toward atypical, deep-tech problems in the physical world.
