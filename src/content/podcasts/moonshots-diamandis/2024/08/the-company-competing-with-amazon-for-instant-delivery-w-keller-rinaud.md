## Thesis

Keller Rinaudo Cliffton, CEO of Zipline, argues that automated logistics will do for physical goods what the internet did for information: near-zero-cost, near-instant delivery to anyone. He tells Peter Diamandis that Zipline got there by starting with one narrow, paid use case (blood in Rwanda), learning by operating, and using permissive regulators before returning to the US. Diamandis frames the conversation as a list of lessons for moonshot entrepreneurs.

## Key points

- **Scale today.** Rinaudo Cliffton says Zipline serves over 4,000 hospitals and health facilities, has flown more than 80 million commercial autonomous miles, has made about 1.1 million deliveries, and operates in eight countries.
- **Rwanda as the start.** He recalls the health minister telling the team to "just do blood". He cites a University of Pennsylvania figure of a 51% drop in maternal mortality at the hospitals Zipline serves, and says it now delivers 75% of Rwanda's blood supply outside the capital.
- **The drone is the easy part.** He estimates the aircraft is about 15% of the complexity. The rest is deconfliction software, distribution-center systems, traffic management for regulators and ordering interfaces.
- **Platform 2 for home delivery.** The new hybrid aircraft docks on a building wall, hovers at about 100 meters and lowers a "Droid" that steers with computer vision to "dinner plate" accuracy. It carries up to 8 pounds, and partners named include Walmart, Cleveland Clinic and Sweetgreen.
- **Lessons for founders.** Rinaudo Cliffton puts his early odds of success below 5%. He says everything takes 10 times longer than planned, early revenue beats unpaid pilots, and hires should be chosen for innate traits over resume experience. Diamandis adds regulatory arbitrage and a "massive transformative purpose".
- **Regulation.** Rinaudo Cliffton argues that US rules such as NEPA protect incumbents and stall clean-energy projects. He links Boeing's problems to a lack of competition.
