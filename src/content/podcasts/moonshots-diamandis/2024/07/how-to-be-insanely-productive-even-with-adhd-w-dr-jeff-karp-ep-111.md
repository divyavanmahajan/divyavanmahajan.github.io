## Thesis

Jeff Karp, a bioengineer at Harvard Medical School and MIT who has co-founded 13 companies, argues that skills like translating research, asking good questions and staying focused come from processes you build, not from innate talent. He traces this view to growing up with undiagnosed ADHD. Peter Diamandis presents the conversation as a lesson for entrepreneurs on building purpose-driven companies.

## Key points

- **Problem definition as a Venn diagram.** Karp says academic projects usually define only the biology or medical problem. A project that can become a product also has to solve manufacturing, patent, regulatory, reimbursement, sales and financing problems, and the real problem definition sits where those circles overlap.
- **An informal advisory board.** For 10 years Karp met patent lawyers, regulatory experts, manufacturers and potential CEOs every two to three weeks. He says these people had no financial stake but became invested because he acted on their advice. He says almost every major project from his lab has spun out into a company.
- **Radical simplicity.** Karp argues that a 10-step synthesis is fine for exploratory work but usually too risky for a translational project. He uses his Profi nasal spray as an example: by using only ingredients already considered safe, the team avoided a drug pathway that would have taken 6 to 10 years.
- **Profi as a business case.** Karp reports 99.99% kill of several respiratory viruses in lab conditions. He says the product is regulated as a cosmetic, so it makes no medical claims. It took about three years to develop, raised $1 million from a single angel investor and sells for about $20 a month.
- **Questions as a learnable skill.** Karp says that after logging audience questions at seminars for months, he found they fell into four or five categories: controls, relevance, overstated conclusions, statistics. Diamandis adds that he uses large language models to ask how someone like Steve Jobs would approach a problem.
- **The book LIT.** Karp's book draws on 40 interviews, including memory champion Nelson Dellis. He also warns that $900 billion a year in marketing and advertising competes for people's attention.
