## Thesis

Recorded live at Moonshots Live 2026, Ben Lamm of Colossal Biosciences tells the Moonshot Mates that biology is reaching an inflection point. He argues that frontier AI models alone will not solve biology; the advantage lies in large comparative genomic data sets and rapidly improving editing and synthesis tools. In his view the limits on designing organisms are now mostly social, ethical and regulatory rather than technical.

## Key points

- **Outsider as founder.** Peter Diamandis notes Lamm started four companies before Colossal, which Diamandis says is worth over $10 billion, with no biology background. Lamm calls that an advantage: he hires experts and asks questions, crediting co-founder George Church.
- **Where AI helps today.** Asked by Dave Blundin about the business model, Lamm says Colossal first used LLMs as middleware linking lab notebooks and Jira. He cites a 40-60% failure rate when repeating published experiments and expects AI to bridge inconsistent naming and suggest experiments over the next 3-5 years, with wet labs still validating results.
- **Data over models.** Lamm argues the data set is worth more than the model. Colossal is working with governments on biobanks for comparative genomics, and a new spin-off is building prediction models that plug into foundation models.
- **The editing curve.** Lamm says most of the field would celebrate 20 edits, while Colossal delivered 300+ at over 90% efficiency a year ago and is testing 1,000. He expects DNA synthesis to overtake multiplex editing, expects prompt-style design of key traits within a decade, and predicts a mammal born fully outside a womb within 24 months.
- **Choosing species.** In response to a Stewart Brand quote, Lamm lists the criteria: ecosystem role, why the species died out, the views of indigenous groups and educational value (the direwolves drew in fantasy fans). He plans herds, not single animals, with 16 mammoth lines in progress.
- **Backing up life.** Lamm calls biodiversity loss the most neglected moonshot and argues it needs government funding, citing a US Fish and Wildlife partnership and work in Dubai. He calls Anthropic's wet-lab announcement validating rather than a threat.
