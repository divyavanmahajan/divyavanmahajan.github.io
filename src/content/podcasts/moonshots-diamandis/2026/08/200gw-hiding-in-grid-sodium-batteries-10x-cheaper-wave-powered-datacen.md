## Thesis

Ramez Naam, founder of Planetary VC, tells the Moonshot Mates that power is cheap next to GPUs but is now the bottleneck for AI, and the scarce part is the poles and wires, not power plants. He argues the fastest near-term gains come from using the existing grid more flexibly, with solar plus batteries next, while fission, fusion, orbital and ocean compute are longer-dated bets.

## Key points

- **Interconnection queues.** Naam says the wait to connect new generation has grown from 15 months to about 45 months. In Texas a new request for hundreds of megawatts may not be served before 2031-2032. By his slide, chips scheduled through 2030 imply about 230 GW of demand against roughly 100 GW of US grid buildout.
- **The hidden 200 GW.** US demand swings between about 400 and 600 GW, so Naam says capacity sits idle off-peak. Citing a paper by Tyler Norris, he claims 1% flexibility unlocks about 100 GW. New Texas rules give interruptible loads a fast path, and he backs a startup that charges on-site batteries at night.
- **Solar and storage.** Naam cites panels at 8 cents a watt, battery prices down 14x since 2010 and sodium-ion possibly another 10x. He describes a 1 GW round-the-clock UAE plant (5 GW solar, 19 GWh storage) at about $6 a watt, and names winter, not night, as solar's hard limit.
- **Nuclear economics.** Naam argues fission is expensive outside China because little is built. Small modular reactors aim to move work into factories, though he doubts commercial units by 2030-2031. He calls Commonwealth Fusion the best-backed fusion bet and says Helion targets power for Microsoft in 2028.
- **Space and ocean compute.** Naam thinks a single gigawatt in orbit by 2030 would be impressive. Diamandis sets out Elon Musk's 100 GW-per-year target as roughly 30,000 Starship launches a year. Naam prefers wave-powered ocean data centres from one of his portfolio companies, targeting about 2 cents a kWh.
- **Pushback.** Blundin argues GPUs are heavily marked up, which could flip the power-versus-chip math. Wissner-Gross questions Naam's view that recursive self-improvement still faces diminishing returns.
