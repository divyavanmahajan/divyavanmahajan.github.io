## Thesis

Jared Isaacman, the 15th NASA administrator, tells Peter Diamandis and the Moonshot Mates that NASA should return to its 1960s playbook: focus on a few very hard goals, learn through many small missions, and let commercial reusable launch carry the cost. He presents the moon base as training for Mars and nuclear propulsion as NASA's distinctive bet.

## Key points

- **Phase one is a junkyard.** Isaacman describes landing landers and rovers on a near monthly basis at the lunar south pole before astronauts arrive in 2028, learning from failures before locking in a "dream state" design. Humanoid robots come in phase two, once Starship and Blue Origin move mass cheaply; he expects one may be smuggled onto an uncrewed lander demo.
- **AI for science.** Through the Genesis program, NASA wants AI to find what it missed in archival data and to help advance propulsion. The DAVINCI Venus probe will use onboard AI to choose which data to send home.
- **Nuclear, the Nautilus way.** SR-1 Freedom reuses the Gateway power and propulsion element and reactor work from INL. Isaacman expects the first crewed Mars mission to use chemically augmented nuclear electric propulsion, and bets NASA, not a private firm, lands first, roughly 15 years out.
- **Budget and culture.** He argues $25 billion a year is plenty if focused, citing the Manhattan Project at $33 billion inflation-adjusted. Programs "too big to fail" become too costly to succeed; modular plans survive changes of administration.
- **China and orbital economy.** Isaacman expects China to meet its 2030 moon goal, calling the relationship a competition. He is measured on lunar economics but expects orbital data centers to happen given SpaceX's capital.
- **UAPs.** Asked by Alexander Wissner-Gross about alleged legacy programs, Isaacman says released footage includes unexplained sightings, but he has seen no evidence of crashed craft or biologics.
