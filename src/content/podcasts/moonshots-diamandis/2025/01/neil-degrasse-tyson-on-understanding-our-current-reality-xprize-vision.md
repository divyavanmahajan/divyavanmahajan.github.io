## Thesis

Recorded live at XPRIZE Visioneering, Neil deGrasse Tyson tells Peter Diamandis that human brains evolved for linear thinking, so people keep underestimating how much the world will change. Walking through 30-year slices of history from his book Star Messenger, he argues that every era thinks it is the special one. Big projects get funded because of war, money or the will of rulers, not curiosity.

## Key points

- **Linear brains.** Tyson uses the algae-covered lake and the rice-on-a-chessboard story to show how badly people read doubling. Diamandis adds his own test for his sons: a million dollars now, or a penny doubled daily for 30 days.
- **Doubling of knowledge.** Tyson says that in the Princeton astrophysics library, the Astrophysical Journal's output doubled about every 30 years. He says patents show a doubling time of a few decades.
- **Failed forecasts.** He cites a New York Central Railroad head in 1900, who doubted 20th-century transport would match the 19th, and says Orville Wright doubted in 1908 that anyone would fly from New York to Paris. He also mentions the "manure catastrophe" that cars solved within about ten years.
- **The flat-curve illusion.** Tyson says that on a linear plot, every point on an exponential looks like the steep present, which is why each generation feels it lives at the peak.
- **Three motivators.** Tyson says Apollo was paid for by Cold War fear after Sputnik and Gagarin, not by curiosity, so it ended once the race was won. In his list, the drivers of costly projects are fear of dying, fear of dying poor, and the will of royalty or deity.
- **Predictions for 2050.** He predicts genome-matched drugs with no side effects, fully self-driving electric cars, and a solar system opened up like the interstate system, with asteroid mining removing wars over resources. In the audience Q&A, he suggests an asteroid-mining XPRIZE.
