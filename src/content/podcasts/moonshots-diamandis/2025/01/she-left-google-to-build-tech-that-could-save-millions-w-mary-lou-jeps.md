## Thesis

Mary Lou Jepsen, founder and CEO of Openwater and formerly at Intel, Google and Facebook/Oculus, argues that medical devices can follow the cost curve of consumer electronics. Her company combines smartphone camera chips, lasers and ultrasound to build low-cost imaging and treatment devices. She argues that the business model matters as much as the technology: open-sourcing the platform spreads development costs and builds trust. Peter Diamandis, who says he is an investor in and adviser to Openwater, frames it as converging exponential technologies.

## Key points

- **Career path.** Jepsen describes building early micro-displays, co-founding One Laptop per Child with Nicholas Negroponte, starting the screen company Pixel Qi, and then working at Google X and Oculus. She left in 2016 to found Openwater, a name Peter Gabriel suggested.
- **Shrinking hardware.** She says Openwater went from multi-million-dollar optical lab setups to hospital carts costing $100,000 to $500,000, and now to a $10,000 console. She expects the price to approach that of a smartphone at volume.
- **The cost problem.** Jepsen cites roughly $658 million and 13 years as the average capitalized cost to get a new device approved in the US. She says 85% of that is device development, not trials.
- **Clinical claims.** She describes research on stroke detection (151 patients at Penn and Brown) and early ultrasound studies on cancer cells and depression. She presents these as early results; the FDA has asked for many more patients.
- **Open source.** Vitalik Buterin offered $50 million if Openwater went open source. Jepsen says the company released all 68 patents, its software and its hardware under AGPL. She compares the approach to Android, with regulatory approvals working like apps on a shared platform.
- **Advice to founders.** Jepsen tells entrepreneurs to study the history of science, revisit ideas that were abandoned decades ago, and measure a company by its impact rather than its headcount.
