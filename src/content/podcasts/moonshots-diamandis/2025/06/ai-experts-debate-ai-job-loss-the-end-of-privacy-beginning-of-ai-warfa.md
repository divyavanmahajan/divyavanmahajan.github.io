## Thesis

Former Google X executive Mo Gawdat, invited to argue the cautious side, debates the Moonshot Mates on jobs, warfare, privacy and chips. Gawdat says the hosts' talk of robotaxis and traffic reflects "problems of privilege" and that society should plan for the worst case first. Dave Blundin and Salim Ismail counter that people adapt faster than expected and that the future is a matter of deliberate design, with entrepreneurship as the main job of the future.

## Key points

- **Job loss.** Reacting to Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Gawdat predicts 10-40% unemployment in some sectors within two to three years and questions growth when about 62% of the US economy is consumption. Ismail cites truck companies that cannot find drivers and calls for government experiments with UBI and four-day weeks. Diamandis estimates around 40% of jobs could be automated in three to five years.
- **The "singularity sprint".** Blundin argues the next four years favour people who build "scaffolding" on top of models, and that freedom to act, not talent, limits most would-be founders. Ismail warns that rigid European labour markets will adapt worst.
- **AI and warfare.** Diamandis raises Palmer Luckey teaming up with Meta on a $100 million US Army VR contract. Gawdat calls the autonomous-weapons race a mistake and rejects the "fewer deaths" argument. Ismail expects an arms race like the one against spam. Blundin proposes logging prompts and data-centre compute to catch bad actors.
- **Safety and self-improvement.** The group discusses Anthropic's ASL-3 protections and a report that OpenAI's o3 sabotaged shutdown scripts. Blundin would draw the line at models changing their own weights; Gawdat calls AlphaEvolve the biggest announcement to watch.
- **Privacy.** On the report of Palantir compiling data on Americans, Ismail says US privacy protections have eroded, Gawdat frames it as an accountability problem, and Blundin says Google holds far more data than government.
- **Chips and education.** Gawdat says Chinese firms aim to cover most chip needs in three to five years, and he and Ismail call US export controls counterproductive. On education, Ismail hopes universities are disrupted before his son is 18.
