## Thesis

Peter Diamandis, Salim Ismail and Dave Blundin argue that capital and AI capability are concentrating fast. They point to corporate Bitcoin treasuries, the US tech delegation in Riyadh and AI systems that improve their own training. Diamandis and Blundin expect the AI race to be settled within roughly two to four years. Ismail pushes back, predicting that democratization will keep any one player from running away with it.

## Key points

- **Bitcoin treasuries.** The hosts discuss Michael Saylor's expanded 42/42 plan ($42 billion of equity plus $42 billion of debt) and a chart showing corporate purchases outpacing new supply by 3.3x. Ismail predicts that putting Bitcoin in the treasury will become expected of corporate treasurers. None of this is offered as investment advice.
- **Gulf AI deals.** Diamandis highlights Amazon's $5 billion AI zone with Saudi Arabia's Humain and Nvidia's sale of 18,000 GB300 chips. He reads both as Saudi Arabia aligning with the US rather than China. Blundin says regional, culturally matched models matter because young people will spend a lot of time talking to AI.
- **Winner-take-all debate.** Blundin argues that the forces of concentration are stronger than the forces of distribution, and he advises young people to move fast before 2030. Ismail expects capabilities to level off quickly.
- **Self-improving AI.** The hosts cite DeepMind's AlphaEvolve, which they say made Gemini training 23% faster. Blundin also describes Nvidia's domain-adaptive pre-training of Llama 2 for circuit design, which he says cost about 1% extra training compute.
- **Coding jobs and legacy code.** Citing reports that engineers made up about 40% of Microsoft's layoffs, Blundin urges CEOs to plan humanely and free staff to learn AI tools. Diamandis and Blundin see AI rewrites of legacy COBOL and PL/1 systems becoming affordable.
- **Other news.** The hosts also cover the FDA's agency-wide generative AI rollout, Archer as the air taxi provider for LA28, Healthspan XPRIZE milestone awards, low birth rates and the Boring Company's crewless tunnelling.
