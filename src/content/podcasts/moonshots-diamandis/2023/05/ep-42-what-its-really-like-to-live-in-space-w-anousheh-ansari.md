## Thesis

Peter Diamandis and Anousheh Ansari, now CEO of the XPRIZE Foundation, walk through her life from a childhood in Tehran to the 2006 flight that made her the first female private space explorer. Ansari argues that accepting risk, keeping curiosity alive and starting from the end goal are what produce moonshots. She also argues that incentive prizes are a high-leverage use of philanthropic money.

## Key points

- **Telecom founder to prize backer.** Ansari, her husband Hamid and brother-in-law Amir left MCI to start Telecom Technologies, which built an early soft switch for voice over IP. Diamandis recalls it sold to Sonus Networks for over a billion dollars. Ansari says watching Dennis Tito buy a ride to the space station pushed her to grow and exit faster.
- **Funding the Ansari XPRIZE.** Diamandis says he pitched about 150 wealthy people for the $10 million purse before finding Ansari in a Fortune list. Burt Rutan's SpaceShipOne won it with two flights, including Brian Binnie's on October 4.
- **Risk as a right.** Ansari says she accepted that teams might die trying and that people willing to take the risk have the right to choose it. She argues that using astronaut deaths as a reason to stop dishonours them.
- **Eleven days in space.** She trained for about nine months in Star City as a backup and got the seat three weeks before launch when the primary flyer was medically disqualified. She describes two days in a cramped capsule with motion sickness, then nine days on the station.
- **Leadership and moonshots.** Ansari tells women to drop the pressure to be perfect and to treat failures as lessons. She says entrepreneurs should only bet on big leaps, not 10% gains.
- **Why billionaires hold back.** Ansari blames a scarcity mindset. Diamandis adds that the Forbes list gamifies keeping wealth. They say XPRIZE brings 10 to 30 times the purse in investment. Her wish list: a near-warp engine, almost free clean energy, and a real voice for 15- to 25-year-olds.
