## Thesis

Mark Hyman, a functional medicine physician promoting his book Young Forever, argues that medicine is in a paradigm shift: from "sick care" aimed at single diseases to a systems view that targets the hallmarks of ageing behind them. Peter Diamandis frames this as the start of a period in which science may add healthy decades. Both say that experimental therapeutics matter less than getting the everyday basics right.

## Key points

- **A field that was off-limits.** Hyman says that until recently, talking about slowing or reversing ageing was a black mark for a credible scientist. He credits billions of dollars of new research funding with changing that, and says his publisher reports pre-orders about tenfold his earlier books.
- **Hallmarks as targets.** Hyman walks through the nine accepted hallmarks of ageing and adds a tenth, the microbiome. He presents them as treatment targets for drugs, lifestyle or cell therapies, and says the question functional medicine adds is why they go wrong in the first place.
- **Measurement changes the game.** Hyman cites DNA methylation tests as a new biomarker for biological age. He says his own test puts him at 43 against a chronological 63, and cites a study claiming an eight-week programme reversed biological age by three years.
- **Drugs versus lifestyle.** On metformin, Hyman points to a diabetes prevention trial where, he says, the drug cut progression by 31% against 58% for lifestyle change. He describes rapamycin dosing as guesswork for now and wants more human trials.
- **Regenerative medicine pipeline.** The two discuss exosomes, plasma exchange, hyperbaric oxygen and senolytics, mostly through personal anecdotes. Diamandis says his company Celularity is starting studies of placenta-derived cells, and describes Fountain Life as vetting therapies for safety before offering them.
- **Slow diffusion.** Diamandis cites a 17-year delay between a breakthrough and its use in clinics. Hyman blames Kuhn-style resistance to new paradigms.
