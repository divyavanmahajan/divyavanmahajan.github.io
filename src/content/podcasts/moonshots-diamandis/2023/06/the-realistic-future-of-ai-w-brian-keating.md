## Thesis

Brian Keating, a UC San Diego astrophysicist and self-described "AI minimalist" and "alien life minimalist", argues that both the fear of AGI and the confidence in extraterrestrial intelligence rest on estimates with no error analysis. Peter Diamandis takes the optimistic side on both questions. He sees AI as the main tool for solving humanity's grand challenges and argues that intelligent life is probably common in the universe.

## Key points

- **Two new intelligences.** Diamandis says humanity is giving birth to AI as a separate intelligence and also to a human-AI hybrid. Keating asks whether an AI could feel joy or pain, and both suggest that today's large language models act mostly as a mirror of human data.
- **Will AI progress saturate?** Keating notes that his Department of Energy compute allocations at Lawrence Berkeley shrink every year as demand rises. He expects AGI could hit similar limits, from heavy demand and from lawyers. Diamandis counters with four drivers: compute still doubling every 18-24 months, labelled data doubling yearly, more efficient training algorithms, and a flood of capital.
- **A Drake equation for AI.** Keating wants AI risk assessed the way he teaches data analysis, with error bars. His San Diego Zoo example gives about 7,000 visitors, with an uncertainty of plus or minus 10,000.
- **Ranking AI risks.** Diamandis lists job loss, misuse by bad actors, and a "terrible twos" phase for early AGI. He calls a Terminator scenario highly improbable but expects voice-cloning deepfakes to disrupt the 2024 election.
- **The alien debate.** Keating assumes eight hurdles to technological life at 0.1% each, which gives odds of 10^-24 against roughly 10^24 stars, so zero or one other civilisation. Diamandis points to life's building blocks in interstellar space and to planets turning up everywhere after astronomers once thought they were rare.
- **Free STEM education.** Asked what XPRIZE he would want, Keating proposes a free university available around the clock to everyone on Earth. Both say education and healthcare are this decade's two big disruptions.
