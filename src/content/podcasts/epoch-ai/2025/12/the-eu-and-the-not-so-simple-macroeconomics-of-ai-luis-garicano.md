## Thesis

LSE economist and former MEP Luis Garicano argues that AI's economic effects turn on whether it becomes autonomous: while it needs human supervision, humans remain the bottleneck, but once it can work alone, whole professions can shift abruptly. He sees early signs of this in falling junior hiring, and warns that the EU's regulation could leave Europe paying higher global interest rates without the growth needed to service its debts.

## Key points

- **Autonomy as the threshold.** Garicano expects growth spurts rather than sustained explosive growth, limited by bottlenecks such as FDA approval. The jump comes "the moment the AI becomes autonomous", for example an AI lawyer rather than a lawyer assisted by AI.
- **Short-run disruption.** If a sector's price falls to zero, consumer surplus rises but measured GDP can fall, and specialists such as neurosurgeons may face long-term unemployment as their human capital loses value.
- **Junior jobs and superstars.** He cites the "Seniority-Biased Technological Change" paper (about 62 million workers) and Brynjolfsson's "Canaries in the Coal Mine": in AI-exposed jobs, junior hiring is falling while senior roles keep growing. At the top, superstar effects push pay up, as with the $100 million offers to AI engineers.
- **The missing training ladder.** With Luis Rayo, Garicano models apprentices who pay for training with menial work. AI devalues that work, and training survives only if AI raises the value of a trained expert by more than it replaces the trainee.
- **EU AI Act and GDPR.** He describes high-risk rules for education and health (error-free data, ten years of records, registration with 55 authorities), claims about 15 GDPR studies all found harm to business, and proposes a "smart second mover" strategy that focuses on the implementation layer.
- **R without G.** Drawing on work by Auclert and co-authors, he expects AI to raise interest rates globally. Europe, with French debt near 120% of GDP plus pension liabilities, could face higher R without higher G. Potlogea pushes back, citing precautionary saving and Europe's position as a net creditor.
