## Thesis

Ege Erdil (the longest timelines at Epoch) and Matthew Barnett (among the shortest) compare their forecasts for transformative AI. Both argue that AI's economic impact will come from automating ordinary cognitive and physical work across the economy, not from a "country of geniuses in a data center". Their disagreement is mostly about how fast AI will gain agency, long-horizon coherence and common sense, skills where Erdil sees no clear trend to extrapolate.

## Key points

- **The numbers.** For sustained GDP growth above 5% a year, Erdil gives a median of about 35 years and Barnett 10 to 15. Erdil says his timelines have shortened from about 40 years since a November 2023 debate, mainly because lab revenue grew faster than he expected.
- **Moravec's paradox.** Erdil argues that skills almost every human shares, such as agency and planning, are harder for AI than math, where models went from below the 5th percentile to top competitive programmers. He points to Claude's struggles with Pokemon and to Colin Fraser's simple file puzzles that reasoning models fail.
- **Compute and paradigms.** Erdil gives about 15% (25% if the compute-centric model is right) to human-brain-level AI by 2030. He notes past breakthroughs took about nine orders of magnitude of scaling, while only about three more are likely this decade.
- **Scale over genius.** Erdil argues innovation comes from scale effects, experiments and complementary investment (Edison's infrastructure, simultaneous discovery of calculus), so adding AI researchers hits diminishing returns. Barnett adds that most efficiency gains come from incremental work across supply chains.
- **Economics of the outcome.** Both say economists understate the technology while AI-risk writers ignore economics. Barnett argues wages could fall below subsistence even with comparative advantage. Erdil compares Daron Acemoglu's estimate of 0.5% GDP impact in 10 years with his own median of about 5% by 2030.
- **Alignment views.** Erdil says current models such as GPT-4o and Claude 3.7 Sonnet seem to have better values than the median person. Both argue that risk attitudes and the causes of war matter more than value differences.
