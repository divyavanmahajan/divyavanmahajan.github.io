## Thesis

Alex Imas (Google DeepMind, University of Chicago) argues that services where a human in the loop is part of the value, the "relational sector", could stay scarce and keep people employed, but stresses that economists lack the data to forecast it. Phil Trammell (Epoch, Stanford) expects new varieties of machine-made goods to keep the human share small, as it has been historically. Dwarkesh Patel pushes on how wealth will be taxed and spread.

## Key points

- **Forecasting humility.** Imas recalls that David Ricardo correctly foresaw the automation of the jobs of his day but missed new service jobs; US prime-age employment is now at its highest since 2000. He calls for a "Manhattan Project for data" on demand elasticities and job creation.
- **Relational value.** In Imas's experiments, a one-off human-made art print is valued well above an AI version, but the human print's price drops a lot when 500 copies exist, while the AI print's does not change. AI work is already seen as a commodity.
- **Variety beats satiation.** Trammell's analogy: a 1400s Mongolian economist would have predicted spending shifting to singers once yurts and yogurt were abundant. Instead new goods appeared. Patel notes an H100 costs more to rent now than three years ago.
- **Messy middle and redistribution.** Both guests think a scenario with mass automation but little wealth creation is a narrow window. Imas worries UBI makes people dependent on whoever holds power and contrasts it with universal basic capital, whose weak spot is targeting; Trammell suggests broad taxes used to buy and distribute shares.
- **No demand collapse.** Imas says the Citrini recession scenario needs implausible conditions: rich people's demand hard-capped and savings not invested. Yale Budget Lab data shows little AI labour impact so far.
- **Greedy accumulators and poorer countries.** Patel and Trammell argue agents that never satiate in capital could come to dominate wealth. For countries like Nigeria, the guests favour indexing AI gains, helped by open models and IPOs, over retraining alone.
