## Thesis

Ada Palmer, a University of Chicago historian and author of *Inventing the Renaissance*, argues that Petrarch's project of raising better rulers on the Roman classics failed on its own terms. Two centuries later, though, it led through libraries, print and Machiavelli's case-study method to Bacon's science and eventually a cure for the plague. She tells Dwarkesh Patel that history rarely goes "my way" but can still go well, and that big technologies arrive as a long series of revolutions.

## Key points

- **Florence's odd republic.** Palmer describes a commoner republic that wiped out its nobility, hired a noble police chief and banished him after his term, and chose nine guild merchants by lottery to rule by consensus from a locked tower. The Medici gained control because so many people were on their payroll that they were bound to make up a plurality of any random draw.
- **Resistance that lost still won.** Even after the Medici became dukes, they routed the Vasari Corridor around the Mannelli family's tower rather than seize it. Palmer argues the republic's long fight left Florentines with more rights than people under tyrants like Alfonso d'Este.
- **Gutenberg's distribution problem.** Gutenberg borrowed about $1.5 million for paper, printed around 300 Bibles and could sell only a handful in his town. Printing paid only after his bankrupt apprentices moved to Venice, a shipping hub, and later through book fairs and quick-turnaround pamphlets. Printers took about 40 years to make money, much as e-books waited for the Kindle.
- **Leonardo the saboteur.** Palmer calls Leonardo and Brunelleschi inventors but not scientists, because they hid or burned their methods. Bacon's "honeybee" ideal and England's new academy of sciences made sharing results the measure of achievement.
- **Lost books and paper.** Most ancient texts were lost not at Alexandria but between 400 and 600 AD, when papyrus decayed and monks chose what to copy onto costly parchment.
- **Censors chase the wrong things.** Enlightenment censors worried about Jansenist treatises, not Voltaire. Of 12 Inquisition trials about science, only Giordano Bruno was executed. After Galileo, inquisitors re-ran experiments, which Palmer says effectively invented peer review.
