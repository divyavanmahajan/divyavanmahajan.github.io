## Thesis

Using Kepler, Copernicus and Darwin as examples, Terence Tao tells Dwarkesh Patel that scientific progress depends on slow verification, persuasion and judgement, not just a flood of hypotheses. AI is strong on breadth and weak on depth. It does not yet build on partial progress, so Tao expects science to be reorganised around human-AI collaboration rather than full automation.

## Key points

- **Kepler as a high-temperature LLM.** Dwarkesh suggests Kepler resembled an LLM sampling at high temperature, trying random relationships (Platonic solids, musical harmonies) against Tycho Brahe's data. Tao adds that Brahe's measurements, ten times more precise than earlier ones, deserve equal credit, and that Kepler's third law rested on only about six data points. Johann Bode's similar curve fit later failed when Neptune was found.
- **Better theories can look worse.** Copernicus's circles were less accurate than Ptolemy's refined geocentric model. Tao argues that whether an idea proves fruitful depends on later work and on culture, so it can't simply be scored by reinforcement learning.
- **Erdős problems and selection bias.** AI has helped solve about 50 of roughly 1,100 Erdős problems, mostly ones with almost no literature, and then progress stalled. Systematic sweeps show AI tools succeed on a given problem about 1-2% of the time, and publicised wins hide the failures.
- **Richer, not deeper.** Tao says his papers now carry more code, plots and literature review. Producing them without AI would take five times longer, but the hardest core reasoning still happens with pen and paper.
- **Missing pieces.** Tao says AIs are like jumping robots: they reach low walls but can't hold a foothold and climb from it. He wants a semi-formal language for mathematical strategy and plausibility, alongside Lean for proofs. He thinks Lean proofs, even unreadable ones, can be broken down and studied lemma by lemma.
- **Serendipity and careers.** Tao defends unplanned encounters and some distraction, noting he loses inspiration after a few months at the Institute for Advanced Study. He tells students to stay adaptable, since even high schoolers can now contribute through AI and Lean.
