## Thesis

After two days touring Intel's most advanced fab, Dwarkesh Patel asks its engineers how chips will keep improving and how much more the industry can produce. Better chips are coming through new transistor designs, wiring and packaging. CTO Pushkar Ranade warns, however, that the world's stock of tools caps leading-edge output at roughly double today's level, while AI compute has been growing about 3x a year.

## Key points

- **Bringing up a node.** Chris Auth says 14A has been in development for more than three years and involves about 2,000 process steps. Intel's lesson from 10nm, which slipped from 2016 to 2018, is to limit how much innovation goes into each node so it stays on schedule.
- **Why High-NA EUV suits Intel.** Auth explains that backside power relaxes the wiring pitch on the front of the chip, so Intel can print it with a single High-NA pass. TSMC, without backside power, would still need multiple passes.
- **Urgency on the line.** At Intel's "Hot Topics" meeting, Kevin Fischer decides how much risk to take on fixes such as unwanted epitaxy nodules. He can send key experiments through the fab as "HotBox 1" lots.
- **The research pipeline.** Jack Kavalieros says ruthenium overtakes copper at around a 20-nanometre pitch, because it can be etched and needs no liner. Stacked CFET transistors take years to reach production because deep etches and fills are hard to get right.
- **Hybrid bonding.** Tyler Osborn describes how interconnect pitch has shrunk from 130-150 micrometres 15 years ago to 25 micrometres in Foveros production, with under 10 micrometres in the lab. He cites an ECTC paper showing reliability data at a 0.45-micron pitch.
- **Capacity and catching up.** Ranade says existing tools could support about 2x more leading-edge wafers, not 10x. He says about 80% of tools carry over from 18A to 14A and about 80% of a fab's costs are fixed. In his view Intel is close to TSMC on technology but behind on scale. He also asks whether fabs really need today's clean-room infrastructure.
