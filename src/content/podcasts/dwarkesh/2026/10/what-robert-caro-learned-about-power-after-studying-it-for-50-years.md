## Thesis

Robert Caro, biographer of Robert Moses and Lyndon Johnson, tells Dwarkesh Patel that every so often a political genius appears who gets big things built or passed despite all the obstacles. Power is what makes such people effective, and it also hardens them. Caro also explains how he researches and structures his books.

## Key points

- **A little Moses today?** Patel points to the Second Avenue subway, where about a mile and a half has opened at a cost of over $4 billion, and to California's $15 billion rail spending. Caro answers that people in the 1920s also said nothing could be built, until Moses came along. He does not want Moses's ruthlessness, but he says someone has to find a way to build.
- **The idealist who hardened.** Caro says the early Moses worked on civil-service reform and opened Jones Beach to New Yorkers. The later Moses evicted tens of thousands of people for the Cross Bronx Expressway and set out to destroy Henry Epstein, who had proposed moving the route two blocks south.
- **Watching power fade.** Caro recalls that during his last long interview, no one phoned Moses's office. If he had an eighth interview, he would ask Moses whether he realised he was changing.
- **Johnson's leverage.** Caro says Johnson's famous persuasion worked because senators knew he could give or withhold committee seats and bills. In Caro's view, Kennedy lacked Johnson's drive to change America, which went back to Johnson's year teaching poor children in Cotulla.
- **Method.** Before writing, Caro reduces each book to one sentence or, at most, a paragraph. He cut 350,000 words from a 1,050,000-word *Power Broker* manuscript and did about 700 interviews for that book alone.
- **Archive and loneliness.** Caro gave his archive to the New-York Historical Society for much less money than other places offered, so it would be easy to reach in New York. He describes the isolation of Hill Country ranch life and his own habit of switching off the phone to concentrate.
