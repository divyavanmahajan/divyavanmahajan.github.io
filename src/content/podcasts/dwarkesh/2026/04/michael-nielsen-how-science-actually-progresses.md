## Thesis

Michael Nielsen, quantum computing pioneer and research fellow at the Astera Institute, and Dwarkesh Patel use the history of relativity, heliocentrism and evolution to ask how science recognises progress before experiments settle the question. Nielsen argues there is no fixed process: bottlenecks arise exactly where previous methods stop working, so progress depends on keeping many research programmes alive. Patel draws the implication that AI cannot simply "close the verification loop" on science the way it does with unit tests.

## Key points

- **Michelson-Morley revisited.** Nielsen says the 1887 experiment tested competing ether theories rather than disproving "the" ether, that Michelson believed in the ether until he died, and that Einstein's thinking did not hinge on it. Muon decay measurements around 1940 later confirmed time dilation, long after physicists had adopted Einstein's view.
- **Long, hostile loops.** Patel cites Aristarchus' heliocentrism, which waited until stellar parallax was measured in 1838, and Prout's whole-number atomic weights, which looked wrong for decades until isotopes were understood. Nielsen notes that the same reasoning found Neptune but failed with the hypothetical planet Vulcan, and that most anomalies turn out to be mundane, like the Pioneer spacecraft's thermal drift.
- **AlphaFold.** Nielsen says most of its success came from decades of experimental structure data in the Protein Data Bank. He suggests such models may become a new kind of explanation that we can mine with interpretability, as Magnus Carlsen reportedly learned from AlphaZero.
- **Why Darwin was late.** Nielsen credits deep time from Lyell's geology as a key enabler, and says Darwin's achievement was showing how central natural selection is across biology.
- **Aliens and the tech tree.** Nielsen expects the science-and-technology tree to be so large that other civilisations would explore different branches, which Patel notes implies large gains from trade.
- **Credit and learning.** Nielsen sees the attribution economy of science as a social construct that open science tries to update. For deep learning, he recommends demanding creative artifacts and time spent stuck over constant flow.
