## Thesis

Elon Musk tells Dwarkesh Patel and John Collison that AI will soon hit a power wall on Earth: chip output grows exponentially while electricity outside China is flat. He predicts that within 30-36 months space will be the cheapest place to run AI, and that whoever scales hardware fastest, which he expects to be xAI, will lead. His recurring management idea is to keep attacking the current "limiting factor".

## Key points

- **Why space.** Musk says a solar panel produces about five times more power in orbit, with no batteries, weather or night. Gas turbine vanes and blades are sold out through 2030, and he expects chips to start piling up unpowered towards the end of the year.
- **Scale targets.** He predicts a few hundred gigawatts of AI launched per year within five years. Patel estimates 100 gigawatts at around 10,000 Starship launches. SpaceX and Tesla are building towards 100 gigawatts a year of solar cell production. Musk also wants a lunar mass driver for petawatt-scale launches.
- **TeraFab.** Musk says chips become the constraint once power is solved, with memory his biggest worry. He plans a fab doing logic, memory and packaging, starting small, and wants a million wafers a month by 2030.
- **Grok and alignment.** He doubts humans can control far smarter AI. He argues xAI's mission of understanding the universe, plus rigorous truth-seeking, gives the best odds, and cites HAL in *2001* as a warning against making AI lie. He praises Anthropic's interpretability work.
- **Optimus and China.** Musk names the hand, real-world intelligence and scale manufacturing as the hard problems. He describes an "Optimus Academy" of 10,000-30,000 robots doing self-play. He argues the US cannot out-labour China, which has about four times the population.
- **SpaceX lessons and DOGE.** He explains the switch to stainless steel for Starship (about 50x cheaper than carbon fibre, similar strength-to-weight at cryogenic temperatures). He also defends DOGE, citing missing Treasury payment appropriation codes, while Patel questions his half-trillion-dollar fraud figure.
