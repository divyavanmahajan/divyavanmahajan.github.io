## Thesis

Brett Harrison, who led FTX US after careers at Jane Street and Citadel Securities, tells Dwarkesh Patel that FTX's problems looked like bad management rather than fraud from where he sat: a tiny inner circle controlled the code and decisions while Sam Bankman-Fried focused on media. He argues the fraud was deliberately hidden, and that independent boards and enforcement, not employee suspicion, are the real safeguards.

## Key points

- **HFT and market efficiency.** Harrison defends passive investing and latency competition, describing tricks such as pre-building a TCP/IP order message in the network card buffer, and argues faster price discovery is worth having.
- **Concentrated control.** He says over 90% of FTX's code was written by Gary Wang and Nishad Singh, who approved all pull requests, and that requests to grow a separate US developer team were repeatedly refused.
- **SBF's leadership.** Harrison describes Bankman-Fried as conflict-avoidant and absent, spending days on calls with investors and media; when told he was doing too much PR, SBF reportedly replied it was the best thing for the company.
- **Resignation and threats.** Harrison's letter asked for weekly communication, separate US executives and a bigger tech team; he says a deputy then warned him SBF would destroy his reputation, and that SBF later told people he had been fired.
- **Red flags in hindsight.** He cites mid-2022 bonuses paid two to three months late, but says audited financials and public volumes gave no hint of missing customer funds.
- **Architect.** His new company builds unified trading infrastructure across centralized exchanges, DeFi and custodians, designed so users never hand over private credentials.
