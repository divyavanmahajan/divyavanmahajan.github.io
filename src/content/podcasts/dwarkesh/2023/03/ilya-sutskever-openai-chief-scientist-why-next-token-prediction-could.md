## Thesis

Ilya Sutskever, OpenAI's co-founder and chief scientist, tells Dwarkesh Patel that next-token prediction is not limited to imitating people: a good enough predictor must understand what produces the data, and can extrapolate to a hypothetical person wiser than anyone. He expects the current paradigm to go very far, sees reliability as the main risk to economic impact, and warns that aligning models smarter than us will be hard.

## Key points

- **Reliability is the bottleneck.** Asked why AI might disappoint economically by 2030, Sutskever names reliability: if people must double-check every answer, value is capped. He compares today's models to self-driving cars that look capable but still need work.
- **Beyond imitation.** He says models are poor at multi-step reasoning only when not allowed to "think out loud", and that most RL data is already AI-generated, with humans training the reward model; he imagines humans doing 1% of the work.
- **Data and hardware.** Sutskever says the data situation is "still quite good" but will run out eventually, multimodal is a fruitful direction, and hardware is not a limitation. He says OpenAI left robotics because there was no path to enough data, and calls TPUs and GPUs almost the same.
- **Alignment.** He expects multiple partial definitions rather than one mathematical one, combining adversarial testing and interpretability, ideally a small well-understood network verifying a large one. He suggests academia can contribute meaningfully here.
- **Business.** On the projected $1 billion 2024 revenue, he cites growth seen since the GPT-3 API, DALL-E and ChatGPT; against commoditisation he points to continued progress and specialisation, and praises Microsoft and Azure as a partner.
- **Progress was likely inevitable.** He argues data, GPUs and transformers arrived together because of cheaper transistors, and that without him and Geoffrey Hinton deep learning might have been delayed by a modest number of years.
