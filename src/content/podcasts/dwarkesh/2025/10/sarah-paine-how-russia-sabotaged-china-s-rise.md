## Thesis

In a live lecture and Q&A with Dwarkesh Patel, military historian Sarah Paine argues that Russia and China both follow the rules of continental empire: avoid two-front wars and allow no great power on your borders. From the mid-19th to the mid-20th century a strong Russia used that logic to keep a weak China down. Today China is strong and Russia weak, so she expects the partnership to end on China's terms, and urges the West to manage the pair through alliances and growth rather than hot war.

## Key points

- **Treaty losses.** Paine says Russia offered help against Britain and France during the Opium Wars in exchange for the Treaties of Aigun (1858) and Peking (1860); with Outer Mongolia, its total gains exceed all US territory east of the Mississippi.
- **Scripting Chinese politics.** She cites the Triple Intervention, the hollow Karakhan Manifesto, the First United Front that ended in Chiang Kai-shek's Shanghai massacre of Communists, and a 1929 war in which over 100,000 Soviet troops kept the Manchurian railways.
- **Stripping Manchuria.** After the 1945 August Storm, Russia removed 83% of Manchuria's electrical power equipment and 86% of mining equipment, took 640,000 Japanese POWs, and Stalin told Mao to halt at the Yangtze.
- **The split.** Paine traces the Sino-Soviet split to Mao's grievances, the Taiwan Strait crises, China's 1964 bomb and the 1969 clash over Zhenbao Island, after which Russia asked the US whether it could strike China.
- **Russian decline.** Soviet growth trailed US growth by 1-2% for a decade before Gorbachev, with budgets up to 55% dependent on energy, while Deng's reforms gave China about 20 years of double-digit growth.
- **The reversal.** Paine argues Putin is draining Russia in Ukraine while the Belt and Road peels away Central Asia, and Siberia holds what China wants, including Lake Baikal's 20%+ of world surface fresh water.
