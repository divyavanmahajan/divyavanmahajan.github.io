## Thesis

Austin Vernon, a chemical engineer working on CO2 electrolysis, tells Dwarkesh Patel that complexity is the hidden constraint across software, car manufacturing and nuclear power, and that very cheap solar electricity will open up new uses for energy rather than just replacing old ones. He thinks simpler designs, whether in factories, reactors or electrolyzers, beat large and complex ones.

## Key points

- **Starship as a weapon.** Vernon argues that Starship cuts launch costs so far that it could deliver cheap kinetic projectiles in large numbers and overwhelm ship defences. He adds that tungsten rods are big bombs, "not a super weapon", and that Taiwan's missile build-up makes an invasion less certain than often assumed.
- **Software and complexity.** In his view, software has not produced runaway productivity growth because digitising the world means capturing all of its complexity, with harder corner cases at every step. He links this to the case for vertically integrated firms.
- **Tesla versus Toyota.** Vernon says lean production at Toyota is too incremental, while Tesla optimises before building the plant, for example through gigacasting. He points out that enterprise value, not market cap, is the fairer comparison with heavily indebted carmakers.
- **Electrofuels and superabundance.** He says products such as ethylene or methanol from electrolysis become competitive at roughly $10-20 per megawatt hour, against about $25 for solar today. In the paper he wrote with Eli Dourado, cheap energy means electric trucks, air freight and small planes; rich countries might double their energy use, while use in Africa could rise about 10x.
- **Nuclear.** Vernon calls the NRC's move to probabilistic risk assessment a success, citing a rise in US reactor capacity factors from about 60% around 1980 to over 90%. He blames weak economics rather than regulation for the lack of new plants and prefers micro-reactors such as the 1-kilowatt KRUSTY project.
- **Finding alpha.** Drawing on Fama, he argues that ordinary investors earn excess returns through legally acquired private information, labour and brand, not by out-thinking public prices.
