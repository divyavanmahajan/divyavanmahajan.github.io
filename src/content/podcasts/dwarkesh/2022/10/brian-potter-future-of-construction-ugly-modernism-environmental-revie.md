## Thesis

Brian Potter, author of the Construction Physics blog and a former engineer at the startup Katerra, argues that construction stays slow and expensive because buildings are highly coupled, one-off projects with low margins, built from materials designed for human hands. Real productivity gains, he tells Dwarkesh Patel, need components redesigned for machines, not robots imitating workers. He also sees environmental review under NEPA as a source of cost and uncertainty with unclear environmental benefit.

## Key points

- **Why prefab struggles.** Potter says a single-family home is roughly 50% labour and 50% materials; a factory cuts labour but not materials, adds overhead and transport, so Katerra's economies-of-scale bet "just does not work out".
- **Design for machines.** Using the steam-to-electric-motor analogy, Potter favours assemblies like insulated metal panels that snap together. He is bullish on AR for site layout but sceptical of 3D printing, which only prints walls worth perhaps 7-8% of a building's value.
- **The Line and big projects.** Potter calls Saudi Arabia's Line project insane: a linear city maximises travel distance, and a cube would save material. He names China, late-20th-century Japan and the US from 1850 to 1970 as the great builders.
- **NEPA.** Environmental impact statements average about four and a half years; the law suggests 150 pages but the average is around 660. Potter argues NEPA is procedural, enforced through citizen lawsuits, and that timeline caps could backfire by inviting "hard look" litigation. He would give wind, solar and transmission the categorical exclusions oil and gas enjoy.
- **Ornament and glass.** In a second session Potter attributes plainer exteriors to air conditioning, float glass and a preference for open, light-filled interiors, plus labour costs. On the idea, raised by Patel via Scott Alexander, that architects impose their own taste, Potter sees some truth but says most must satisfy paying owners.
