## Thesis

Steve Hsu, a Michigan State theoretical physicist and cofounder of Genomic Prediction, tells Dwarkesh Patel that predicting traits from DNA has become a solvable machine learning problem once enough genomes are available. In his view the resulting predictors are simple and additive, and the business advantage lies in clinic relationships and data access rather than in the algorithms themselves.

## Key points

- **A data-scaling problem.** Hsu says his group used compressed sensing (L1-penalized regression) to predict in a 2012 paper that a few hundred thousand genomes would be needed to predict height, and that around 2017, with half a million genomes, the predicted "phase transition" appeared where they had said it would.
- **The IVF use case.** According to Hsu, Genomic Prediction genotypes embryo biopsies to flag outlier disease risks, working with 200 to 300 IVF clinics on six continents from a lab in New Jersey.
- **Moats and competition.** Hsu sees a strong first-mover advantage in clinic trust and channel relationships, but no lasting scientific moat. He concedes that a data-rich player such as 23andMe could build better predictors.
- **Moving to software.** He expects clinics to sequence on site and upload data to the cloud, which in his view makes the service hard to regulate by geography.
- **Data gaps.** Hsu says most training data is European, so prediction quality drops for other ancestry groups. He expects non-European biobanks to close the gap within about five years, and argues that controversy keeps cognitive measures out of most genomic datasets.
- **Talent and generalists.** Patel and Hsu discuss physicists moving into finance and tech. Hsu argues that physicists are trained to reconcile noisy data with models, and that people like Jeff Bezos stand out across several largely independent abilities rather than one.
