## Thesis

Edward Glaeser, chair of Harvard's economics department and author of Triumph of the City and Survival of the City, tells Dwarkesh Patel that cities endure because people learn and enjoy life through in-person contact, and that new communication technologies tend to complement it in the long run. In his view the bigger threats are self-made: local vetoes on housing, monopoly school systems, and policies that could deepen joblessness.

## Key points

- **Decline is sticky.** Glaeser says house prices show momentum over one year and mean reversion over five or more, while population decline persists for decades because housing stays put, as in Detroit after 1950.
- **Why people stopped moving.** He attributes falling mobility among poorer Americans to expensive housing in high-opportunity areas and to place-bound family support; a third of non-employed prime-age men live in their parents' homes.
- **Housing and local power.** Glaeser argues neighbours overweight the costs of new projects while would-be residents have no vote, and favours stronger landowner rights. He calls a land value tax sensible but not a panacea, and wants builders with somewhat more power than today, though not Robert Moses' methods.
- **Jobs over UBI.** Glaeser frames the opioid epidemic mainly as a supply-side story around OxyContin, and argues that a job provides purpose and social connection, so large US cash transfers could worsen joblessness. He supports modest cash transfers in the poorest countries.
- **Remote work.** Citing call-centre studies in which remote workers were as productive but 50% less likely to be promoted, he expects hybrid work for skilled staff, little impact from the metaverse, and pressure on high-tax states as firms find it easier to relocate.
- **Silicon Valley and the Sunbelt.** He traces the Valley from Federal Telegraph and Fred Terman to Fairchild, warns it now resembles a one-industry town like 1950s Detroit, and favours educated, pro-business cities such as Austin, Charlotte and Atlanta. His largest open question is protecting low-lying cities like Manila and Mumbai from climate change.
