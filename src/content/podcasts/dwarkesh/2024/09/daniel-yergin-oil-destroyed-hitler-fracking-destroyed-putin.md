## Thesis

Daniel Yergin, author of *The Prize*, argues that oil explains much of the 20th century, from Rockefeller's lighting monopoly to the two world wars and the 1973 crisis, and that supply and demand ultimately override the drama. He tells Dwarkesh Patel that US shale has become a geopolitical asset, and that the current energy transition differs from past ones, which were additions rather than replacements, so it will take longer than many expect.

## Key points

- **Oil as a strategic commodity.** Yergin says World War I established oil as strategic; in World War II Rommel ran out of fuel, Patton's 1944 advance was held back, and six out of seven barrels used by the Allies came from the United States.
- **The obsolescing bargain.** Citing his mentor Raymond Vernon, Yergin explains why producing countries kept renegotiating with Western companies; today a host country typically gets 80% of the profit.
- **Petrostates and sovereign wealth.** Yergin rates the UAE and Abu Dhabi as the best users of 1970s windfalls, with a fund probably worth a trillion dollars and over half of GDP no longer from oil.
- **Shale's geopolitical impact.** US output rose from 5 million barrels a day in 2008 to over 13.2 million; Yergin says LNG exports to Europe kept Putin from breaking the coalition backing Ukraine, and recalls Putin calling shale "barbaric" in 2013.
- **AI and electricity.** Yergin cites a projection that data centers could take 10% of US electricity by 2030, up from about 4%, after a decade of 0.35% annual growth.
- **A slower transition.** He notes the world used more coal last year than ever, and that copper supply would need to double by about 2035 while a new US mine takes 29 years.
