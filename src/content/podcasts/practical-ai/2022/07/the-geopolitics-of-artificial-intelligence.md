## Thesis

Chris Benson argues that AI is now a valuable national and corporate resource that shifts the balance of power through prestige, economics, academia and military capability. Daniel Whitenack adds that AI built from one actor's perspective can be irrelevant or harmful elsewhere. Both hosts conclude that strategy only pays off when it is tied to concrete local problems, and that practitioners work inside this larger context whether they notice it or not.

## Key points

- **Regulation lags deployment.** Whitenack asks whether rules keep up with AI deployments and suggests a wide gap. Benson calls GDPR a limited, ambiguous first step whose hoped-for follow-up has partly stalled.
- **Prestige and talent.** Benson says national AI strategies mix prestige, economic and military aims, and notes a shortage of AI professors in the United States and elsewhere that affects universities' pull. Whitenack sees similar talent effects at SIL.
- **Practical over generic strategy.** Benson observes that many national and corporate strategies look alike and points to the show's AI in Africa series as a model of research aimed at local needs. Whitenack notes that machine translation for all languages ignores languages with no written form.
- **Power beyond states.** Benson describes the war in Ukraine as a moment in which cheap, increasingly autonomous systems change conflict and investment. Whitenack points to IBM, Dell, Meta and Apple leaving Russia, and Elon Musk's Starlink, as signs that technology holders can rival nation states.
- **Autonomy and humans in the loop.** Benson expects autonomy to become pervasive and argues that keeping humans in the loop for non-human-centred work will eventually stop making sense; airliner pilots may remain mainly for reassurance.
- **Copilot debate.** Whitenack has moved from Vim to VS Code for GitHub Copilot but thinks programming will not be automated; Benson is less sure. Benson recommends ai.gov, the US National Artificial Intelligence Initiative, as a starting point.
