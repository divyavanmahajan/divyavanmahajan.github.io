## Thesis

Hamed Alemohammad and Abba Barde of the Radiant Earth Foundation, joined by Joyce Nabende of the Makerere AI Lab as guest co-host, argue that satellite data can track progress on the UN's 17 sustainable development goals, but only if it is turned into ML-ready benchmark datasets and local practitioners are trained to use it. Alemohammad describes Radiant Earth as a collaborative agency whose success is measured by what its users build.

## Key points

- **A real big data problem.** Alemohammad says government agencies (NASA, ESA, JAXA) and a growing commercial sector run the satellites, and their data is moving to the cloud, producing petabytes a day at global scale.
- **Standardising catalogues.** Because each provider had its own API and metadata, Radiant Earth helped convene the open SpatioTemporal Asset Catalog (STAC) spec, built by around 25 organisations and now adopted by major data providers.
- **Radiant MLHub.** Rather than raw imagery, the hub offers labelled training data and, more recently, a model registry. Barde says a few lines of Python load a dataset; anyone can also contribute data.
- **Crop mapping in practice.** Alemohammad cites governments in East Africa that lacked current cropland maps during the locust swarms, and a project with the Western Cape Department of Agriculture that became the "Spot the Crop" competition on Zindi, supported by GIZ's FAIR Forward programme.
- **GANs for scarce labels.** Barde describes modifying an MSG-GAN to generate labelled images for every satellite band, so 2,000 labelled images can be expanded, which he says improved their crop-type classifier.
- **Capacity building.** In May 2021 Radiant Earth, Makerere and FAIR Forward ran an ML for Earth observation bootcamp for about 40 participants. Nabende notes that startups as well as academics took part; the material has since become an online course.
