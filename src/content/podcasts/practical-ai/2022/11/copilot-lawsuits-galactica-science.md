## Thesis

Prompted by the FTX collapse, Daniel Whitenack asks what kind of controversy could set back the AI industry. He and Chris Benson conclude that the Copilot lawsuit and the Galactica backlash are not such events, but both show that law and governance lag behind the technology, and that how a model is released shapes whether people trust it.

## Key points

- **What a real setback looks like.** Benson puts unintentional loss of life caused by AI inference, such as an autonomous airliner crash, at the top of the scale; he expects it would cause pause and regulation rather than end AI.
- **The Copilot lawsuit.** Whitenack describes the 3 November class action against GitHub, Microsoft and OpenAI, which claims suggestions carry the fingerprints of original authors without the attribution open source licences require. He cites Tim Davis's tweet showing Copilot emitting his LGPL code.
- **Boilerplate or copying.** Benson asks whether code is structured enough that many programmers write near-identical functions. Whitenack notes that in the editor he cannot tell where a block came from, and points to Tabnine, trained on permissively licensed code, as one fix.
- **Real legal risk.** Citing Elaine Atwell's article "GitHub Copilot Isn't Worth the Risk", Whitenack judges that advocacy groups like the Software Freedom Conservancy would target large organisations, not small teams. He still uses Copilot; Benson expects years of litigation and market-based risk mitigation.
- **Galactica.** Meta AI's model was trained on 48 million papers, textbooks and other scientific sources. Whitenack reads out Janelle Shane's example of Galactica inventing giraffes on lunar missions; Benson calls it a misstep driven by the race to release.
- **Release strategy.** Whitenack contrasts OpenAI's controlled API, Stability's open release under the OpenRAIL licence, and overclaiming. From his translation work, he argues that asking users to help find a model's uses yields better feedback than promising it solves a problem.
