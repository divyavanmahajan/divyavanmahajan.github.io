## Thesis

Lukas Egger, head of the Innovation Office and Strategic Projects at SAP Business Process Intelligence, argues that innovation projects rarely fail on technology or people; they fail on organisational setup and incentives. In his view an innovation team should be a temporary, supporting function that helps product owners de-risk ideas and spreads a habit of change across the whole company, instead of a walled garden that owns innovation.

## Key points

- **Pioneers, settlers, town planners.** Egger borrows this model to separate people who are comfortable with failure from those who drive scale and constant improvement. Benson, who works in the defence industry, says his workplace uses "pioneers and builders" and asks where a rule-breaking pioneer is most useful.
- **A one-to-two-year tour.** Egger tells everyone joining his office to stay at most two years, then take a project they love into the organisation and champion it there.
- **Risks in parallel.** He argues engineers neglect viability and business models, and recommends working on desirability, viability and feasibility at the same time rather than in sequence.
- **A de-risking scorecard.** His team scores ideas on a four-by-four grid: four phases (identify, discover, explore, pre-build) against four risk categories (desirability, viability, feasibility, stakeholder management). The idea's owner and senior leaders pick which risk to reduce next from what he calls an "a la carte menu".
- **Outcome over output.** Drawing on his machine learning background, Egger warns against valuing a project just because it uses AI. The goal is fast, data-driven decisions; a model is only one possible output.
- **Delivering bad news.** He recommends agreeing quantifiable win and fail criteria up front and keeping feedback loops short, so a "failure" usually means a reset with more knowledge rather than a cancelled project. He also argues that most value comes from incremental change, not disruption.

## Quotes

> "I want people to change from pioneers to settlers. I don't want them to ever get stuck." — Lukas Egger
