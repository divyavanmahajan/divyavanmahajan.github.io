## Thesis

In a panel recorded at a digital olfaction summit hosted by Aryballe, Daniel Whitenack asks Mary Fischer-Mullins (Senior Director of Project Strategy, Cox Automotive) and Yanis Caritu (Chief Software Officer, Aryballe) how AI adoption has changed their organisations. Both argue that the hard parts are people, data quality and labelling, and that machine learning should be used with discernment rather than as a black box.

## Key points

- **Machine learning with discernment.** Caritu says Aryballe always knew it would use machine learning for its odour sensors, but built a team mixing data scientists with physics, chemistry and signal-processing experts, and now spends more time understanding and pre-processing data than on pipelines, which he calls commodities.
- **The pandemic as accelerator.** Fischer-Mullins describes how, in March 2020, Cox Automotive's business shifted to digital platforms faster than planned, and says AI helps build trust in a repeatable experience for clients.
- **Smell in used-car sales.** Cox touches three out of four cars on the road, she says, and odour sensing could convey things like smoke or industrial use when buyers can no longer stick their head in a car. Caritu cites an in-car study that detected malodour with 94% accuracy.
- **Keeping up with devices.** For imaging, Fischer-Mullins notes phones moved from the iPhone 8 to the iPhone 14, with LiDAR from the iPhone 12, so capture tools must support every tier. She sees human bias as the biggest challenge.
- **Explicit plus implicit models.** Caritu splits sensor behaviour into a part described by physical laws and a part that must be learned; correcting for humidity with hardware and explicit methods reduces what the model has to learn.
- **Labelling is the bottleneck.** He argues odour data cannot be labelled after the fact, unlike images, which slows the move to big data in digital olfaction.
