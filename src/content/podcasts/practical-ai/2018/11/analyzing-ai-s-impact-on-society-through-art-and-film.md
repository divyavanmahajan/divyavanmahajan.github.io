## Thesis

Brett Gaylor, a documentary filmmaker at Mozilla, argues that creative work helps lay people understand and feel how AI shapes their lives. He sees this as necessary before the public will back fixes for problems such as bias. Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson add that practitioners can help by releasing open tools and models.

## Key points

- **From remix to surveillance.** Gaylor made "Rip, a Remix Manifesto" and then the interactive series Do Not Track with the National Film Board of Canada, which won a Peabody. One episode used Michal Kosinski's Facebook-likes personality model, whose approach, Gaylor says, Cambridge Analytica later copied after being refused a licence.
- **Mozilla's view of AI.** Gaylor says Mozilla wants AI systems to be transparent, collect no more data than needed, have open code for bias review and be built with input from civil society.
- **Bias concerns.** Gaylor cites ProPublica's "Machine Bias" report on reoffending risk scores, saying it shows how such systems repeat past inequalities. Whitenack links this to hiring bias discussed with Lindsey Zuloaga.
- **Creative Media Award winners.** Gaylor describes Stealing Your Feelings (a film that reacts to emotions read through your webcam), A Week with Wanda (an over-eager AI assistant by email), Survival of the Best Fit (hiring bias), the Training Commission, What Do You See?, Mate Me or Eat Me (dating app bias) and Do Not Draw a Penis (algorithmic censorship).
- **Not magic.** Gaylor criticises coding ads that present programming as magic. Whitenack says workshop students are sometimes disappointed to learn ML is a set of well-defined functions.
- **Open source helps creators.** Gaylor says open libraries let artists innovate on content instead of building a full AI stack. Whitenack adds that creative uses can reveal implications practitioners missed.
