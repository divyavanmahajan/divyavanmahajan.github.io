## Thesis

Himani Agrawal, a machine learning engineer at AT&T, argues that people from science and engineering already have many data science skills. What they mostly lack is industry tooling and vocabulary. She says bootcamps and professional communities fill that gap, and that people from varied backgrounds bring creativity to a field short of expertise. Daniel Whitenack, who made a similar move from academia, agrees.

## Key points

- **Optimization as a thread.** Agrawal frames ML as optimization. She started by using genetic algorithms to find the best slope for dams in civil engineering, then did a PhD in computational biology on HIV proteins.
- **Finding industry.** Agrawal says the Grace Hopper Celebration (22,000 attendees in Houston, she says) and an internship at Microsoft Research showed her how her skills could apply across many domains.
- **Bootcamp bridge.** Agrawal calls the 12-week Galvanize data science immersive transformational. It came alongside an internship on early Parkinson's diagnosis and Jeremy Howard's fast.ai course. Whitenack says he used Thinkful.
- **Communities.** Agrawal recommends Women in Machine Learning and Data Science meetups, Women in Machine Learning workshops at NIPS and ICML, and MLConf San Francisco on November 14.
- **ML at AT&T.** In AT&T's Chief Data Office, Agrawal analyses streaming alarm data against network topology to find where outages start, avoiding visits to customers' homes. She also combines weather, flood and power data to time repairs after hurricanes. Benson, who worked in AT&T network engineering about 20 years earlier, says they had no ML then.
- **Titles and perception.** Agrawal says data scientist, ML engineer and AI engineer are effectively the same role. She adds that colleagues outside the field often see ML as magic.
