## Thesis

Recorded at NVIDIA GTC 2019, Chris Benson talks with Anima Anandkumar, director of machine learning research at NVIDIA and Bren professor at Caltech. Anandkumar describes a non-traditional route into AI driven by curiosity about maths, and argues that the next step for deep learning is combining existing knowledge, such as physics, with data rather than learning everything from scratch.

## Key points

- **Early start.** Anandkumar says her engineer mother, her father's manufacturing business and a maths-teacher grandfather made maths feel friendly; she first met programming through CNC machines at around eight. She studied electrical engineering at IIT Madras and did a PhD at Cornell under Lang Tong, with a final year at MIT with Alan Willsky.
- **Into AI via communications.** Her PhD on distributed statistical inference asked how sensors could send only relevant inferences instead of raw data, which led her to graphical models and latent variables.
- **Tensors.** At Microsoft Research, with Jennifer Chayes, Sham Kakade and Daniel Hsu, she began using tensors (multidimensional arrays) to extract topics from word co-occurrences. Her topic detection algorithm later shipped in AWS Comprehend, and she worked on the SageMaker launch.
- **Tensors in deep learning.** She argues networks should keep multidimensional structure instead of flattening to matrices, which gave high compression with good accuracy; she mentions an upcoming cuTENSOR library at NVIDIA.
- **NeurIPS renaming.** Anandkumar says the old NIPS name became a problem as the field grew, citing offensive t-shirts and a corporate party she describes as toxic, and explains why she spoke out. Benson backs a welcoming community.
- **Physics plus learning.** With Caltech aerospace professor Soon-Jo Chung, her team learned the ground effect for drone landing as a residual on an existing controller with Lyapunov stability guarantees, giving faster yet smooth landings.
