## Thesis

Samuel Taylor, a data scientist at Indeed speaking at All Things Open, argues that open source tools carry most practical machine learning work, and that side projects built with them are the best way to learn the field and show what you can do. He gives advice on choosing projects and vetting libraries.

## Key points

- **Full-stack data science at Indeed.** Taylor describes a model where one data scientist owns a model from idea, data and labels through training, deployment and monitoring. Use cases include salary estimation and job-to-seeker fit models. He moved into the role through an internal transfer after software and data engineering work.
- **Why AI started open.** Taylor's untested theory is that researchers from academia want to publish code alongside papers. Benson suggests Google open-sourced TensorFlow to capture mindshare; Taylor agrees, citing Google's experience of Hadoop rebuilding its MapReduce ideas outside the company.
- **Limits to openness.** Taylor says a Visa fraud model should not be released. Benson recounts the DoD CIO pushing back on a Government Accountability Office goal to open source 20% of government code because it is weapon-systems code.
- **Favourite tools.** Taylor names Jupyter and scikit-learn first, then Pandas, Matplotlib, Seaborn and Facebook's Prophet for time series. Whitenack asks how to vet GitHub code; Taylor suggests asking a trusted colleague, then checking stars and forks, and Whitenack adds commit history.
- **Side projects.** Taylor's examples are a model that emailed him the top 10 jobs each week, a Last.fm music recommender, a hackathon game teaching the American Sign Language alphabet, and a pun generator. He says good projects are tangible, useful to you, or scratch a personal itch.
- **Choosing between libraries.** Taylor recommends prototyping in both options and, where possible, testing both in production behind a feature flag.
