## Thesis

Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson, who say they are not experts in trade or international politics, look up four of the Chinese AI companies the US Commerce Department added to its Entity List. Whitenack concludes the companies do top-tier research but that questions about government involvement shadow their work; Benson argues the judgement turns on intent and that transparency about state ties would ease foreign concerns.

## Key points

- **Context.** Whitenack cites UN and other reports that about a million Uyghurs have been held in what China calls re-education camps, and a surge of papers on ethnicity recognition. He also mentions the US-China trade negotiations and the Hong Kong protests. The listing bars US companies from selling technology to at least eight AI-focused firms.
- **Hikvision.** Whitenack finds an English website focused on cameras, industrial machine vision and traffic management. Benson reads a Bloomberg article saying the company is controlled by the Chinese government and uses AI for people counting and large-scale facial recognition.
- **iFlytek.** Benson cites MIT Technology Review: a voice platform serving over 400,000 developers and a valuation of about $12 billion. Both hosts say its consumer and education focus comes across as less ominous.
- **Megvii and Yitu.** Whitenack reads Wired and TechCrunch coverage describing Megvii as a facial recognition unicorn valued at over $1 billion, backed by Alibaba, Ant Financial and the Bank of China. Yitu advertises face recognition benchmark wins plus cancer detection and Mandarin speech work; Benson reads a CNBC piece projecting a $9.6 billion facial recognition market by 2022.
- **Intent and transparency.** Benson contrasts US caution with the UK's willingness to keep trading with China, and compares Lockheed Martin's openly known government work with ties he describes as unclear in China.
- **Resource.** Whitenack recommends François Chollet's TensorFlow 2.0 and Keras overview Colab notebook.
