## Thesis

Chris Benson, who gave about 20 talks the previous year, and Daniel Whitenack map the kinds of AI events and argue that their main value is targeted access to experts and long-term relationships rather than sitting through every talk. They recommend a gradual path from local meetups to conference and keynote talks, and share how to write abstracts and present.

## Key points

- **Four event types.** Whitenack sorts events into grassroots meetups, large industry conferences (O'Reilly AI, Strata, QCon, Spark Summit, MLConf), smaller tool- or topic-focused events (TensorFlow Dev Summit, spaCy IRL, AllenNLP Summit) and research conferences (NeurIPS, EMNLP, ICLR, CVPR). Benson warns large conferences can be expensive and commercial; Whitenack suggests a focused event may beat a $2,000-3,000 broad one.
- **Meetups as a first step.** Benson founded the Atlanta Deep Learning Meetup and spoke at its first session himself. Whitenack says about half of meetups are engaged, so try more than one.
- **Why attend.** Benson plans conference time around problems experts can help with and values friendships, such as with Mat Ryer. Whitenack recalls using conferences to ask about TensorFlow on Docker with GPUs, and to meet open source collaborators and his distributed team.
- **Getting involved.** Both recommend volunteering, which can earn a pass for those without budget. Whitenack advises planning submissions far ahead and asking contacts to review abstracts.
- **Abstracts.** Benson relays advice from O'Reilly's Ben Lorica: describe how you solve the problem, not just what it is. Whitenack says to skip tired clichés about data being the new oil and lead with specifics.
- **Delivery.** Whitenack urges practising out loud, assuming little audience knowledge and cutting slide text, then checking slides zoomed out. Benson separates visuals for speaking from visuals for study and warns against rambling.
