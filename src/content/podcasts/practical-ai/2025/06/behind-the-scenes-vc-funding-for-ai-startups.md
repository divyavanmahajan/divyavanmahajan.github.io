## Thesis

Daniel Whitenack, CEO of Prediction Guard, walks Chris Benson through the mechanics of venture funding and his own experience raising a seed round. His argument is that VC money makes sense only for products with very large markets and a need to scale fast. In today's crowded AI market, companies that have a product but not yet proven fit face the toughest questions about competition and differentiation.

## Key points

- **Who should raise VC.** Whitenack separates service firms (he notes consultancies like Accenture are making a lot of money from AI), niche "lifestyle" products and products aimed at markets worth tens or hundreds of billions of dollars. Only the last group fits venture capital.
- **How funds and valuations work.** In his example, a $50,000,000 fund spread across 30 companies relies on a power law: one big exit returns the whole fund, three more return it again, and the rest a third time. A headline such as Perplexity raising 500,000,000 at a 14,000,000,000 valuation describes the price per share, not the cash invested. New share pools dilute existing holders.
- **Why Prediction Guard raised.** The company bootstrapped for its first year to year and a half. Whitenack expects AI consolidation within three to five years and decided that taking part required capital to scale fast.
- **Stuck in the middle.** Investors back great teams before they have a product, or proven sales engines at Series A. A seed-stage company with traction sits in between, where investors cannot tell offerings like LangFlow and LangChain apart.
- **The founder's time.** Five to seven VC pitches a day took time away from sales in a fully technical company. Whitenack reframes delegating work he enjoys as giving others a chance to grow, and wants Prediction Guard to set a more trustworthy standard than exploitative players, citing the reported Builder.ai collapse.
