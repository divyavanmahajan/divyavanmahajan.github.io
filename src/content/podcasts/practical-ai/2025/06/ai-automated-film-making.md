## Thesis

Sami Arpa, CEO and co-founder of Largo AI, tells Daniel Whitenack that while text-to-video gets the attention, AI pays off earliest in development and pre-production, where most projects die. Largo analyses scripts, proposes casts and forecasts revenue. Arpa insists the tool should only work "forward" on human-made content and never generate stories in reverse, so that it adds to creativity rather than making films look alike.

## Key points

- **Adoption is rising.** Arpa says Netflix and Amazon were early adopters through recommendations and micro-genres. By his measure, industry use of AI has risen from 2% when Largo started to around 30% today, despite two Hollywood strikes.
- **Where projects fail.** Most money goes into production and distribution, but development can take six or seven years. About 90% of films are eliminated before pre-production, so cheap early analysis helps newcomers and small producers compete with studios.
- **Films as structured data.** Out of his EPFL PhD in computational aesthetics, Arpa built "genre recipes" that map a film over time in a nine-dimensional genre space. This gives models a compact representation to learn from.
- **Two kinds of models.** Shallow models predict finances from past data, with about 80% accuracy; Arpa says audience shifts such as heat waves limit how much further they can go. Simulated focus groups use digital twins of real people with roughly a one-year lifetime and can catch outliers. Streaming forecasts are built by correlating social noise with household viewership.
- **Budget matters most.** In Largo's models, budget has the strongest influence on financial results. Arpa expects AI to cut production costs sharply, leading to more films, more competition and a "frequency game" for filmmakers.
