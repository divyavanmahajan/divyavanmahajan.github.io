## Thesis

Kyle Daigle, COO of GitHub, argues that code assistants succeeded because they fit how developers already work, and that the frontier has moved from completing lines to turning a described problem into working software. As AI writes more of the code, he says, communication and problem description become the central developer skills, and the next gains will come outside the editor: review, CI, operations and eventually ambient, privacy-respecting assistance.

## Key points

- **Fit the workflow.** Daigle says the best AI tools help people do work they already do, while tools that make humans contort to get value struggle for traction. He urges sceptics to keep experimenting, likening resistance to his own early dismissal of JavaScript as a Rubyist.
- **New muscles.** Daigle names two skills: writing down your personal rules and prompt instructions for a project, and describing problems clearly in writing, which he sees as what "prompt engineering" really is.
- **Learning to code.** Responding to Whitenack's mention of a Y Combinator cohort with 95% AI-generated code, Daigle compares vibe coding to changing a light switch versus being an electrician, and sees an untapped opportunity in AI that fixes production errors, writes a test and deploys to canary.
- **Connected context.** He hopes MCP lets one tool combine context from error trackers, databases and cloud services, rather than each vendor's AI seeing only its own slice.
- **Copilot's direction.** Daigle describes model choice, chat, agent mode in VS Code Insiders, Workspace and Spark, and assigning issues to Copilot as you would a teammate, aiming at the other 80% of dev time beyond the editor. A public-code matching filter addresses licensing concerns and is required on some SKUs.
- **Ambient AI.** His aspiration is AI that finishes his thought using his email, calendar and other data without 55 integrations, with privacy as the biggest gap rather than LLMs.
