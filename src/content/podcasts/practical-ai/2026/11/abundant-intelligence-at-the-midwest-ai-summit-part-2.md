## Thesis

Three talks from the Midwest AI Summit, introduced by Daniel Whitenack, look at how organisations decide what AI should do. Don Barger argues for agreeing boundaries before building, Mark Lay frames AI adoption as four management decisions, and Darren Taillie shows two small agent deployments that removed real work.

## Key points

- **Charter before code.** Barger, director of innovation and AI at the International Mission Board, says eight people spent a couple of days writing an AI charter almost four years ago. It rules out AI posing as a human, sole AI decisions about people, covert monitoring, high-risk autonomous actions and "soul care". He used it to turn down a request for an AI counsellor chatbot.
- **Faithbot.** Barger's faith-grounded chatbot at faithbot.tools has about 2,500,000 users without paid advertising and runs in 51 languages, each with its own LLM. He removed its ability to pray, and it routes users in crisis towards people and crisis services.
- **Delegate like a manager.** Lay, CIO at Noblis, weighs each AI task by reward and by the risk and consequence of error, then decides "how much of a leash" to use. His team spent a year cataloguing over a thousand datasets, narrowing them to about 115-120 critical ones with about 30 data stewards.
- **A new kind of technical debt.** Lay warns about apps built by departments, vendor upgrades that break those apps, the cost of reversing course and whether teams have the capacity to keep evolving what they deploy.
- **Text-to-SQL in Teams.** Taillie, senior data analytics manager at AeroCore Technologies, which washes jet engines, describes a two-person analytics team. They built an AgentForge agent with MCP servers, put it in Microsoft Teams, and let leaders query work orders themselves.
- **Vision verification.** Every morning at 08:15, a vision model checks crews' photos against the tail numbers entered for work orders and flags mismatches for a human. Taillie says it worked well enough that the monthly reconciliation meeting was cancelled because there were no incorrect work orders.
