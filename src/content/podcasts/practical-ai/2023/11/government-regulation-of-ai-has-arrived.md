## Thesis

Chris Benson and Daniel Whitenack review the White House executive order on safe, secure and trustworthy AI from October 30, 2023, and the Bletchley Declaration that followed. Benson says the order has the effect of law and was more detailed and expert-informed than he expected. Both hosts point out that it assigns tasks and deadlines without saying how they will be done or paid for.

## Key points

- **Order vs. declaration.** Benson explains that an executive order acts as law unless Congress or the Supreme Court overrides it. He describes the Bletchley Declaration from the AI Safety Summit on November 1-2 as short, non-binding and worth supporting.
- **Sharing safety tests.** Developers of the most powerful models must share red-teaming and safety test results with the government under the Defense Production Act. Benson expects the Department of Commerce to receive most of these reports, though no system for this exists yet.
- **Compute thresholds.** Section 4.2 sets a 90-day timeline and a threshold of 10 to the 26 operations, lowered to 10 to the 23rd for biological sequence models. Whitenack asks where fine-tuning ends and building a new model begins.
- **Gaming the line.** Using a Kentucky dry-county analogy, Whitenack predicts that teams will train models just under the threshold. He thinks this could push the field toward doing more with less. Benson notes the irony that the extra regulatory work favours large players, even though the order talks about equity.
- **Standards and labelling.** NIST is asked to set standards for red-teaming. The order also calls for labelling and detecting AI-generated content, which Whitenack says remains an open research problem, especially for text that humans and models edit together.
- **What comes next.** Benson expects many agency actions over the next year, a lot of litigation, and new compliance businesses. Whitenack hopes the work produces tools that help developers build safe systems.
