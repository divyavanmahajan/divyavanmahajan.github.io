## Thesis

Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson use Kyutai's Moshi release to argue that small, open research labs can still beat the largest players and that open models drive experimentation. They then read generative AI's drop on the Gartner hype cycle as a healthy correction: a model alone solves nothing, and value comes from engineering it into larger software systems.

## Key points

- **Kyutai beat OpenAI to market.** Whitenack notes that the non-profit lab Kyutai, which he believes is around eight people backed by roughly a thousand GPUs, released its real-time multimodal voice assistant Moshi before OpenAI shipped the GPT-4o voice mode it had demoed.
- **Small enough to run locally.** Whitenack says the models are sized to run on a single GPU and will be open sourced; Benson points to the press release's claim that Moshi can run on an unconnected device, which suits IP-sensitive enterprises, edge devices and defence.
- **Deja vu with OpenAI.** Whitenack compares Kyutai to OpenAI's early open, non-profit stance, which later closed up. Benson argues open source survives where companies share common foundations below their point of differentiation, citing Hugging Face hosting a million models.
- **Into the trough.** Benson reports that generative AI, after a long time at Gartner's peak of inflated expectations, is now heading into the trough of disillusionment while large companies keep spending heavily on it.
- **A return to engineering.** Whitenack sees the disappointment as being about integration, not capability: LLMs still live in software stacks that need testing, versioning and hosting. He notes Gartner's "composite AI", which reaches the plateau sooner, sounds much like classic data science.
- **Efficiency or new markets.** Asked whether AI is just efficiency gains rather than a new market like the web, Benson calls the two different but related, and Whitenack expects hard-to-predict new things, as Amazon was hard to predict early on.
