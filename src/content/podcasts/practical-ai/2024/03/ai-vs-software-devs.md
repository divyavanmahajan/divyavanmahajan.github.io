## Thesis

With the regular hosts away, producer Jerod Santo stitches together segments from three Changelog shows about AI and software developers. Kevin Ball argues that tools like Cognition Labs' Devin are useful in skilled hands but are marketed far beyond what they deliver. The Go Time panel treats code generation as a productivity tool rather than a replacement, and José Valim worries about how a small language like Elixir stays visible to closed models.

## Key points

- **Devin's numbers.** Ball notes Devin resolves 13.86% of issues, about one in seven, and asks how a team would know which one succeeded. He calls giving a hallucination-prone LLM raw access to spin up cloud resources something he would not sign up for.
- **Demos versus production.** Santo compares the launch to cherry-picked Midjourney samples and says human-in-the-loop review is the necessary step. He argues that prompt engineering is a leaky abstraction that products will hide.
- **Jobs and economics.** Ball expects short-term disruption if engineers become three to five times more productive, but argues cheaper software makes new businesses viable, as jQuery and later tooling did for JavaScript.
- **Solutions keep changing.** Johnny Boursiquot says engineering means handling exceptions and changing requirements, not producing static code. Kent Quirk calls LLMs fundamentally non-creative and describes Honeycomb using field names from a customer's data to turn plain-language requests into queries.
- **Tooling as table stakes.** Quirk compares Copilot to power tools in woodworking, and Boursiquot warns that companies banning AI coding tools will lose engineers.
- **Elixir and closed models.** Valim says Elixir's first-class documentation makes it ready for retrieval-augmented generation, but its community knowledge sits in the Elixir Forum rather than Stack Overflow. He uses GPT-4 in Livebook because it gives the best results, while fearing a closed ecosystem, as with Chrome and GitHub.
