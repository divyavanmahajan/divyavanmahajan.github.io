## Thesis

Mike Lewis, chief AI architect at Synthony in Cincinnati, argues that today's language models already deliver enterprise value, and that disappointment usually comes from people who have only tried a chatbot. In his view the value lies upstream: carefully curated context, task-specific personas built into code, and teaching non-technical staff how the models actually work.

## Key points

- **Astronomers and astronauts.** Lewis contrasts commentators with builders, saying he has built about 150 tools. His unusual path runs from industrial design and a NASA internship, through 20 years and roughly 2,000 commissioned portraits, to early DALL-E beta access and the OpenAI API.
- **Solution archetypes.** His team narrowed 35 proven solution patterns to 15, such as generating new ideas and preserving know-how. His first app, built on GPT-3, was "Andy", a focus-group facilitator persona that spawns about eight other personas. Lewis says it produced the best idea in the seven-year history of a publisher's product team: a pocket-sized card deck with QR codes.
- **Think upstream.** Using Kroger's grid-based warehouse robots as an example, Lewis argues that solutions rarely look like a robot copying a human, so organisations should curate context before automating steps.
- **Proofs of concept in hours.** To get past privacy concerns he starts with synthetic data, for example an invented drug for a pharma client. He uses a bot called PersonaCraft to write proof-of-concept scripts during meetings.
- **Training champions.** Lewis's bootcamp teaches tokens and context windows with a "peanut butter and..." exercise, and only "layer one" knowledge. His five-step method: dictate the end goal (transcribed locally with MacWhisper), validate it, curate assets, validate again, then work and synthesise outputs.
- **Advocacy and access.** He imagines low-cost agents acting for marginalised people. He also says knowledge jobs as they exist today will go, and that the transition needs empathy.
