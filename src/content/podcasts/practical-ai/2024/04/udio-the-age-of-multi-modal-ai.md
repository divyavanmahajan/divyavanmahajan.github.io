## Thesis

Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson treat Udio, a beta music generator that produces music, lyrics and sung vocals from a text prompt, as evidence for their prediction that 2024 is the year of multimodal AI. Whitenack then explains how the field moved from separate speech, vision and NLP models chained together to foundation models that process several modes jointly, using LLaVA as the worked example.

## Key points

- **Songs from a prompt.** Whitenack plays "Dune, the Broadway musical" by Udio user Bobby B, prompted with styles such as "teen pop show tunes", and his own pop rock song about two podcast hosts, generated in about 30 seconds with auto-written lyrics and title.
- **Who owns a prompt.** Whitenack, stressing he is not a lawyer, understands that machine-generated work is not copyrightable in the US today, but compares careful prompting and lyric editing to photography, which was eventually accepted as art.
- **Inevitability.** Benson expects the music industry to respond with lobbying and lawsuits, as actors struck over AI video, and advises musicians to get on board quickly. He also pitches an opt-in service that reads your emotions and generates a personal soundtrack.
- **From chains to joint models.** Whitenack describes the old split into computer vision, speech technology and NLP specialisms, versus GPT vision and Gemini, which accept images or whole videos with a text prompt.
- **How LLaVA works.** LLaVA pairs OpenAI's CLIP vision encoder with a language model such as Llama and trains a projection matrix that maps image embeddings into the language model's space; either part can be swapped, for example for Gemma or Intel's BridgeTower.
- **Human-like perception.** Benson links the trend to Ed Yong's book An Immense World, arguing that human learning is multimodal by nature.
