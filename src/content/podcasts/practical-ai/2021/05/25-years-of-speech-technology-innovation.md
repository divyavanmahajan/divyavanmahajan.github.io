## Thesis

Jeff Adams, who has worked in speech technology since 1995 at Kurzweil Applied Intelligence, Dragon, Nuance, Yap and Amazon before founding Cobalt Speech and Language, argues that speech will take a central but not exclusive role in a multimodal mix of interfaces. He sees access to suitable labelled data as the main barrier, especially for smaller languages.

## Key points

- **N-grams still work.** Adams explains statistical language modeling as predicting the next word, with the "zero problem" of unseen sequences at its core. Corpora have grown from about 50 million words to billions, yet he says n-grams remain the workhorse because they are fast.
- **Language versus acoustic models.** At Dragon and Nuance, Adams ran the language modeling group, which competed with the acoustic team over accuracy gains in each release. At Amazon, he says, Alexa forced acoustic models to handle far-field speech with echoes and reverberation.
- **Unexpected applications.** Adams lists detecting depression, Alzheimer's and congestive heart failure from speech, language-learning feedback, pilot fatigue monitoring, and VocaliD's synthetic voices for people who will lose their voice to surgery.
- **Multimodal, not speech-only.** He argues some companies pushed the pendulum too far toward speech and expects a balance with keyboards, touch and gestures. Speech can also assist human-to-human conversations, such as prompting a bank representative.
- **Data and small languages.** Adams says a language with 5 million speakers can still be ignored by large players for lack of labelled data.
- **Cambodian oral histories.** Cobalt took on a pro bono project with a BYU group that had about 4,000 hours of Khmer recordings, roughly a thousand transcribed, to build a speech recognizer. His lesson: involve speech experts before collecting data.

## Quotes

> "it is still really hard to beat Ngram technology for modeling statistical language models." — Jeff Adams
