## Thesis

Daniel Whitenack and Chris Benson apply Barry Johnson's polarity mapping framework to a concrete case, machine translation of COVID information into local languages, to ask how an organisation should balance human and artificial intelligence. Benson's recurring argument is that strategy must start from a validated need rather than a technology, and that the best outcomes come from teaming the strengths of both sides.

## Key points

- **Need before approach.** Benson, a strategist at Lockheed Martin, says most technology conversations start with an exciting solution in search of a problem. He uses the simple NABC framework (Need, Approach, Benefit, Competition) because complex strategy methods do not stick.
- **What humans bring.** Benson points to empathy and the fact that the end user is a human; Whitenack adds trust and adaptability, noting public corpora contain little COVID-domain text, so a translation model may not know the domain while a human adapts quickly.
- **What AI brings.** Once development cost is absorbed, Benson says a model scales to many users without the limit of a small pool of translators. Whitenack frames AI's fit in terms of scale (say, four million sentences) and complexity (such as IoT sensor anomalies), and notes human-plus-model teams often beat either alone; Benson calls this manned-unmanned teaming (MUM-T).
- **Bias on both sides.** Benson says humans carry the same biases as data. Whitenack relays a coworker's point that human biases are "covered by shame" and hidden, whereas model errors can be counted on a test set.
- **Trust and warning signs.** Benson, a new private pilot, trusts AI for procedural tasks like flying but not for complex, nonlinear situations. Both see forcing a favoured approach onto a problem, or outputs that intended users do not adopt, as signs of imbalance.
- **Resources.** Whitenack recommends investing in your editor and Drew's Vimcasts course; Benson recommends the NABC value proposition framework.
