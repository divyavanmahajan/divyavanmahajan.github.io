## Thesis

Chandler McCann, general manager of DataRobot's federal practice, argues that nonprofits get lasting value from AI when they are treated like regular customers and taught to own and maintain their own models, not handed a one-off hackathon app. His water-point project with the Global Water Challenge is his example: a clean, standardised dataset plus simple predictive models changed how one government budgets for water.

## Key points

- **The problem.** McCann says roughly one in four people rely on non-traditional water sources such as hand pumps and wells, and about 25% of the water points in the data are broken at any time. Brian Banks of the Global Water Challenge built the first standardised database of these points to find out why.
- **The data.** Records include location, photos, water source, technology, installer, installation year, whether the community pays, and whether a management structure exists. Data comes from national assessments (such as in Swaziland and Sierra Leone) and from local NGOs.
- **Cleaning first.** About 1,600 free-text values for technology type reduced to 12-14 real categories. The matching was done by hand at first and is now being automated with Paxata. Image classification is being explored to fill missing fields.
- **What predicts failure.** McCann says the age of the water point was the strongest predictor across countries, followed by who installed it and the region, since remote areas may have poor access to parts.
- **Policy impact.** After trips to Sierra Leone and Liberia in 2019, McCann reports that Sierra Leone's Ministry of Water used the models' insights in budgeting and passed a national policy requiring data in water-service decisions.
- **AI for Good programme.** The programme drew applications from 10 countries on five continents. Partners include Kiva (predicting unfunded loans), DonorsChoose, Anacostia Riverkeeper (E. coli forecasts), UCSF and University Hospital Mannheim. McCann stresses that models go stale and need refreshing.
