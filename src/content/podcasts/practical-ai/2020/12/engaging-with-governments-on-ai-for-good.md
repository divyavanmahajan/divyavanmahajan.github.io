## Thesis

This is a panel Daniel Whitenack moderated at the R|Gov conference, with Danya Murali, a quantitative analyst at Arcadia Power, and Emily Martinez, interoperability unit chief at the NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene. Both argue that government data is a strong base for equitable data work, provided practitioners handle demographic data deliberately and build trust with the communities affected.

## Key points

- **What "for good" means.** Murali defines it as using data and AI in ways that are equitable across all communities, citing police facial recognition as a worrying case and COVID tracing as a positive one. Martinez says that from local government and public health she has always been doing data for good.
- **Public-private work on COVID.** Martinez describes contracting vendors for a new point-of-dispensing system for pop-up vaccination clinics, replacing tracking that had been done on paper.
- **Government data in energy.** Murali, formerly at the Energy Information Administration, says Arcadia combines EIA power plant fuel and emissions surveys with customer usage to estimate each customer's CO2 footprint and the CO2 avoided by wind and solar.
- **Where to find data.** The panel points to NYC and New York State open data portals (aggregated, with patient data kept internal), EIA.gov, the EPA's calculator and Kaggle. Whitenack adds Mozilla Common Voice and the OPUS parallel corpus for language work.
- **Race-conscious analysis.** Murali argues that zip codes can proxy for race and income, so analysts should examine those relationships at every stage rather than ignore them; Arcadia has a diversity, equity and inclusion chapter and checks customer demographics for over-indexing.
- **Trust and open tools.** Martinez says the city already knows where disparities lie and relies on community ties. Both see growing use of R, open source and community forums; Martinez notes the health department moving from SAS to an R server.

## Quotes
