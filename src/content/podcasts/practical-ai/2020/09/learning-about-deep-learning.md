## Thesis

Will Ramey, Global Head of Developer Programs at NVIDIA, describes how the company moved from chips to an AI platform through small, measured experiments, and how the NVIDIA Deep Learning Institute (DLI) grew the same way. He argues that hands-on training should follow proven practice rather than the research frontier, and that AI education now has to serve practitioners, non-specialists and education itself.

## Key points

- **From gaming to science to AI.** Ramey recalls that after CUDA launched in 2009, scientists asked why they should use gaming technology for real science; deep learning researchers later found GPUs almost ideally suited to training neural networks.
- **Cloud lit the fuse.** He credits open-source frameworks (Caffe, Torch, TensorFlow, MXNet, PaddlePaddle) and pre-packaged GPU instances from cloud providers with letting startups experiment without building data centres.
- **DLI's origin.** Ramey once flew around the world with pallets of gamer laptops to teach CUDA; AWS GPU instances let the training move to the cloud. When internal experts added Jupyter notebooks on deep learning, sign-ups eclipsed everything before. DLI has trained over 250,000 people.
- **Choosing curriculum.** Ramey says training should cover what is already proven: a year earlier recommender systems were moving too fast for a course, but by 2020 best practices were settled enough to build one.
- **GTC goes online.** Over 60,000 people joined the first virtual GTC; the October 2020 edition offered 600+ sessions and 16 DLI workshops across time zones. On the $40 billion Arm acquisition, Ramey defers to Jensen Huang.
- **Three education priorities.** Ramey names training practitioners to analyse data and test model outputs, giving school-age children a basic grasp of how AI works, and applying AI to adapt learning to individual learning styles. He is optimistic, citing COVID-19 simulation work in healthcare.
