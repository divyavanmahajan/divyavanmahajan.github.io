## Thesis

Astro Teller traces X's attempt to connect the 2.6 billion people without reliable internet. Loon solved the technical problem of steering balloons in the stratosphere but was wound down because the economics did not work for telecom partners. Its balloon-to-balloon laser links became Taara, which Mahesh Krishnaswamy and Strive Masiyiwa present as a cheaper way to carry high-speed data over rivers, cities and the last mile.

## Key points

- **Sailing the stratosphere.** Nick Kohli explains that balloons at 60,000 ft and above were steered by pumping air in and out to change altitude and catch winds blowing in different directions. Sal Candido says early demos missed by 1,000 km until the team built an AI model, which taught itself to tack like a sailboat.
- **Proof in a disaster.** After Hurricane Maria, Kohli says five balloons over Puerto Rico, working with AT&T, gave hundreds of thousands of subscribers a signal.
- **Why Loon ended.** Kohli says the cost of delivering a service that operators could resell, plus rising bandwidth demand, meant the complexity for partners stayed too high.
- **Light instead of radio.** Krishnaswamy describes Taara's traffic-light-sized terminals, which use moving mirrors to lock a laser beam between rooftops, and accelerometers to compensate when monkeys shake a tower in India.
- **Crossing the Congo.** Masiyiwa challenged Taara to link Kinshasa and Brazzaville across a river too deep and fast for cable; he says setup took under half a day and Brazzaville's data costs, previously five times higher, dropped.
- **Users as resellers.** In Nairobi's Pipeline area, residents resold $10 connections for $300-400, so Taara built an app, Taara Share, to formalise it. Krishnaswamy's next step is an optical phased array chip already linking at 25 Gbps.
