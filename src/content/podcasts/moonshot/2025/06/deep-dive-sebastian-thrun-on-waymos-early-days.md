## Thesis

Sebastian Thrun, co-founder of X, tells Astro Teller that he first thought a self-driving car was impossible, but Larry Page talked him into trying. He credits the early success of the project (then called Chauffeur, now Waymo) and of X to written, non-negotiable milestones, small trusted teams and freedom from management overhead. He closes with a strongly optimistic view of technology and AI.

## Key points

- **From Street View to Chauffeur.** Thrun says a Stanford student project to photograph San Francisco became Street View after Google bought the rights in 2007, before the planned spinout with Teller was incorporated. Page later proposed a car that could drive anywhere in California.
- **Milestones as contracts.** Inspired by the fixed deadline of the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, Thrun set the "Larry 1000": 1,000 miles of the hardest California roads, chosen by Page and Sergey Brin, driven fully hands-off. He says the value was clarity for the team.
- **Exponential safety progress.** Early cars managed about 10 feet before something went wrong, Thrun recalls, but the distance between critical takeovers grew roughly tenfold every year and a half.
- **How X was run.** Modelled on Lockheed's Skunk Works: no finance staff, a ban on PowerPoint updates, secrecy, few hires, and a no-publication rule. Thrun says manager Bill Coughran taught him that management means removing roadblocks.
- **Failures and drones.** Thrun recounts a failed in-ear magnet idea; Teller notes it was revived as Wolverine and spun out as Iyo. Thrun says Wing came from Page's unit-economics spreadsheets and predicts that within 100 years all transport will be airborne.
- **Technology and jobs.** Thrun argues that everything that can be made will be made, so the job is to prevent misuse. He cites farming going from feeding four people to 400 per farmer as evidence that new technology creates more jobs than it destroys.
