## Thesis

In a live SXSW panel moderated by Nicholas Thompson, Astro Teller argues that moonshots create value through learning, so the speed of learning loops sets efficiency and failure must lose its stigma. Adam Savage and Catie Cuan add that understanding is physical, that art and engineering share one exploratory process, and that ideas come from play and from teams.

## Key points

- **Out of reach.** Teller names a space elevator, which would need material hundreds of times stronger than steel for its weight, such as miles of carbon nanotubes. He says X watches trends it does not control, such as turning landfill waste into value, and that Waymo depended on AI progress that was faster than predicted.
- **Failure and learning.** Teller says nobody learns when they are right, and that stopping a project is cheap because false negatives cost nothing when problems and ideas are plentiful. Savage describes rebuilding a MythBusters penny-drop segment around a tube showing a penny's two terminal velocities.
- **Robots and motion.** Cuan expects billions of robots and calls that a choreographic problem; she cites Stanford research on which car decelerations read as friendly. At Everyday Robots she dropped nine months of reinforcement-learning work on robot flocking for an approach that took a month or two.
- **Moonshot compost.** Teller describes ocean CO2 sensors an X team brought from about $30,000 to roughly a dollar, still on the shelf, and explains why X has artists in residence. Savage calls his skills "arrows in a quiver".
- **Structure and risk.** Teller says X moved from "fire and forget" to people sharing time across projects, and uses the monkey-and-pedestal joke: tackle the riskiest part first.
- **Longevity and biology.** Cuan thinks people may live to 130 and wants caretaking robots for ageing populations. Teller predicts programmable biology will matter as much this century as computers did in the last, while worrying about how longevity is being chased.
