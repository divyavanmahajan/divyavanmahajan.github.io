## Thesis

Catie Cuan, former artist in residence at Everyday Robots, tells Astro Teller that robots differ from computers mainly because they move, so the experts in emotionally meaningful motion, choreographers, belong in robot design. She argues that the perception of safety matters as much as safety itself, and that people today are choosing whether future robots will be beautiful or alienating.

## Key points

- **Motion as social cue.** Cuan contrasts a robot that approaches very close before stopping with a person who adjusts course early in a hallway, and a robot that grabs a cup without first looking at it. Unpredictable motion, she says, reads as creepy.
- **Music mode.** Everyday Robots mapped each robot joint to an instrument sound (bells for the gripper, cello for the torso). Cuan says a study found people rated robots playing it as more animate, likable, intelligent and safe, and valued that the sound reflected real motion.
- **Learning to flock.** Six months of reinforcement learning looked fine in simulation but poor on real robots. Following a suggestion from Benjie Holson, the team started from the Boids flocking algorithm and used imitation learning to learn its weights and Cuan's choreographic preferences. Teller says that riding with the flock felt like being included rather than in control.
- **Design and expectations.** The robot was under five feet tall, one-armed and wheeled. Cuan, citing Rodney Brooks, argues that humanoid looks raise expectations and lower tolerance for mistakes.
- **Fear and jobs.** Cuan says most people know robots only from fiction. She cites a Wall Street Journal figure that 57% of today's jobs did not exist in the 1960s, and points to new roles such as robot veterinarians at Everyday Robots.
- **Legacy and future.** She says the field saw machine learning in robotics as a dead end until the 2022 SayCan work on Everyday Robots hardware. She expects robots to help ageing people such as her 75-year-old father live independently, and to take on dangerous work.

## Quotes

> "The main difference between a robot and a computer is that a robot moves. The people who are experts at creating compelling, emotionally affective, contextually relevant motion are choreographers." — Catie Cuan

> "It's the narrative, not the actual number of jobs that are changing, but it's the story that we tell that is so scary for people about that." — Catie Cuan
