## Thesis

Astro Teller presents two X projects aimed at community resilience. Sarah Russell's Bellwether argues that the hard part of forecasting natural disasters is organising thousands of incompatible geospatial data formats, and that once solved, it unlocks fire planning, insurance pricing and damage assessment. Jacques Gagne's 280 Earth argues that direct air capture can become a business by running cheaply on waste heat and selling water and cooling alongside carbon removal.

## Key points

- **The data is the "monkey".** Russell says geospatial data has no spreadsheet equivalent, only about 8,000 formats; Alphabet's retained archive (Earth Engine) gave Bellwether historical data to train on. The team started with wildfire.
- **Firefighter input.** Former Forest Service fire director Robert Baird supplied overlays, shape files and fuels-work data and corrected model output against local knowledge. Bellwether is now piloted in the US to plan burn lines and fuel work, and can rerun scenarios such as a controlled burn.
- **Absolute risk for insurers.** Russell describes per-home scores: probability of fire in 12 months and over five years, probability of burning at least 50%, plus the top five factors raising and lowering risk, instead of the industry's relative low/medium/severe bands.
- **Disaster imagery.** For the National Guard, Bellwether works out what aerial photos are pointing at, given only camera GPS; it processed 70,000 images after Hurricane Helene.
- **Moving the sponge.** Gagne's team moves a silica-based sorbent through separate air and heating stations instead of heating a box, desorbing at around 70°C. Each ton of CO2 also captures two tons of water, and he claims ten tons of data-centre evaporation can be saved.
- **Spinning all the way out.** Helen Riley explains that factory-scale capital fell outside Alphabet's profile, so 280 Earth became independent with Alphabet as a minority investor. Its 500 ton-per-year prototype is to be followed by a 5,000 ton module in Dallas, Oregon.
