## Thesis

Jason Rugolo, who founded iyO after working on the idea at X, argues that large language models make natural-language computing practical, and that its natural home is a device worn in the ear. First, though, the device has to "do no harm" to hearing and give people enough reason to wear it all day. Astro Teller and Rugolo describe machine-mediated hearing as a new layer of software between the world and your ears.

## Key points

- **Fast idea churn at X.** Rugolo says he worked through 30-something ideas, including "nuclear humans" and Matrix-style digital living, before the "Attention is all you need" paper led him to audio. He notes that X's earlier Siren project, around 2011, had the same idea six or seven years too early.
- **Why wearables are hard.** Rugolo argues that people treat glasses and watches as self-expression, and that badly balanced hardware causes pressure points within about two hours. iyO chose not to hide its device but to make it valuable enough to wear openly, like eyeglasses.
- **Machine-mediated sound.** Outward-facing microphones feed an in-ear computer that runs apps. The first prototype had 16 microphones per ear and took six months to build; after that, the "super hearing" app took two weeks. Rugolo says it outperformed hearing aids.
- **Latency limits.** In Teller's "holy" moment, a knob faded between two people talking at the same time. Rugolo explains that sound changes must happen within about 30 milliseconds, so this processing runs on the device. Translation (the "Babl" app) can tolerate cloud round trips.
- **Privacy in both directions.** Rugolo says nobody likes speakerphone: an in-ear-canal microphone keeps your input private, and whispered output keeps answers private.
- **Launch and agents.** iyO announced the company with a TED Talk and is pre-selling a first device in Wi-Fi and LTE versions. An agent studio in beta lets developers choose from around 20 language models; Rugolo describes "Einstein", a physics tutor, and "Deege", a Spotify DJ.
