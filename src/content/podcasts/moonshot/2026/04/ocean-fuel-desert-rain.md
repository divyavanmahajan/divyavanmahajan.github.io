## Thesis

Astro Teller presents H2E and Foghorn as X in microcosm. Each team built a working prototype, wrote down cost-based kill criteria early, found it could not reach that cost, wound the project down and published what it had learned. Neil Treat, Claudia Truesdell and Kathy Hannun argue that cost was the deciding factor in both cases: it decided whether the device could help people on $2 to $8 a day, and whether a commodity fuel could compete.

## Key points

- **The water problem.** Treat cites 2.2 billion water-stressed people, some spending up to 50% of their income on drinking water. H2E's dataset, published in Nature, found more than a billion of them live where sunlight and humidity are ample. It has about 300 citations.
- **How the box worked.** Truesdell describes two airstreams passing through a rotating desiccant wheel (silica gel in fiberglass, from an HVAC rotor maker), heated by a solar thermal top. Treat says the collector put out about 1,200 watts, partly by absorbing downwelling infrared.
- **Kill criteria and the gap.** The target was $0.01 per liter at down to 30% humidity. Treat says they reached line of sight to about $0.10, an order of magnitude off. He thinks a new material could close the gap.
- **Foghorn's idea.** Hannun explains that acidifying seawater releases CO2. Combined with hydrogen from electrolysis, it makes methanol that works with existing engines. She argues it is easier to reinvent the fuel than every car, plane and boat.
- **Why it was killed.** Hannun set a $5-per-gallon cost target. Fragile membranes and the energy needed to pump seawater pushed the team toward partnering with desalination plants, including a trip to the UAE, but no path to $5 appeared. She cites X's high opportunity cost.
- **What came after.** Hannun says the work was published in the International Journal of Greenhouse Gas Control and as a Harvard Business School case. Matt Eisaman later co-founded Ebb Carbon with the same technology, and Hannun went on to start Dandelion.
