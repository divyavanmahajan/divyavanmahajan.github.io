## Thesis

Astro Teller talks with two X engineers about making and storing energy in unusual ways. Fort Felker explains why Makani, a tethered kite that generated wind power, worked technically but was closed in 2020: it could not be radically cheaper than conventional turbines. Raj Apte describes Malta, a heat-pump "battery" that he judged worth building only because it could beat where lithium prices would be in the future.

## Key points

- **Why a kite.** Felker says turbines with towers and blades as long as a football field and weights of hundreds of tons cannot reach islands or deep water offshore. Makani's carbon-fibre kite flew loops a kilometre or more across and sent power down a 5-kilovolt tether.
- **Planned failure and hedged bets.** Teller asked Felker to crash at least five aircraft early. Felker had the team develop two tether designs in parallel; the solid-rod tether broke on the first flight and the wound one worked.
- **Why Makani ended.** Felker says detailed cost models showed only a marginal saving, while conventional wind became much cheaper between 2006 and 2020. His advice is to start hard cost modelling as soon as the product is defined well enough. The autonomous flight controller was published.
- **Malta's physics.** Apte describes Nobel laureate Bob Laughlin's idea: a reversible heat engine that stores heat in molten salt (up to 600°C) and cold in antifreeze or alcohol. Round-trip efficiency is about one half to two thirds, against Apte's rough 70-80% for pumped hydro, but it needs no valley.
- **A kill date.** Apte's techno-economic model showed that lithium would become cheap enough around 2040 to make the turbomachinery uneconomic, so the team had to move immediately. Malta is now a separate company preparing a first-of-a-kind site in Spain.
- **Systems engineering.** Teller says X does its best work combining known pieces in surprising ways rather than proving new science. Apte argues that large grid shares beyond 20-30% renewables need large-scale storage.
