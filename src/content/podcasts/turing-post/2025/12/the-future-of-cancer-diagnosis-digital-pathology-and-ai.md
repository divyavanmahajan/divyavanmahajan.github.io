## Thesis

Akash Parvatikar, who leads the PathologyMap platform at HistoWiz, argues that cancer diagnostics is still moving from glass slides under a microscope to digital images, and that AI will arrive in pieces as an assistant to pathologists, not a replacement. In conversation with Ksenia Se, recorded for Breast Cancer Awareness Month, he names data quality and explainability as the main barriers to clinical adoption.

## Key points

- **Two parallel roads.** Parvatikar says digitization and AI tool-building are running side by side and should converge within a couple of years. Digital pathology only began in 2017, when the US FDA approved scanners for primary diagnosis, and COVID sped it up.
- **Whole-slide images are huge.** One slide image runs from a few hundred megabytes to several gigabytes, at 40x magnification, so models work on tiles that are processed and stitched back together: a "needle in a haystack" problem.
- **The data bottleneck.** He calls the lack of high-quality annotated data the biggest problem: pathologists disagree, especially on pre-invasive breast lesions, so a model trained on NYU labels may fail on Stanford's.
- **Agents in pieces.** Agentic workflows are far off for diagnosis but plausible for steps like slide quality control, which HistoWiz is exploring. PathologyMap also lets users run AI apps from several vendors on the same slide.
- **Timeline and economics.** Mayo Clinic has digitized 12 million slides. He expects more FDA approvals for diagnostic assistants in two to three years, cheaper storage than keeping physical slides for about 25 years, and digital sign-outs of perhaps 50 cases instead of 30, which would help with the US shortage of pathologists. He cites Tempus as an example of multimodal precision medicine.
- **Hopes and fears.** Telepathology and fast second opinions excite him. What worries him is heat-map "explainability" from developers who lack domain knowledge, and he recommends the WHO Classification of Tumours ("Blue Book") to newcomers.
