## Overview

May on Practical AI split between voice assistants and machine learning tooling. Two episodes involved Amazon Alexa, one on smart home predictions and one on a long career in speech technology. The other two looked at tools: a compiler that makes models run fast on varied hardware, and a new machine learning stack for the Elixir language.

## Main themes

### Alexa and the predictive smart home

- Evan Welbourne describes a hunch as “an algorithmically derived intuition that Alexa has”: a prediction with a confidence score, surfaced only when the model is highly confident ([Hunches](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/generating-hunches-using-smart-home-data/)).
- Feedback is explicit or implicit, such as a customer turning a lowered thermostat back up. Welbourne argues the end customer is the only one who can label the context of their home ([Hunches](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/generating-hunches-using-smart-home-data/)).
- More than 140,000 device types connect to Alexa, and homes produce sparse data. Automatic actions came only after early, rarer hunches had earned trust, with customers consenting in advance ([Hunches](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/generating-hunches-using-smart-home-data/)).
- Jeff Adams, a former Alexa lead, says Alexa was the first breakthrough product to handle speech at a distance, with echoes and reverberation in a room ([Speech technology](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/25-years-of-speech-technology-innovation/)).

### Speech technology and small languages

- Adams says n-gram language models remain the workhorse because they are fast, even as corpora have grown from about 50 million words to billions ([Speech technology](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/25-years-of-speech-technology-innovation/)).
- He expects speech to take a central but not exclusive role, balanced with keyboards, touch and gestures ([Speech technology](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/25-years-of-speech-technology-innovation/)).
- Adams says a language with 5 million speakers can still be ignored by large players for lack of labelled data. Cobalt took on a pro bono Khmer speech recognition project with a BYU group ([Speech technology](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/25-years-of-speech-technology-innovation/)).
- His advice to communities collecting language data: “reach out first and let's get involved from the beginning rather than have to fix things after the fact” ([Speech technology](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/25-years-of-speech-technology-innovation/)).

### Compiling models for any hardware

- Luis Ceze says TVM grew from the idea of a common intermediate representation across the cross product of models, frameworks and hardware targets. It was deployed at Amazon, Facebook and Microsoft before OctoML was formed ([Apache TVM](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/apache-tvm-and-octoml/)).
- There are billions of ways to compile one model for one target, so TVM uses learned models of hardware behaviour to pick fast options. Its optimisations leave accuracy unchanged, with quantization optional ([Apache TVM](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/apache-tvm-and-octoml/)).
- Ceze says ARM built its compilers on TVM and NVIDIA contributes commits. He adds that compilation cannot get around physics: a model that does not fit in memory will not run ([Apache TVM](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/apache-tvm-and-octoml/)).

### Machine learning in Elixir

- José Valim explains that JAX's immutable arrays convinced him Elixir could do numerical computing. Nx adds tensors and numerical definitions compiled for the GPU ([Elixir](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/elixir-meets-machine-learning/)).
- Sean Moriarity's Axon builds Keras-style networks from plain functions, and Daniel Whitenack says its API felt familiar to a Python user ([Elixir](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/elixir-meets-machine-learning/)).
- Livebook targets common notebook complaints with explicit dependencies, sequential evaluation and a Markdown-based format that works with version control ([Elixir](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/05/elixir-meets-machine-learning/)).
