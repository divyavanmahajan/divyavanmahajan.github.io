## Overview

September had two episodes, and hardware ran through both: AI chips, Intel's push into accelerators and running models at the edge. A panel of Intel AI innovators argued that the field's hard problem had moved from model accuracy to production. A news episode added AI in medicine and synthetic dance video.

## Main themes

### Chips and edge hardware

- Benson cites a VentureBeat article arguing AI is reversing the commoditisation of chips, so “we're seeing lots of chip startups instead of just software startups”, such as Graphcore and Cerebras, with ASICs as a likely future ([Hardware acceleration](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/ai-in-healthcare-synthesizing-dance-moves-hardware-acceleration/)).
- Whitenack points to Intel's Movidius sticks for running neural networks at low power on a Raspberry Pi or drone. Benson says Intel has come back hard from CPUs into AI chips ([Hardware acceleration](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/ai-in-healthcare-synthesizing-dance-moves-hardware-acceleration/)).
- David Ojika describes FPGAs as “essentially like GPUs for acceleration”, focused on inference, and credits Intel with making them usable for data scientists. A Netrolux panellist argues connected devices must decide on-device because networks cannot carry all their data ([Quora questions](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/answering-recent-ai-questions-from-quora/)).

### From accuracy to production

- Asked what comes after 97% accuracy, Ojika says the next phase is productionising models at the edge and on IoT devices. Vinay Rao counters that sub-problems such as driving at night may still have much lower accuracy ([Quora questions](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/answering-recent-ai-questions-from-quora/)).
- The panel says beginners need neither a GPU nor a maths PhD to start: Peter Ma learned with pre-trained models, and Ali points to boards and sticks under $70. Ali warns that people will hit limits without maths ([Quora questions](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/answering-recent-ai-questions-from-quora/)).
- Rao says the goal should be “to get to a prototype really fast”, which needs a multidisciplinary team. He describes AutoML as machine learning on machine learning and says classical methods still matter ([Quora questions](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/answering-recent-ai-questions-from-quora/)).

### New applications and synthetic media

- Benson highlights Johns Hopkins work using deep learning on scans to catch a hard-to-treat cancer earlier, which he files under the show's AI-for-good theme. Whitenack mentions reinforcement learning for designing new molecules ([AI in healthcare](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/ai-in-healthcare-synthesizing-dance-moves-hardware-acceleration/)).
- Whitenack shares Berkeley work that transfers one dancer's moves onto other people. Benson's six-year-old daughter did not realise the video was generated, and he expects generated motion to become indistinguishable from real life ([Dance synthesis](/podcasts/practical-ai/2018/09/ai-in-healthcare-synthesizing-dance-moves-hardware-acceleration/)).
