## Overview

November on Practical AI was about the tooling around machine learning: labeling data, structuring knowledge and building apps for ML teams. Three guest episodes covered Label Studio, Grakn and Streamlit. In between, the hosts unpacked OpenAI's Rubik's Cube robot hand and the backlash over how it was announced.

## Main themes

### Labels decide model quality

- Michael Malyuk of Heartex describes labeling as creating metadata for every item in a data set. He says model quality is in most cases tied directly to how accurately the data is labeled ([Data labeling](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/open-source-data-labeling-tools/)).
- He names time and quality as the two major challenges, plus bias between annotators. Quality checks include training a model on a verified fraction and comparing consensus between several annotators ([Data labeling](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/open-source-data-labeling-tools/)).
- Malyuk calls Label Studio “the first open source data labeling tool that you can not only download and run”, since teams can also embed it in their pipelines. Interfaces take 5 to 25 lines of HTML-like config ([Data labeling](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/open-source-data-labeling-tools/)).
- Heartex's commercial offering adds pre-trained models, team collaboration and quality control on top of the open source tool ([Data labeling](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/open-source-data-labeling-tools/)).

### Simulation, robot hands and hype

- The hosts explain that OpenAI's hand pairs a vision network and a reinforcement learning control policy with the classic Kociemba solver for the moves themselves ([Robot hands](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/robot-hands-solving-rubik-s-cubes/)).
- The policy was trained entirely in simulation with automatic domain randomization. OpenAI “randomized it in increasingly difficult ways”, for example by changing the simulated cube's size ([Robot hands](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/robot-hands-solving-rubik-s-cubes/)).
- Chris Benson calls the headline a marketing mistake and says marketing must not overreach the science. Daniel Whitenack notes that scrutiny of research claims now happens publicly on Twitter rather than in private peer review ([Robot hands](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/robot-hands-solving-rubik-s-cubes/)).

### Knowledge graphs and reasoning

- James Fletcher of Grakn Labs describes the goal as moving from a graph full of data to a graph full of knowledge. Grakn adds a typed schema of entities, relations and attributes on top of a graph database ([Knowledge graphs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/intelligent-systems-and-knowledge-graphs/)).
- Fletcher calls automated reasoning “the artificial intelligence of the 80s”. He favours rules where they can be written, because they generalise perfectly and are explainable ([Knowledge graphs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/intelligent-systems-and-knowledge-graphs/)).
- He sees machine learning over graphs as the intuition that complements reasoning, and describes moving from random walks to GraphSAGE and DeepMind's Graph Nets ([Knowledge graphs](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/intelligent-systems-and-knowledge-graphs/)).

### Apps for ML teams

- Adrien Treuille says every ML team he has seen ends up creating apps, drawing on his time at Google X and Zoox ([Streamlit](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/build-custom-ml-tools-with-streamlit/)).
- He argues that relying on Flask or a separate app team slows down the iteration cycle. Streamlit turns an ordinary Python script into an interactive web app ([Streamlit](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/build-custom-ml-tools-with-streamlit/)).
- The library stays free and open source, while a paid Streamlit for Teams will add deployment, authentication and logging ([Streamlit](/podcasts/practical-ai/2019/11/build-custom-ml-tools-with-streamlit/)).
