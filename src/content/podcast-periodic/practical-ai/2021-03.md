## Overview

March on Practical AI was about the costs and the discipline behind machine learning. One episode questioned the field's habit of buying accuracy with compute, another made the case for testing ideas on live systems, and a third looked at who gets to work in data science at all. A fourth episode covered a startup applying deep learning to drug discovery.

## Main themes

### Green AI: efficiency as a research goal

- Roy Schwartz and Jesse Dodge argue that small accuracy gains from ever-larger compute budgets carry two costs: carbon emissions and a research community where only a well-funded few can compete ([Green AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/green-ai/)).
- Schwartz says the cost of inference is probably the most influential source of emissions, because cheap single calls run at enormous scale. Dodge cites an NVIDIA claim that about 90% of machine learning cloud cost goes to inference ([Green AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/green-ai/)).
- Dodge says “most papers just don't report anything” on efficiency. He asks for FLOPs, training curves or hyperparameter search results so others can compare at lower budgets ([Green AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/green-ai/)).
- To change incentives, the pair helped set up green or efficient NLP tracks at conferences. Schwartz calls released pre-trained models such as BERT a green success, and Dodge is watching adapters as a cheaper alternative to full fine-tuning ([Green AI](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/green-ai/)).

### Testing ideas on live systems

- David Sweet, author of Tuning Up, says quant traders and recommender-system engineers share one workflow: an idea, an offline check, then an online test. He calls the online step the hardest but the most accurate ([Recommender systems and trading](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/recommender-systems-and-high-frequency-trading/)).
- Sweet says A/B testing “is the most robust, reliable, and believable way to do things”. Response surface methodology, multi-armed bandits and Bayesian optimization build on it, with Bayesian optimization automating the choice of what to test next ([Recommender systems and trading](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/recommender-systems-and-high-frequency-trading/)).
- On trading, he says financial data has a low signal-to-noise ratio and competitors trade signals away. Experienced quants estimate only about one tested idea in ten works ([Recommender systems and trading](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/recommender-systems-and-high-frequency-trading/)).

### Women and diversity in data science

- Margot Gerritsen says WiDS began in 2015 after a Stanford data science conference had only male speakers. It now runs over 200 conferences a year and has over 600 ambassadors ([WiDS](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/women-in-data-science-wids/)).
- She disputes the view that a growing pipeline will fix things. Below roughly 25-30% representation, she says, a group stays seen as different and change does not happen organically ([WiDS](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/women-in-data-science-wids/)).
- Gerritsen calls “like hires like” the deepest problem in hiring and suggests tracking attrition as well as hiring. She argues the majority, plus parents and teachers, must drive the change ([WiDS](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/women-in-data-science-wids/)).

### Deep learning as a bet in drug discovery

- Atomwise CEO Abe Heifets argues that pharma still runs most experiments physically while other engineering fields simulate first, and that AI can shift that balance ([Drug discovery](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/deep-learning-technology-for-drug-discovery/)).
- He warns that the field has rewarded overfitting to benchmarks. In his view only prospective tests, across many targets and partner labs, show whether a model works ([Drug discovery](/podcasts/practical-ai/2021/03/deep-learning-technology-for-drug-discovery/)).
