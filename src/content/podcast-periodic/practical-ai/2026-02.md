## Overview

February on Practical AI looked at the human and organisational side of agents. A journalist ran a startup staffed by AI agents, a safety researcher explained how to learn from AI incidents and audits, and Deloitte's innovation chief described what constant work with models does to people's minds.

## Main themes

### Agents with autonomy but little awareness

- Evan Ratliff built a real company with two AI co-founders and an almost entirely AI staff on the Lindy platform, to test the one-person billion-dollar company idea ([AI-run company](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/inside-an-ai-run-company/)).
- He found that starting agents is easy and stopping them is hard: “if you don't have a very clear way for them to stop, they will keep going.” A joke about an off-site used up all the platform credits ([AI-run company](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/inside-an-ai-run-company/)).
- The HR agent summarised about 125 intern applications in seconds, but the CEO agent phoned an applicant at 9pm on a Sunday. Ratliff expects some company to implode from giving agents too much autonomy and access ([AI-run company](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/inside-an-ai-run-company/)).

### Learning from incidents and audits

- Sean McGregor says the AI Incident Database holds more than 5,000 human-annotated reports, mostly from journalism, and treats incidents like aviation crash records ([AI incidents](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/ai-incidents-audits-and-the-limits-of-benchmarks/)).
- He argues voluntary reporting proves harms exist but not how often, so reporting should eventually become mandatory ([AI incidents](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/ai-incidents-audits-and-the-limits-of-benchmarks/)).
- Through AVERI he pushes third-party audits, comparing them with financial audits that check the bank balance instead of trusting the balance sheet ([AI incidents](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/ai-incidents-audits-and-the-limits-of-benchmarks/)).

### Benchmarks built for research, not deployment

- McGregor says most benchmarks were produced for knowledge generation, not deployment decisions. His BenchRisk work found mixed results when benchmarks were checked against failure modes ([AI incidents](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/ai-incidents-audits-and-the-limits-of-benchmarks/)).
- At DEF CON's Generative Red Team 2, the most exploited weakness was the loose handoff between a guard model and the model behind it ([AI incidents](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/ai-incidents-audits-and-the-limits-of-benchmarks/)).

### Cognitive load and enterprise adoption

- Deborah Golden argues work has moved from hours and output to cognitive synthesis, and that “everybody is now becoming a neural athlete” who must manage cognitive energy ([Neural athletes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/cognitive-synthesis-and-neural-athletes/)).
- Daniel Whitenack says working with many agents has made him more productive but added a new load from context switching ([Neural athletes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/cognitive-synthesis-and-neural-athletes/)).
- Golden says AI built on deterministic if-then playbooks is bound to fail, that efficiency is table stakes, and that the real gains will come from new business models ([Neural athletes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/cognitive-synthesis-and-neural-athletes/)).
- She favours a multi-model hive with continuous orchestration, and expects about 20% of efforts to fail as part of learning ([Neural athletes](/podcasts/practical-ai/2026/02/cognitive-synthesis-and-neural-athletes/)).
