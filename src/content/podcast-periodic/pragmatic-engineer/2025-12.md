## Overview

December had two long conversations with Gergely Orosz. Michelle Lim, Warp's first engineer and now founder of Flint, explained how to choose and succeed at an early AI startup. Bryan Cantrill of Oxide traced servers from Sun's dotcom years through AWS and Kubernetes to a rack you can buy and own.

## Main themes

### Being a founding engineer

- Lim counts a founding engineer as one of roughly the first five who join in the company's first few months. She moved from Facebook to Slack to Robinhood and says each smaller company gave her more ownership ([Founding engineer](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/being-a-founding-engineer-at-an-ai-startup/)).
- She chose the most equity of three salary options at Warp and asked Zach Lloyd for references from engineers he had managed. As a founder, her key question to referees is “would you want to work with this person again?” ([Founding engineer](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/being-a-founding-engineer-at-an-ai-startup/)).
- Lim prefers a product-first versus code-first split to front-end versus back-end, and warns that many startups use product engineer to mean front-end only. She advises volunteering for unwanted but business-critical work, as she did with Warp's Hacker News presence and later enterprise sales ([Founding engineer](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/being-a-founding-engineer-at-an-ai-startup/)).
- At Flint she is building websites that act on their own, for example generating a competitor comparison page overnight. She compares this to autonomous vehicles, with perception, decisions and control in one closed loop ([Founding engineer](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/being-a-founding-engineer-at-an-ai-startup/)).

### Booms, busts and the cloud

- Cantrill says Sun did more interesting technical work in the bust than in the boom: ZFS, DTrace and the Service Management Facility all came from 2001-2005. He argues that innovation needs a degree of desperation that good times make hard to find ([Oxide](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/the-history-of-servers-the-cloud-and-whats-next-with-oxide/)).
- His lesson from the dotcom era is that a boom “will go on longer than you think possible” and then collapses faster than expected ([Oxide](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/the-history-of-servers-the-cloud-and-whats-next-with-oxide/)).
- From running Joyent, he describes 2010 to about 2014 as a period of relentless AWS execution with a price cut at every re:Invent, and claims S3 margins helped fund Amazon's war on retail. He thinks Kubernetes took off because customers felt locked into AWS and wanted cloud neutrality ([Oxide](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/the-history-of-servers-the-cloud-and-whats-next-with-oxide/)).

### Owning the cloud, and where LLMs help

- Oxide's founding belief in 2019 was that elastic cloud computing is the future, but that companies should be able to buy and run it in their own data centre, not only rent it. The whole stack is open source, from the Hubris OS to the Omicron control plane ([Oxide](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/the-history-of-servers-the-cloud-and-whats-next-with-oxide/)).
- The rack was designed from a clean sheet: a DC bus bar, 32 compute sleds, blind-mated networking and Oxide's own switch. Cantrill calls blind-mated networking an early bet-the-company decision ([Oxide](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/the-history-of-servers-the-cloud-and-whats-next-with-oxide/)).
- Staff use Claude Code for polishing, tests and reading documents, and Cantrill says a writing-heavy culture makes a team ready to consume documents with LLMs. He says the models gave almost no help with hardware bring-up, such as a weeks-long CPU reset bug ([Oxide](/podcasts/pragmatic-engineer/2025/12/the-history-of-servers-the-cloud-and-whats-next-with-oxide/)).
