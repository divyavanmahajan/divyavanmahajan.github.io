## Overview

March on Turing Post opened with four open-weight episodes released on the same day: MiniMax, two parts with Reflection AI's Ioannis Antonoglou, and Nathan Lambert. Ksenia Se then spent two episodes with OpenAI's Michael Bolin on Codex and the engineer's role, and closed the month in a self-driving car with NVIDIA's Ali Kani.

## Main themes

### The case for open-weight frontier models

- Antonoglou argues open weights speed up science, make models safer through outside scrutiny and counter the concentration of power in closed labs. He says Chinese labs have shown it can be a business ([Reflection, part 1](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/why-reflection-ai-bets-their-business-on-open-weights-ioannis-antonogl/)).
- He says open models have lagged for lack of a commercial engine, not because openness slows them. Reflection moved from coding agents to a general model trained from scratch, funded by a Series B of more than $2 billion ([Reflection, part 2](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/what-reflection-ai-offers-to-beat-closed-labs/)).
- Lambert expects open models to stay six to nine months behind closed ones, but says the US should fund them as the engine of research. He says “the best open models from China have probably exceeded my expectations in how legitimately good they are.” ([Lambert](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/why-the-us-need-open-models-nathan-lambert-on-what-matters-in-the-ai-a/)).
- Olive Song says MiniMax researchers wanted to open-source because of the community, and that open weights let users fine-tune and keep their data on their own infrastructure ([MiniMax](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/inside-minimax-how-they-build-open-models/)).

### Reinforcement learning as an engineering problem

- Song says progress comes from engineering discipline and first-principles debugging. She recalls an RL run that stalled until the team found a precision problem in the LM head ([MiniMax](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/inside-minimax-how-they-build-open-models/)).
- Agentic RL, in her view, needs hard, diverse goals, scaled and varied environments and strong RL infrastructure. The weakest point of open models is adapting to unfamiliar tools and environments ([MiniMax](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/inside-minimax-how-they-build-open-models/)).
- Antonoglou names long-horizon credit assignment as the key research question. He sees AGI as an agent that can do on a computer what a human can, reached through better engineering rather than breakthroughs ([Reflection, part 2](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/what-reflection-ai-offers-to-beat-closed-labs/)).

### Codex and harness engineering

- Bolin says “The model is going to dominate the experience, for sure. But we’ve found there’s still a lot of room for innovation in the harness.” ([Codex](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/openai-s-michael-bolin-on-codex-harness-engineering-and-the-real-futur/)).
- His team keeps the harness small with few tools and relies on OS-level sandboxing instead of constant human check-ins. Codex usage is up about five times since the start of the year ([Codex](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/openai-s-michael-bolin-on-codex-harness-engineering-and-the-real-futur/)).
- He says the biggest change is throughput through parallel work. Code review volume has gone up, but Codex does much of that review itself ([Codex](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/openai-s-michael-bolin-on-codex-harness-engineering-and-the-real-futur/)).

### What engineers are still for

- Bolin expects agents to write most code. He argues the human contribution is taste, judgement and asking the right questions ([Engineers](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/openai-s-michael-bolin-what-engineers-still-matter-for-in-the-age-of-c/)).
- His main worry is people giving up taste too early and ending up with bloated projects. Harnesses may later run their own periodic architecture reviews ([Engineers](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/openai-s-michael-bolin-what-engineers-still-matter-for-in-the-age-of-c/)).

### An open stack for self-driving

- Kani says NVIDIA's Level 2 setup costs under $1,000: “You could put this computer and the sensors less than $1,000, so it's not expensive to put this in every car.” ([NVIDIA](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/inside-nvidia-s-plan-to-bring-self-driving-to-every-car-ali-kani-expla/)).
- An end-to-end model, Alpamayo, plans trajectories while a classical stack, Halos, enforces road rules. NVIDIA open-sources its software and tools and plans Level 4 trials next year ([NVIDIA](/podcasts/turing-post/2026/03/inside-nvidia-s-plan-to-bring-self-driving-to-every-car-ali-kani-expla/)).
