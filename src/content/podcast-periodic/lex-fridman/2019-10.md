## Overview

October 2019 had three episodes, two of them looking back at landmark IBM systems. Garry Kasparov revisited his matches against Deep Blue and David Ferrucci explained how Watson won Jeopardy, and both used AlphaZero as a reference point. Michio Kaku looked far ahead to alien contact, digitised minds and a theory of everything.

## Main themes

### Machines in closed games, humans in open ones

- Kasparov argues machines will always beat humans in closed systems such as chess, Go and video games. They win not by solving the game but by making fewer mistakes ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).
- He calls AlphaZero, trained on about 60 million games, a first step towards machine-generated knowledge. He adds that machines cannot tell which questions are relevant and would need hundreds of thousands of games to fix exposed weaknesses ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).
- Kasparov says that by 1998 he saw “it's no longer human versus machines it's about human working with machines”. He warns the greatest danger is humans interfering with machines' superior knowledge ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).
- On Deep Blue, he notes the 1997 loss was the first match he ever lost, that he won in 1996, and that he believes the 1997 match was not fairly organised ([Kasparov](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/garry-kasparov-chess-deep-blue-ai-and-putin/)).

### Watson, prediction and explanation

- Ferrucci describes Watson as a self-contained system with its corpus in memory on roughly 2,000 to 3,000 cores. It generated thousands of candidate answers and combined hundreds of scorers with machine learning ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).
- A component was added only if it improved end-to-end accuracy, which let teams work independently. IBM turned the challenge down twice before Ferrucci delivered it in four years ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).
- Ferrucci separates pattern-based prediction from reasoning that can be justified. He might not need driverless cars to explain themselves if they are “ten times safer than humans”, but expects people to demand explanations for sentencing, parole and treatment ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).
- His company, Elemental Cognition, combines neural pattern learning with shared frameworks. He guesses general AI is closer to 20 years away than 10 ([Ferrucci](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/david-ferrucci-ibm-watson-jeopardy-deep-conversations-with-ai/)).

### Kaku's long view

- Michio Kaku argues that with billions of potential Earth-size planets, alien life is near-certain, and predicts we will pick up signals this century. He expects aliens to be peaceful and mostly ignore us ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).
- He points to recorded mouse memories and planned memory chips as steps towards a brain net, and describes digitised personalities beamed by laser to avatars on the Moon or Mars ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).
- Kaku presents string theory as the only surviving candidate for a theory of everything, while saying it could still be wrong. He argues Einstein failed at unification because he lacked a physical picture to guide him ([Kaku](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2019/10/michio-kaku-future-of-humans-aliens-space-travel-physics/)).
