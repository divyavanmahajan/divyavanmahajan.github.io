## Overview

May 2020 mixed machine learning insiders with guests from security, finance, robotics and neuroscience. Ilya Sutskever looked back at AlexNet and forward to AGI, Dawn Song showed how easily models are fooled, and Stephen Schwarzman framed AI as a race the US must organise for. Sertac Karaman and Karl Friston rounded out the month.

## Main themes

### Deep learning and the road to AGI

- Ilya Sutskever says the ideas behind deep learning existed long before it worked; what was missing was large datasets, enough compute and conviction. Alex Krizhevsky's fast CUDA kernels made the ImageNet result possible ([Ilya Sutskever](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/ilya-sutskever-deep-learning/)).
- He argues the field keeps underestimating neural networks and that AGI will likely be deep learning plus a few more ideas, with self-play among them ([Ilya Sutskever](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/ilya-sutskever-deep-learning/)).
- On control, Sutskever pictures “humanity are like the board the board members of a company where the agi is the ceo”, with a reset button. He thinks a body is useful for AGI but not necessary ([Ilya Sutskever](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/ilya-sutskever-deep-learning/)).

### Machine learning under attack

- Dawn Song describes perturbed stop signs that fool classifiers across viewing distances and angles, and poisoned face-recognition data that acts as a backdoor ([Dawn Song](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/dawn-song-adversarial-machine-learning-and-computer-security/)).
- She says attackers currently have the upper hand, and that her group's attack on an imitation translation model transferred to Google Translate ([Dawn Song](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/dawn-song-adversarial-machine-learning-and-computer-security/)).
- Her proposed defences are consistency checks, multi-sensor fusion, differential privacy and clear data ownership. Daphne Koller adds that networks can grow more confident as inputs drift from training data ([Dawn Song](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/dawn-song-adversarial-machine-learning-and-computer-security/), [Daphne Koller](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/daphne-koller-biomedicine-and-machine-learning/)).

### The US, China and AI's social licence

- Stephen Schwarzman explains his $350 million gift to MIT's College of Computing as a bid to strengthen US competitiveness, saying “there's sort of like a global race on uh in ai quantum and other major technologies” ([Stephen Schwarzman](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/stephen-schwarzman-going-big-in-business-investing-and-ai/)).
- He warns that a hostile public reaction or regulation could cripple the field, and wants universities, AI companies, governments and media working together ([Stephen Schwarzman](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/stephen-schwarzman-going-big-in-business-investing-and-ai/)).
- Schwarzman contrasts China's plan to teach every schoolchild computer science with limited US exposure and calls for a federal moonshot. Dawn Song expects collaboration instead, because academic research is public ([Stephen Schwarzman](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/stephen-schwarzman-going-big-in-business-investing-and-ai/), [Dawn Song](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/dawn-song-adversarial-machine-learning-and-computer-security/)).

### Robots that fly and drive

- Sertac Karaman says the real challenge is putting robots into places where humans are present, and bets that high density will come first with autonomous cars ([Sertac Karaman](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/sertac-karaman-robots-that-fly-and-robots-that-drive/)).
- He describes Optimus Ride's geofenced deployments, aiming for ten people supervising 50 vehicles, and expects early deployments to use lidar ([Sertac Karaman](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/sertac-karaman-robots-that-fly-and-robots-that-drive/)).
- Through the AlphaPilot challenge he works on drones faster than human pilots; over ten runs, he says, machines beat humans slightly but consistently ([Sertac Karaman](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/sertac-karaman-robots-that-fly-and-robots-that-drive/)).

### Brains, movement and the free energy principle

- Karl Friston argues that anything that exists must look as if it minimises variational free energy, and that autonomous movement is what separates life from mere existence ([Karl Friston](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/karl-friston-neuroscience-and-the-free-energy-principle/)).
- He says “the central importance of movement I think has yet to really hit machine learning”, which relies on big data rather than active sampling ([Karl Friston](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2020/05/karl-friston-neuroscience-and-the-free-energy-principle/)).
