## Overview

November paired a learning theorist with two builders of programming tools and communities. Vladimir Vapnik argued that the hard part of learning is choosing good predicates, while Guido van Rossum and Jeff Atwood talked about designing for programmers and about how communities govern themselves.

## Main themes

### Learning from fewer examples

- Vapnik says classical textbooks take the admissible set of functions as given, while constructing it is the hardest problem. In his view, “every predicate is invented to decrease the admissible set of functions.” ([Vapnik](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/vladimir-vapnik-statistical-learning/)).
- Using the duck proverb, he argues three well-chosen predicates can do the work of many observations, since one example carries at most one bit of information. Choosing useful predicates is the teacher's job ([Vapnik](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/vladimir-vapnik-statistical-learning/)).
- He says invariants can cut the observations needed a hundredfold, and challenges researchers to match deep learning's roughly 99.5% on MNIST with a hundred times less data ([Vapnik](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/vladimir-vapnik-statistical-learning/)).

### Doubts about deep learning and Software 2.0

- Vapnik calls deep learning and hand-made features fantasy: “Mathematics does not know deep learning. Mathematics does not know neurons; it is just functions.” He cites the Representer theorem for shallow solutions, while Fridman pushes back on deep learning's empirical success ([Vapnik](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/vladimir-vapnik-statistical-learning/)).
- Van Rossum would not call training neural networks programming, and dislikes the press calling opaque learned systems algorithms. He notes most developers abandoned bug-free code long ago and rely on retries and timeouts ([van Rossum](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/guido-van-rossum-python/)).

### Designing Python and governing it

- Van Rossum aimed Python between shell scripting and C, with a budget of about three months. He borrowed indentation from ABC and string literals and numbers from C ([van Rossum](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/guido-van-rossum-python/)).
- He says fewer details and a large library make iteration faster, like using a breadboard before designing a PCB ([van Rossum](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/guido-van-rossum-python/)).
- After months of debate he approved PEP 572 on assignment expressions and wrote his resignation as BDFL the next morning. He named no successor, confident the core developers could govern the language ([van Rossum](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/guido-van-rossum-python/)).

### Communities, people and iteration

- Atwood describes building Stack Overflow from Digg-style voting, Wikipedia-style editing and reputation, and making it stricter by banning open-ended questions ([Atwood](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/jeff-atwood-stack-overflow-and-coding-horror/)).
- He believes every community should own its software and rules, and built Discourse because forum software had not improved. Discourse earns money from hosting rather than ads ([Atwood](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/jeff-atwood-stack-overflow-and-coding-horror/)).
- Atwood measures a software company by how quickly one agreed word change reaches users. He says effective programmers end up working mostly in English, with people ([Atwood](/podcasts/lex-fridman/2018/11/jeff-atwood-stack-overflow-and-coding-horror/)).
